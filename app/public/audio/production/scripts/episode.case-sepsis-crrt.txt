Now: The trajectory matters.

Shock phenotyping. Identify inadequate effective circulation by integrating flow, vascular tone, congestion, oxygen delivery and organ response.

Build the mechanism first. Shock is inadequate effective tissue perfusion and oxygen utilization, not merely hypotension. Flow, vascular tone, capacitance, congestion and extraction can fail in different combinations.

At the bedside, phenotype the circulation repeatedly. A patient can move from one dominant mechanism to another during resuscitation.

Two traps to actively avoid: Naming shock once and never revisiting the model. And: Treating MAP as the only endpoint.

The integration move is this: Choose interventions that both treat the suspected mechanism and generate information about whether the model was correct.

Keep these terms available in your mental model: flow, tone, congestion, perfusion.

What expert-level use of this concept looks like is this: Recognize mixed shock. Choose an intervention that tests a physiologic hypothesis and define the response expected if that hypothesis is correct.

Now connect Shock phenotyping to Septic shock physiology. Distributive shock with dynamic changes in vascular tone, venous capacitance, myocardial performance, microcirculation and oxygen extraction.

The relationship matters because Septic shock can combine vasoplegia, venous pooling, myocardial dysfunction, endothelial leak and disturbed microcirculation. Cardiac output may be low, normal or high. At the bedside, reassess vascular tone, flow, congestion and perfusion after each intervention rather than assuming a fixed distributive phenotype.

Now connect Shock phenotyping to Right ventricular failure. Failure of a thin-walled ventricle that is highly sensitive to acute afterload, volume, perfusion pressure and ventricular interaction.

The relationship matters because The RV is thin-walled and highly sensitive to acute afterload. When it dilates, wall stress rises, coronary perfusion can fall and septal shift can reduce LV filling. At the bedside, track systemic pressure, venous congestion, echo geometry, oxygenation, CO2, acid-base status and ventilator pressures together. Small changes in P V R or intrathoracic pressure can have large hemodynamic consequences.

Now: What has not been controlled?.

Source control. Definitive management of infected material, devitalized tissue or an infected device can be as important as antimicrobial therapy.

Build the mechanism first. Antibiotics cannot sterilize every infected collection, necrotic tissue or infected device. Persistent nidus can sustain inflammatory and microbial burden despite apparently appropriate antimicrobial therapy.

At the bedside, ask early whether drainage, debridement, device removal or surgery is required and whether it is actually happening.

Two traps to actively avoid: Escalating antibiotic breadth when the real failure is lack of source control. And: Waiting for complete hemodynamic normalization before addressing a time-sensitive source.

The integration move is this: Sepsis treatment is host support plus antimicrobials plus definitive control of the source.

Keep these terms available in your mental model: drainage, debridement, device removal.

What expert-level use of this concept looks like is this: Identify when antibiotics alone are unlikely to succeed. Escalate procedural or surgical source control as part of the resuscitation plan.

Now: AKI changes the treatment landscape.

C R R T physiology & prescription literacy. Continuous renal replacement uses diffusion and/or convection to manage solute, acid-base and fluid balance in unstable patients.

Build the mechanism first. C R R T uses diffusion, convection and ultrafiltration to control solute, acid-base and volume continuously. Prescribed dose can differ substantially from delivered dose.

At the bedside, separate total effluent from net patient fluid removal and interpret access, filter and return pressures as clues to the physical circuit.

Two traps to actively avoid: Calling every effluent liter “fluid removed from the patient.” And: Ignoring downtime and clotting when assessing delivered therapy.

The integration move is this: C R R T is both renal support and a hemodynamic intervention.

Keep these terms available in your mental model: effluent dose, CVVH, CVVHD, CVVHDF, filtration fraction.

What expert-level use of this concept looks like is this: Explain diffusion versus convection and net ultrafiltration. Recognize how circuit, anticoagulation and prescribed versus delivered dose change therapy.

Now: Removing fluid from a shocked patient.

Multisystem treatment tradeoffs. A therapy that helps one organ can worsen another; expert care weighs global benefit, reversibility and monitoring.

Build the mechanism first. Organ-support interventions frequently redistribute risk: peep can help oxygenation but worsen RV load; fluids can increase flow but worsen congestion; sedation can improve synchrony but obscure neurologic assessment.

At the bedside, before intervening, name the intended benefit, the organ/system at risk and the signal that would tell you the tradeoff has become unfavorable.

Two traps to actively avoid: Optimizing one organ in isolation. And: Treating guideline targets as independent goals that can all be maximized simultaneously.

The integration move is this: Expert critical care is often choosing the least harmful global strategy rather than a perfect organ-specific intervention.

What expert-level use of this concept looks like is this: Explain at least two competing organ effects of a proposed intervention. Choose the least harmful testable strategy when the optimal answer is uncertain.

Now: Changing clearance changes drug exposure.

Antimicrobial PK/PD in critical illness. Critical illness changes volume of distribution, clearance and target attainment, so dose strategy must match drug and patient physiology.

Build the mechanism first. Critical illness alters volume of distribution, protein binding and clearance. Drug efficacy depends on whether exposure matches the relevant PK/PD target.

At the bedside, account for augmented renal clearance, AKI, C R R T, fluid shifts and obesity rather than assuming standard dosing produces standard exposure.

Two traps to actively avoid: Reducing doses reflexively in early sepsis because creatinine is high. And: Ignoring extracorporeal clearance.

The integration move is this: The antimicrobial choice and the exposure strategy are separate decisions.

Keep these terms available in your mental model: time above MIC, AUC/MIC, augmented renal clearance.

What expert-level use of this concept looks like is this: Explain why standard dosing can underdose a shocked or hyperdynamic patient. Integrate renal support and changing clearance into antimicrobial strategy.
