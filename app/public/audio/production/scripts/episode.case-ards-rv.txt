A R D S physiology. Diffuse inflammatory lung injury producing heterogeneity, shunt, low aerated lung volume and risk from ventilator-induced injury.

Build the mechanism first. A R D S produces heterogeneous inflammatory lung injury, shunt, edema and a reduced aerated “baby lung,” making regional overdistension possible even at seemingly modest tidal volumes.

At the bedside, integrate gas exchange, compliance, recruitability, prone response, driving pressure and hemodynamics rather than treating the P/F ratio alone.

Two traps to actively avoid: Equating severe hypoxemia with universal recruitability. And: Improving oxygenation at the cost of excessive distending pressure or RV load.

The integration move is this: A R D S management is a tradeoff between oxygenation, mechanical injury and circulation.

Keep these terms available in your mental model: baby lung, shunt, recruitability.

What expert-level use of this concept looks like is this: Integrate oxygenation, mechanics, recruitability and hemodynamics rather than treating a P/F ratio in isolation.

Right ventricular failure. Failure of a thin-walled ventricle that is highly sensitive to acute afterload, volume, perfusion pressure and ventricular interaction.

Build the mechanism first. The RV is thin-walled and highly sensitive to acute afterload. When it dilates, wall stress rises, coronary perfusion can fall and septal shift can reduce LV filling.

At the bedside, track systemic pressure, venous congestion, echo geometry, oxygenation, CO2, acid-base status and ventilator pressures together. Small changes in P V R or intrathoracic pressure can have large hemodynamic consequences.

Two traps to actively avoid: Reflexively giving fluid to every hypotensive RV patient. And: Focusing on systemic pressure while ignoring worsening RV dilation and congestion.

The integration move is this: Every intervention should be judged by its effects on RV preload, RV afterload, contractility and coronary perfusion.

Keep these terms available in your mental model: RV-PA coupling, septal shift, transmural pressure.

What expert-level use of this concept looks like is this: Recognize RV shock physiology before systemic hypotension becomes profound. Predict how peep, hypercapnia, hypoxemia, fluids and vasoactives may change RV performance.

Now: The vent becomes part of the circulation.

peep. Positive end-expiratory pressure used to preserve recruitment and oxygenation, with important mechanical and hemodynamic effects.

Build the mechanism first. peep can prevent end-expiratory collapse and recruit unstable lung units, but can also overdistend already-open units and transmit pressure to the thorax.

At the bedside, judge a peep change by oxygenation, compliance, driving pressure, hemodynamics and the disease’s recruitability rather than by oxygen saturation alone.

Two traps to actively avoid: Chasing oxygenation while ignoring falling cardiac output. And: Assuming higher peep is either universally protective or universally harmful.

The integration move is this: The useful peep is the one that improves the whole patient, not just the alveolar oxygen number.

Keep these terms available in your mental model: recruitability, intrinsic peep.

What expert-level use of this concept looks like is this: Explain when increasing peep may recruit lung versus overdistend it. Anticipate circulatory effects in preload-dependent or RV-failing patients.

Pulmonary vascular resistance. Resistance faced by the RV, influenced by lung volume, oxygenation, CO2, pH, pulmonary vascular disease and mechanical ventilation.

Build the mechanism first. Pulmonary vascular resistance reflects the combined behavior of alveolar and extra-alveolar vessels and is affected by lung volume, oxygen, CO2, pH and pulmonary vascular disease.

At the bedside, both severe derecruitment and overdistension can raise RV afterload. Hypoxemia, hypercapnia and acidosis can compound the problem.

Two traps to actively avoid: Assuming more peep always lowers P V R by recruiting lung. And: Ignoring gas chemistry as an RV afterload intervention.

The integration move is this: Interpret ventilator changes through both lung mechanics and the pulmonary circulation.

Keep these terms available in your mental model: RV afterload, West zones.

What expert-level use of this concept looks like is this: Explain the U-shaped relationship between lung volume and pulmonary vascular resistance. Identify reversible contributors to acute RV afterload.

Tie this back to the patient. Ask what variable is changing, what downstream effect you expect, and what bedside finding would support or contradict that model.

Ventricular interdependence. The ventricles share a septum and constrained pericardial space, so pressure or volume loading of one changes filling and geometry of the other.

Build the mechanism first. The ventricles share a septum and a constrained pericardial space. Acute RV pressure or volume loading can shift the septum and reduce LV diastolic filling.

At the bedside, a falling LV stroke volume in RV failure may occur despite apparently adequate intravascular volume because geometry, not supply, is limiting filling.

Two traps to actively avoid: Treating LV output and RV function as separate systems. And: Calling septal flattening a descriptive echo finding without hemodynamic meaning.

The integration move is this: When one ventricle enlarges or its pressure rises, ask what that does to the other ventricle’s filling conditions.

Keep these terms available in your mental model: septal shift, pericardial constraint.

What expert-level use of this concept looks like is this: Use septal position and loading conditions to explain falling LV output during RV distension.

Tie this back to the patient. Ask what variable is changing, what downstream effect you expect, and what bedside finding would support or contradict that model.

Now: There is no free intervention.

Multisystem treatment tradeoffs. A therapy that helps one organ can worsen another; expert care weighs global benefit, reversibility and monitoring.

Build the mechanism first. Organ-support interventions frequently redistribute risk: peep can help oxygenation but worsen RV load; fluids can increase flow but worsen congestion; sedation can improve synchrony but obscure neurologic assessment.

At the bedside, before intervening, name the intended benefit, the organ/system at risk and the signal that would tell you the tradeoff has become unfavorable.

Two traps to actively avoid: Optimizing one organ in isolation. And: Treating guideline targets as independent goals that can all be maximized simultaneously.

The integration move is this: Expert critical care is often choosing the least harmful global strategy rather than a perfect organ-specific intervention.

What expert-level use of this concept looks like is this: Explain at least two competing organ effects of a proposed intervention. Choose the least harmful testable strategy when the optimal answer is uncertain.
