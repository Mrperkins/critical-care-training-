An indigestion story at 35 weeks
Case conference. “It feels like bad indigestion”. This is a fictional teaching case, with clinical review pending. A 30-year-old patient is pregnant at 35 weeks and 1 day. Nausea and upper abdominal pain worsen through the afternoon. The patient initially attributes the symptoms to reflux. There is no external bleeding. The patient’s explanation is understandable: upper abdominal discomfort often sounds gastrointestinal. Our task is to test that explanation against the pregnancy context and the rest of the examination.

The abdominal complaint needs a systemic assessment
Right upper quadrant tenderness and malaise are present. The patient is alert. The team is concerned about a systemic pregnancy complication rather than treating the pain as isolated indigestion. The pain is the opening complaint, but it may be a sign of organ involvement. Ask what else would have to be true for reflux to explain the whole presentation.

Pressure adds concern to the pain story
Heart rate is 112 per minute. Blood pressure is 162 over 108. Saturation is 97 percent. Fetal heart rate is 155 per minute. The systolic pressure is in a severe range at this assessment. It adds urgency to the symptom pattern and needs prompt repeat assessment and management under the obstetric pathway.

Three abnormalities form a pattern
Platelet count is 62 billion per liter. A S T is 280 units per liter. L D H is 920 units per liter. The laboratory reports schistocytes on the blood smear. The low platelets, liver enzyme elevation and evidence of hemolysis turn an apparently isolated complaint into a multisystem problem. Interpret the pattern together rather than treating each result as a separate issue.

HELLP changes the urgency
Hemolysis, elevated liver enzymes and low platelets support HELLP syndrome in context. Severe or escalating abdominal pain also raises concern for hepatic complications requiring urgent specialist assessment. Naming HELLP explains why the pain cannot simply be observed as indigestion. The intensity and course of pain remain important because complications may change the next definitive action.

Which finding changes your working explanation?
Which laboratory pattern connects the abdominal pain to a time-critical obstetric disorder? Return to the first impression of reflux. Which combination now makes that explanation insufficient, and what would you communicate before the patient leaves the referring unit?

Escalate for systemic obstetric disease
The platelet count and evidence of liver injury and hemolysis change the meaning of an apparently gastrointestinal complaint. Obstetric evaluation must accompany resuscitation. Escalate immediately to obstetric and critical care teams and coordinate definitive care. Monitor for bleeding, neurological change and worsening abdominal pain; repeat laboratories as directed. Treat associated severe hypertension and provide indicated seizure prophylaxis under the obstetric pathway. The clinical plan needs to anticipate more than the presenting symptom. Laboratory trajectory, bleeding risk and the potential for rapid change all influence the destination and the resources required.

The trend belongs in the handoff
The receiving team arranges urgent obstetric management. Serial symptoms and laboratory values are communicated, with preparation for hemorrhage and blood products if required. A single panel establishes the concern; serial values help describe its pace. The receiving team needs both the most recent results and how quickly the symptoms and abnormalities have developed.

HELLP: let the laboratory pattern reframe the complaint
Case synopsis. Abdominal pain became a systemic emergency when interpreted with thrombocytopenia, hemolysis and liver injury. HELLP required prompt specialist management. The useful move was revisiting the original explanation when the evidence stopped fitting. Carry that habit into other apparently ordinary complaints in pregnancy.
