Postpartum dyspnea with a failing circulation
Case conference. Peripartum cardiomyopathy complicated by cardiogenic shock and pulmonary edema. This is a fictional teaching case, with clinical review pending. A 36-year-old patient is ten days postpartum. Rapidly worsening dyspnea is accompanied by confusion and scant urine. The patient has received a small fluid intervention with no improvement. The partner calls EMS because severe postpartum breathlessness is followed by confusion and very little urine. The patient is cool despite wet lungs and elevated neck veins. The small fluid intervention has not improved perfusion. This is a low-output congested shock presentation requiring cardiac as well as obstetric expertise. This companion presentation begins where a congestive illness becomes a circulatory emergency. The next decisions have to account for the lungs and the circulation at the same time.

Congestion and low output can coexist
The patient is cool and clammy with weak pulses, crackles and elevated neck veins. The team recognizes simultaneous congestion and poor perfusion. Crackles may dominate the first impression, but the perfusion examination changes the urgency and the support plan. Avoid allowing one organ system to occupy the whole assessment.

Support decisions have hemodynamic consequences
Heart rate is 138 per minute. A resting adult heart rate is commonly 60 to 100 beats per minute. Pregnancy, pain and medications can shift the baseline. This is marked tachycardia with weak pulses, consistent with a stressed low-output circulation. Tachycardia may accompany pain, anxiety or reduced circulating volume; weak pulses, cool skin and oliguria would add evidence of poor perfusion. Blood pressure is 78 over 48. An adult pressure is commonly around 90 over 60 to below 120 over 80 at rest. A systolic pressure below 90 is concerning in an acutely ill patient; perfusion and baseline matter. This is severe hypotension with congestion and poor perfusion, supporting cardiogenic shock rather than isolated postpartum fluid retention. Low output may produce cool clammy skin, confusion, weak pulses and oliguria; all are present in this case. Respiratory rate is 34 per minute. This resting adult respiratory-rate reference is 12 to 18 breaths per minute; assess depth and effort as well as the count. This is marked tachypnea. Pulmonary edema and metabolic stress from shock can both increase the drive to breathe. Crackles, orthopnea, recession or inability to speak comfortably can accompany pulmonary edema; respiratory fatigue requires escalation. Saturation is 88 percent on supplemental oxygen. At sea level, oxygen saturation is usually 95 to 100 percent in a person without a special oxygen target. Verify signal quality and the oxygen support in use. This is persistent hypoxemia despite oxygen in a congested patient. Respiratory escalation must account for its effect on blood pressure. Hypoxemia can cause restlessness, tachycardia or cyanosis; oxygen saturation alone does not measure perfusion or cardiac output. Read the oxygenation and pressure together. An intervention intended to help breathing must be assessed for what it does to an already vulnerable circulation.

Organ stress belongs in the shock story
Lactate is 5.8 millimoles per liter. The whole-blood lactate reference here is 0.5 to 2.0 millimoles per liter for adults. A high value is a stress and perfusion clue, not a diagnosis by itself. This is hyperlactatemia in a clinically low-output state. It supports the perfusion assessment but is followed with examination and urine output. Shock may cause confusion, cool skin and oliguria; these are documented rather than inferred from lactate alone. Creatinine is 1.8 milligrams per deciliter. A general adult creatinine reference is 0.6 to 1.2 milligrams per deciliter. Pregnancy usually lowers creatinine; a value above 1.1 or doubling from baseline is a preeclampsia severe-feature criterion without another renal cause. This exceeds the general adult reference and suggests renal impairment in the shock context; baseline and serial results are needed to define acute injury. Renal hypoperfusion and congestion can produce scant urine and fluid accumulation; scant urine is part of this presentation. Use the laboratory results to track the consequences of poor perfusion alongside the examination. They contribute to the evolving picture rather than choosing the definitive cardiac diagnosis on their own.

Define function and evaluate the cause
Bedside echocardiography shows severe global left-ventricular dysfunction. The differential includes peripartum cardiomyopathy and other causes of acute heart failure or shock. A single echocardiographic finding does not replace etiological evaluation. Hazy bilateral perihilar and lower-zone opacities illustrate airspace fluid; a normally aerated lung is more lucent. The device and leads belong to that reference patient. Echocardiography helps define the functional problem. The team still needs to evaluate why it is present and whether a competing diagnosis changes the immediate treatment.

Would another fluid intervention help?
Would more fluid necessarily improve the pressure in a patient who is both congested and poorly perfused? Think through the next intervention as well as the next number you want to improve. What evidence would make more fluid helpful, and what evidence would make it poorly tolerated?

Coordinate a cardiac shock plan
Cardiogenic shock requires support of forward flow while respecting pulmonary congestion. Respiratory interventions can also alter hemodynamics, so escalation needs careful planning. Activate cardiogenic-shock and obstetric consultation with a destination capable of advanced circulatory support. Support oxygenation and perfusion under specialist guidance; reassess tolerance of each intervention. Communicate the postpartum timing and evaluate alternative causes including ischemia and embolic disease. The transfer decision is part of the resuscitation. A patient with this combination may need capabilities beyond routine respiratory support, so the receiving team should help shape the plan early.

Trend both sides of the response
The receiving team prepares intensive care and possible mechanical support while transfer stabilization continues. The handoff includes urine output, oxygen requirement and response to each intervention. A shock team coordinates vasoactive and inotropic support, respiratory escalation and transfer to a center capable of mechanical circulatory support. Specialist echocardiography shows severe global left-ventricular dysfunction; evaluation does not identify acute infarction, pulmonary embolism or a prior cardiomyopathy. Those findings establish peripartum cardiomyopathy in this synthetic case. Circulation remains inadequate despite initial support, so temporary mechanical circulatory support is used in the receiving unit. Over subsequent days perfusion, urine output and lactate improve. Long-term cardiac recovery remains uncertain and requires serial imaging; stabilization is the reported outcome, not a promise of a normal heart. Describe the effect of each intervention on both breathing and circulation. That makes opposing responses visible and helps the next team continue an individualized plan.
