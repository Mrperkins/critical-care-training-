Headache and flashing lights
Case conference. Headache and flashing lights at 33 weeks. This is a fictional teaching case, with clinical review pending. A 28-year-old patient is pregnant at 33 weeks and 2 days, with a singleton pregnancy. Persistent headache, flashing lights and right upper quadrant discomfort develop over several hours. There is no seizure, vaginal bleeding or known chronic hypertension. The symptom story already asks us to consider more than an isolated blood pressure problem. Keep the headache, vision change and abdominal discomfort connected as the observations arrive.

Repeat the pressure; keep examining
The patient is alert with brisk reflexes and mild dependent edema. The lungs are clear. Severe blood pressure readings persist when repeated with an appropriate cuff. Confirming a reading matters, but confirmation should lead to action. The repeat severe pressure and the neurological symptoms belong together in the escalation call.

Two patients, one immediate stabilization plan
Maternal heart rate is 104 per minute. Blood pressure is 174 over 114 and remains severe on repeat assessment. Maternal saturation is 97 percent on room air. Fetal heart rate is 150 per minute at this assessment. The fetal rate is one observation at one moment. It cannot clear the mother’s severe hypertension or symptoms. Stabilizing maternal physiology is central to the care of both patients.

Look for organ involvement
Platelet count is 118 billion per liter. A S T is 85 units per liter. Creatinine is 1.2 milligrams per deciliter. The laboratories add to a syndrome we already suspected from the bedside. Avoid waiting for one particular urine result before recognizing the severe clinical features already present.

Preeclampsia with severe features
The findings support preeclampsia with severe features. Proteinuria testing contributes to evaluation but is not required to recognize severe disease when other qualifying features are present. Naming the syndrome helps organize parallel tasks. One treatment addresses seizure risk, another addresses dangerous pressure, and obstetric assessment determines the next definitive steps.

What problem does each medication treat?
Does magnesium replace an antihypertensive? Can a currently normal fetal rate justify delaying maternal stabilization? Imagine someone reports that magnesium is running and asks whether the blood pressure problem is now covered. Separate the purpose of the therapies before continuing.

Control pressure and prevent seizures
Seizure prevention and treatment of severe blood pressure serve different purposes. Maternal stabilization protects both patients, and the fetus requires ongoing assessment rather than a single reassuring number. Begin urgent obstetric escalation and treat persistent severe hypertension under the obstetric protocol. Give indicated magnesium for seizure prophylaxis and monitor breathing, reflexes, renal function and infusion delivery. Coordinate maternal and fetal monitoring and the specialist plan for delivery after stabilization. A good handoff names the goal of each intervention. It also carries the exact administration times and the monitoring findings, so a new team can continue treatment without guessing what has already happened.

Improved pressure is a response, not discharge criteria
Blood pressure improves under treatment and headache lessens, but the patient still requires specialist obstetric management. The handoff includes repeat pressures, symptoms, laboratory trends and exact medication times. The early improvement is useful, but it does not erase the underlying pregnancy emergency. We still need a coordinated destination, monitoring plan and specialist decision about delivery.

Severe preeclampsia: match therapy to its purpose
Case synopsis. Severe maternal hypertension with neurological symptoms required urgent care. Blood pressure control and seizure prophylaxis were complementary interventions, not interchangeable ones. Remember the distinction between two urgent tasks that happen at the same time. The disease lesson can now build the physiology around a clinical decision you have already worked through.
