Sleeping upright after delivery
Case conference. “I cannot breathe lying down”. This is a fictional teaching case, with clinical review pending. A 33-year-old patient is three weeks postpartum with no known cardiac disease. Progressive dyspnea and leg swelling have been attributed to recovery and sleep loss. The patient now sleeps sitting upright and has a cough. Fatigue and poor sleep are easy explanations after a new baby. Ask what the patient has had to change to breathe comfortably; sleeping upright gives that complaint a different meaning.

The examination suggests congestion
There are bilateral crackles, elevated neck veins and peripheral edema. The patient is tachypneic but alert. The findings come from more than one part of the circulation. Together they make isolated dependent swelling an inadequate explanation for the patient’s difficulty breathing.

Maintained pressure with respiratory compromise
Heart rate is 122 per minute. Blood pressure is 118 over 76. Respiratory rate is 30 per minute. Saturation is 89 percent on room air. The pressure is currently preserved. The tachypnea and hypoxemia still demand support and an explanation. Heart failure does not have to arrive with hypotension to be important.

BNP adds support, not a complete diagnosis
B N P is 1,480 picograms per milliliter. Creatinine is 1.1 milligrams per deciliter. The biomarker is consistent with the congestive picture, but it does not identify the cause by itself. We need imaging and evaluation of other postpartum causes of breathlessness.

Global systolic dysfunction enters the story
A synthetic echocardiography report describes global left-ventricular systolic dysfunction with ejection fraction 28 percent. Chest imaging shows pulmonary congestion. Peripartum cardiomyopathy is considered after evaluation for other causes; BNP alone does not establish the diagnosis. An optional comparison image in the visual presentation comes from a different patient; it is not this fictional patient’s study. The echocardiographic finding gives us a functional explanation for the congestion. The postpartum timing raises peripartum cardiomyopathy, while the team continues the work needed to establish the cause.

Support breathing and coordinate cardiac care
Orthopnea, hypoxemia, pulmonary findings and systolic dysfunction indicate pathological heart failure. Pulmonary embolism and other causes of postpartum dyspnea must also be evaluated. Support oxygenation and consider noninvasive respiratory support when appropriate. Coordinate cardiology and obstetric care and treat congestion under a pregnancy-aware heart-failure pathway. Monitor perfusion and rhythm and avoid reflexive large fluid administration. Care has to fit the postpartum context as well as the cardiac physiology. The handoff should include timing after delivery, current respiratory support and the evidence for ventricular dysfunction.

Follow the response beyond the oxygen number
With respiratory and congestion-directed treatment, breathing improves. Cardiac follow-up and a longer-term plan are arranged; early symptom relief does not establish recovery of ventricular function. Improved breathing is the beginning of a response assessment. We still need a sustained trajectory and a shared specialist plan for the ventricular dysfunction and its cause.

Peripartum heart failure: listen to the functional history
Case synopsis. Postpartum dyspnea was a heart-failure presentation. Orthopnea and objective cardiac dysfunction required a cardiac evaluation rather than attribution to normal recovery. The story changed when fatigue became an inability to lie flat. Functional detail can expose disease hidden behind a familiar life circumstance.
