Sleeping upright after delivery
Case conference. Peripartum cardiomyopathy presenting with acute systolic heart failure and pulmonary edema. This is a fictional teaching case, with clinical review pending. A 33-year-old patient is three weeks postpartum with no known cardiac disease. Progressive dyspnea and leg swelling have been attributed to recovery and sleep loss. The patient now sleeps sitting upright and has a cough. EMS finds a mother sleeping upright because lying down produces breathlessness. She reports that ankle swelling and fatigue were initially called normal postpartum recovery, but breathlessness now interrupts conversation and routine infant care. The diagnosis is heart failure associated with peripartum cardiomyopathy after competing causes are evaluated. Fatigue and poor sleep are easy explanations after a new baby. Ask what the patient has had to change to breathe comfortably; sleeping upright gives that complaint a different meaning.

The examination suggests congestion
There are bilateral crackles, elevated neck veins and peripheral edema. The patient is tachypneic but alert. The findings come from more than one part of the circulation. Together they make isolated dependent swelling an inadequate explanation for the patient’s difficulty breathing.

Maintained pressure with respiratory compromise
Heart rate is 122 per minute. A resting adult heart rate is commonly 60 to 100 beats per minute. Pregnancy, pain and medications can shift the baseline. This is tachycardia in a congested patient and may be compensating for reduced stroke volume. Tachycardia may accompany pain, anxiety or reduced circulating volume; weak pulses, cool skin and oliguria would add evidence of poor perfusion. Blood pressure is 118 over 76. An adult pressure is commonly around 90 over 60 to below 120 over 80 at rest. A systolic pressure below 90 is concerning in an acutely ill patient; perfusion and baseline matter. This is preserved pressure. Systolic heart failure and pulmonary edema can occur without hypotension. Congestion commonly produces orthopnea, elevated neck veins, crackles and edema; all are documented here. Respiratory rate is 30 per minute. This resting adult respiratory-rate reference is 12 to 18 breaths per minute; assess depth and effort as well as the count. This is tachypnea. Pulmonary congestion increases respiratory work and impairs gas exchange. Heart-failure respiratory distress may produce orthopnea, crackles and accessory-muscle use; orthopnea and crackles are present. Saturation is 89 percent on room air. At sea level, oxygen saturation is usually 95 to 100 percent in a person without a special oxygen target. Verify signal quality and the oxygen support in use. This is hypoxemia on room air in the setting of pulmonary edema. Hypoxemia can cause restlessness, tachycardia or cyanosis; oxygen saturation alone does not measure perfusion or cardiac output. The pressure is currently preserved. The tachypnea and hypoxemia still demand support and an explanation. Heart failure does not have to arrive with hypotension to be important.

BNP adds support, not a complete diagnosis
B N P is 1,480 picograms per milliliter. BNP interpretation depends on the assay and clinical setting. This BNP assay lists 0 to 100 picograms per milliliter as its reference; that is not a universal pregnancy reference or a stand-alone diagnosis. This is markedly elevated and supports cardiac wall stress. It does not diagnose peripartum cardiomyopathy by itself. Heart failure may cause orthopnea, neck-vein distension, edema and crackles; the echocardiogram and full evaluation connect the biomarker to this case. Creatinine is 1.1 milligrams per deciliter. A general adult creatinine reference is 0.6 to 1.2 milligrams per deciliter. Pregnancy usually lowers creatinine; a value above 1.1 or doubling from baseline is a preeclampsia severe-feature criterion without another renal cause. This is within the general adult reference near its upper end. At three weeks postpartum, trend and prior renal function matter more than treating a pregnancy threshold as an automatic diagnosis. Renal congestion or low perfusion can cause oliguria and rising creatinine during heart failure; monitor response to diuresis. The biomarker is consistent with the congestive picture, but it does not identify the cause by itself. We need imaging and evaluation of other postpartum causes of breathlessness.

Global systolic dysfunction enters the story
A synthetic echocardiography report describes global left-ventricular systolic dysfunction with ejection fraction 28 percent. Chest imaging shows pulmonary congestion. Peripartum cardiomyopathy is considered after evaluation for other causes; BNP alone does not establish the diagnosis. The real comparison chest film comes from a different patient with flash pulmonary edema after a pacing problem, not a postpartum patient. Hazy bilateral perihilar and lower-zone opacities illustrate airspace fluid; a normally aerated lung is more lucent. The device and leads belong to that reference patient. This image teaches the edema pattern, not the cause of our patient’s cardiomyopathy. Etiology comes from postpartum timing, echocardiography and exclusion of alternatives. The echocardiographic finding gives us a functional explanation for the congestion. The postpartum timing raises peripartum cardiomyopathy, while the team continues the work needed to establish the cause.

Support breathing and coordinate cardiac care
Orthopnea, hypoxemia, pulmonary findings and systolic dysfunction indicate pathological heart failure. Pulmonary embolism and other causes of postpartum dyspnea must also be evaluated. Support oxygenation and consider noninvasive respiratory support when appropriate. Coordinate cardiology and obstetric care and treat congestion under a pregnancy-aware heart-failure pathway. Monitor perfusion and rhythm and avoid reflexive large fluid administration. Care has to fit the postpartum context as well as the cardiac physiology. The handoff should include timing after delivery, current respiratory support and the evidence for ventricular dysfunction.

Follow the response beyond the oxygen number
With respiratory and congestion-directed treatment, breathing improves. Cardiac follow-up and a longer-term plan are arranged; early symptom relief does not establish recovery of ventricular function. Oxygen, appropriately selected noninvasive respiratory support and intravenous diuresis improve congestion under cardiology and obstetric supervision. The ejection fraction of 28 percent represents substantial systolic dysfunction; a usual adult value is about 55 to 70 percent. This explains the orthopnea and pulmonary edema, while etiology still requires evaluation. The synthetic workup finds no pulmonary embolism, acute coronary cause or preexisting structural disease. A postpartum heart-failure plan accounts for lactation, medications and follow-up echocardiography. Breathing improves during admission, but ventricular recovery is not assumed from symptom relief. Improved breathing is the beginning of a response assessment. We still need a sustained trajectory and a shared specialist plan for the ventricular dysfunction and its cause.
