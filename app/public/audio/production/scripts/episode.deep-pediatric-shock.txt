This is a level 4 critical-care session. The goal is not to memorize a list. The goal is to build a model that lets you predict what the patient will do next.

By the end, you should be able to recognize pediatric shock before hypotension; and reason from age, size and physiology rather than scaled adult thresholds.

Now: How children compensate.

Pediatric shock physiology. Children often preserve blood pressure until late while compensating with tachycardia and vasoconstriction; age and size alter interpretation and therapy.

Build the mechanism first. Children can maintain blood pressure through tachycardia and vasoconstriction until late, while higher metabolic demand makes oxygen-delivery failure progress quickly.

At the bedside, use age-adjusted physiology, mental status, perfusion, pulse quality and urine output rather than waiting for hypotension.

Two traps to actively avoid: Using adult thresholds. And: Calling a normal pediatric BP reassuring despite poor perfusion.

The integration move is this: Pediatric shock is often compensated until it suddenly is not.

Keep these terms available in your mental model: compensated shock, age-adjusted, weight-based.

What expert-level use of this concept looks like is this: Recognize compensated pediatric shock before hypotension. Use age, size and disease-specific physiology rather than scaled-down adult assumptions.

Now: Oxygen delivery and metabolic demand.

Oxygen delivery. DO2 is cardiac output multiplied by arterial oxygen content; saturation alone does not describe tissue oxygen delivery.

Build the mechanism first. Oxygen delivery equals cardiac output multiplied by arterial oxygen content, which is dominated by hemoglobin-bound oxygen rather than dissolved oxygen.

At the bedside, normal saturation can coexist with critically low delivery in severe anemia or low-flow shock. Ask about hemoglobin and flow whenever tissue perfusion looks worse than the pulse oximeter.

Two traps to actively avoid: Using SpO2 as a proxy for whole-body oxygen delivery. And: Forgetting that raising saturation from already-high levels adds little content.

The integration move is this: Saturation tells you how full the carriers are; hemoglobin tells you how many carriers exist; cardiac output tells you how fast they move.

Keep these terms available in your mental model: CaO2, DO2, VO2.

What expert-level use of this concept looks like is this: Predict effects of hemoglobin, saturation and flow on oxygen delivery. Explain why a normal SpO2 can coexist with inadequate DO2.

Tie this back to the patient. Ask what variable is changing, what downstream effect you expect, and what bedside finding would support or contradict that model.

Now: Distributive, hypovolemic and pump failure.

Shock phenotyping. Identify inadequate effective circulation by integrating flow, vascular tone, congestion, oxygen delivery and organ response.

Build the mechanism first. Shock is inadequate effective tissue perfusion and oxygen utilization, not merely hypotension. Flow, vascular tone, capacitance, congestion and extraction can fail in different combinations.

At the bedside, phenotype the circulation repeatedly. A patient can move from one dominant mechanism to another during resuscitation.

Two traps to actively avoid: Naming shock once and never revisiting the model. And: Treating MAP as the only endpoint.

The integration move is this: Choose interventions that both treat the suspected mechanism and generate information about whether the model was correct.

Keep these terms available in your mental model: flow, tone, congestion, perfusion.

What expert-level use of this concept looks like is this: Recognize mixed shock. Choose an intervention that tests a physiologic hypothesis and define the response expected if that hypothesis is correct.

Tie this back to the patient. Ask what variable is changing, what downstream effect you expect, and what bedside finding would support or contradict that model.
