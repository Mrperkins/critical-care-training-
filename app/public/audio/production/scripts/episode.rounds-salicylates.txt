You're listening to Salicylates: do not steal the patient’s compensatory ventilation. A mixed acid-base disorder where falling pH changes toxin distribution.

This is a level 4 critical-care session. The goal is not to memorize a list. The goal is to build a model that lets you predict what the patient will do next.

By the end, you should be able to recognize severe salicylate poisoning from physiology, not just concentration; and explain why loss of compensatory ventilation can abruptly worsen toxicity.

Now: Respiratory alkalosis plus metabolic acidosis.

Salicylate toxicity. Salicylates create respiratory alkalosis plus metabolic acidosis, uncouple oxidative phosphorylation and become more CNS-penetrant as pH falls.

Build the mechanism first. Salicylates stimulate respiration, uncouple oxidative phosphorylation and create mixed respiratory alkalosis/metabolic acidosis; lower blood pH increases nonionized drug penetration into tissues.

At the bedside, preserve compensatory ventilation, monitor acid-base trajectory and consider alkalinization/extracorporeal removal when severity warrants.

Two traps to actively avoid: Calling a near-normal pH reassuring. And: Intubating without matching the patient’s extraordinary minute ventilation.

The integration move is this: Acid-base management is toxicokinetic management because pH changes tissue distribution.

Keep these terms available in your mental model: alkalinization, mixed disorder, ion trapping.

What expert-level use of this concept looks like is this: Recognize why a near-normal pH can hide severe mixed acid-base disease. Explain why airway management can be hazardous if compensatory ventilation is lost.

Advanced acid-base reasoning. Integrate respiratory and metabolic processes, compensation, anion gap, albumin, chloride and mixed disorders.

Build the mechanism first. pH reflects interacting respiratory and metabolic processes. Compensation has expected patterns, so deviations can expose mixed disorders that a single pH label hides.

At the bedside, calculate the anion gap, correct for relevant confounders, compare compensation and consider chloride, albumin and lactate in context.

Two traps to actively avoid: Calling a normal pH normal acid-base physiology. And: Treating bicarbonate concentration as if it identifies the mechanism by itself.

The integration move is this: Acid-base analysis should lead back to the disease process causing the disturbance.

Keep these terms available in your mental model: delta gap, expected compensation.

What expert-level use of this concept looks like is this: Identify mixed disorders that a single pH label hides. Connect acid-base abnormalities to the underlying physiology and treatment.

Ventilator emergency troubleshooting. A structured patient-to-device approach to acute hypoxemia, high pressure, low pressure, dyssynchrony and post-intubation deterioration.

Build the mechanism first. Acute ventilator deterioration can originate from the patient, tube, circuit or machine. Peak and plateau pressures help separate resistance from compliance when measurements are trustworthy.

At the bedside, look at the patient first, rescue oxygenation/ventilation if the device cannot be trusted, then trace from patient to tube to circuit to machine.

Two traps to actively avoid: Staring at the ventilator while the patient deteriorates. And: Treating every high-pressure alarm as bronchospasm.

The integration move is this: Use pressure pattern, capnography, exam and circuit inspection to localize the problem quickly.

Keep these terms available in your mental model: DOPES, peak pressure, plateau pressure, auto-peep.

What expert-level use of this concept looks like is this: Separate resistance, compliance, circuit and patient problems using waveforms and bedside examination. Prioritize oxygenation and manual ventilation when the device or circuit cannot be trusted.

Tie this back to the patient. Ask what variable is changing, what downstream effect you expect, and what bedside finding would support or contradict that model.

Now: Alkalinization and extracorporeal removal.

C R R T physiology & prescription literacy. Continuous renal replacement uses diffusion and/or convection to manage solute, acid-base and fluid balance in unstable patients.

Build the mechanism first. C R R T uses diffusion, convection and ultrafiltration to control solute, acid-base and volume continuously. Prescribed dose can differ substantially from delivered dose.

At the bedside, separate total effluent from net patient fluid removal and interpret access, filter and return pressures as clues to the physical circuit.

Two traps to actively avoid: Calling every effluent liter “fluid removed from the patient.” And: Ignoring downtime and clotting when assessing delivered therapy.

The integration move is this: C R R T is both renal support and a hemodynamic intervention.

Keep these terms available in your mental model: effluent dose, CVVH, CVVHD, CVVHDF, filtration fraction.

What expert-level use of this concept looks like is this: Explain diffusion versus convection and net ultrafiltration. Recognize how circuit, anticoagulation and prescribed versus delivered dose change therapy.
