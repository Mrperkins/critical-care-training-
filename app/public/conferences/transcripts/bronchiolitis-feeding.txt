Too breathless to feed
Case conference. Too breathless to feed. This is a fictional teaching case, with clinical review pending. A 5-month-old infant, measured weight 6.4 kilograms, was born at term. After three days of coryza, the infant has increasing cough, reduced feeds and fewer wet diapers. There has been no witnessed apnea.

What do you notice?
At the bedside. The infant is alert with subcostal recession, diffuse crackles and wheeze, and copious nasal secretions. Feeding attempts increase distress.

Vital signs
The initial vital signs are as follows. Heart rate is 164 per minute. Respiratory rate is 66 per minute. Oxygen saturation is persistently 88 percent on room air. Temperature is 37.9 degrees Celsius. These are observations at this point in the case, rather than treatment targets.

Laboratory findings
Laboratory findings. A bedside glucose is 86 milligrams per deciliter. Routine blood tests have not been obtained because they would not currently change management. Interpret these results alongside the examination and the timing of sampling.

Diagnostic findings
Diagnostic findings. This is a clinical presentation of bronchiolitis. Routine chest radiographs and viral testing are not necessary for every typical case; testing depends on the clinical question and setting.

What would you prioritize?
Think it through. What does the inability to feed add to the respiratory assessment? Consider your answer while the case continues. No screen interaction is needed.

Clinical reasoning & priorities
Conference discussion. Respiratory distress can compromise both ventilation and hydration. Supportive care and reassessment are central; wheeze in an infant does not automatically imply asthma. Priorities. 1. Support oxygenation and clear obstructing nasal secretions when needed. 2. Plan hydration that the infant can tolerate safely. 3. Escalate respiratory support for persistent distress, hypoxemia, apnea or fatigue under the local pediatric pathway.

Reassessment & handoff
Reassessment and handoff. With support, saturation reaches 94 percent, but respiratory rate remains high. The team continues to assess effort and hydration rather than declaring success from saturation alone. This is an authored fictional course; improvement is not guaranteed in a real patient.
