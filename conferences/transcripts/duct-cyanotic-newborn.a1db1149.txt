Oxygen produces little change
Case conference. Pulmonary atresia with intact ventricular septum and duct-dependent pulmonary blood flow. This is a fictional teaching case, with clinical review pending. A 3-day-old term newborn weighs 3.5 kilograms. The infant has persistent central cyanosis and feeds poorly. Supplemental oxygen produces little change in saturation. The parent calls EMS because the infant’s lips and tongue stay blue despite warming and supplemental oxygen. There is little wheeze or focal chest asymmetry. The initial working diagnosis is cyanotic congenital heart disease while lung disease, pulmonary hypertension and infection are evaluated. Echocardiography, not skin color alone, will identify the anatomy. When the expected response to oxygen is small, revisit the mechanism of the hypoxemia. The lungs are part of the question, but the route blood takes through the circulation matters too.

Urgent echocardiography changes the plan
Echocardiography is needed urgently to distinguish structural disease and pulmonary hypertension. A small preductal–postductal difference does not rule out a cardiac lesion.  The purpose of echocardiography is practical: different circulations may need different support goals. We should avoid applying one generic target to every cyanotic newborn.

What would you do while defining the circulation?
Should you keep increasing oxygen while postponing a cardiac evaluation? Think about the balance between immediate supportive care and time to a definitive explanation. Repeatedly increasing oxygen is not a substitute for that explanation.
