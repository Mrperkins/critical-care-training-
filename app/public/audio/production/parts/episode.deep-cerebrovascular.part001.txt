This is a level 5 critical-care session. The goal is not to memorize a list. The goal is to build a model that lets you predict what the patient will do next.

By the end, you should be able to choose pressure/perfusion priorities according to ischemic versus hemorrhagic pathology and treatment phase; and recognize hydrocephalus, herniation and delayed ischemia as evolving neurologic emergencies.

Now: LVO and the salvageable penumbra.

Acute ischemic stroke & LVO. Large-vessel occlusion threatens salvageable tissue over time; perfusion depends on collateral circulation, pressure and rapid reperfusion.

Build the mechanism first. Large-vessel occlusion abruptly reduces regional cerebral blood flow; collateral circulation temporarily sustains penumbral tissue until reperfusion or infarction occurs.

At the bedside, recognize cortical/LVO syndromes, protect oxygenation and perfusion, establish last-known-well and move rapidly toward imaging/reperfusion pathways.

Two traps to actively avoid: Lowering blood pressure reflexively before reperfusion strategy is defined. And: Letting transport/airway procedures unnecessarily delay thrombectomy-capable care.

The integration move is this: Time, collaterals and perfusion determine how much brain remains salvageable.

Keep these terms available in your mental model: penumbra, large-vessel occlusion, thrombectomy.

What expert-level use of this concept looks like is this: Recognize LVO syndromes and time-sensitive reperfusion pathways. Avoid secondary hypoxemia/hypotension while protecting access to definitive therapy.

Now connect Acute ischemic stroke & LVO to ICP, CPP & cerebral perfusion. Cerebral perfusion depends on the gradient across the cerebral circulation and is influenced by pressure, venous outflow, CO2 and intracranial compliance.

The relationship matters because CPP is the pressure gradient available to drive cerebral blood flow and is influenced by arterial pressure, ICP, venous pressure and autoregulation. Intracranial compliance falls as compensatory reserve is exhausted. At the bedside, interpret ICP as a dynamic system with waveform, position, venous drainage, CO2 and sedation effects. A “normal” MAP does not guarantee adequate cerebral perfusion.

Stress-test the model. Imagine the patient changes in a way that makes lowering blood pressure reflexively before reperfusion strategy is defined tempting. Before acting, state the mechanism you think is operating, predict what your intervention should change, and name the finding that would make you stop and reconsider.
