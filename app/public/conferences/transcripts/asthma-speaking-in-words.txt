One word per breath
Case conference. The child who can only say one word. This is a fictional teaching case, with clinical review pending. An 8-year-old child, measured weight 26 kilograms, has asthma and a previous intensive care admission. A viral illness preceded rapidly worsening dyspnea. Home albuterol brought little relief. The child arrives sitting forward, speaking one word per breath. A caregiver reports no choking episode or allergen exposure. Before looking at a gas, listen to how much of a sentence this child can manage. One word per breath tells us that ordinary conversation is competing with the work of breathing.

Alert does not mean comfortable
The child is frightened and alert, with intercostal recession, prolonged expiration and diffuse wheeze. Air entry is reduced at both bases. The trachea is central and pulses are strong. Imagine kneeling beside the bed rather than talking over the child. You can follow air entry, effort and interaction together. The loudness of the wheeze is only one part of that picture.

A strained respiratory system
Heart rate is 148 per minute. Blood pressure is 110 over 68. Respiratory rate is 38 per minute. Oxygen saturation is 90 percent on room air. Temperature is 37.6 degrees Celsius. Read these observations as a group: rapid breathing, tachycardia and hypoxemia in a child who is still working hard. Preserved pressure does not reduce the urgency of the breathing problem.

Does the gas fit the child?
Venous blood pH is 7.34. Venous carbon dioxide is 43 millimeters of mercury; this is not an arterial oxygen measurement. Potassium is 3.4 millimoles per liter. The temptation is to find one value that sounds reassuring and relax. Here the bedside trajectory has to carry more weight than a carbon dioxide value that seems unremarkable in isolation.

A clinical diagnosis first
The clinical pattern favors an acute severe asthma exacerbation. A chest radiograph is not routinely required for a typical presentation. Imaging becomes relevant if asymmetry, fever with focal findings, or a complication changes the differential. An optional comparison image in the visual presentation comes from a different patient; it is not this fictional patient’s study. A test should answer a question. If the story becomes asymmetric or a complication is suspected, the imaging question changes. A routine film should not become a detour from effective treatment.

What would convince you treatment is working?
Does loud wheeze tell you how close the child is to respiratory failure? What would you trend during the first treatment cycle? Keep those three observations in mind. We are about to reassess the child; decide what combination would make you more confident that the treatment is reaching its target.

Treat, then return to the bedside
Wheeze requires airflow. Severity depends on work of breathing, air entry, mental status and response to treatment. An apparently reassuring carbon dioxide value does not overrule a tiring child. Give oxygen and repeated inhaled bronchodilator therapy with an effective interface; add indicated adjuncts under the pediatric protocol. Reassess air entry, speech, effort and alertness after each treatment; check drug delivery before calling treatment ineffective. Prepare escalation and pediatric critical care consultation while preserving spontaneous ventilation when feasible. Think of the first treatment as both therapy and a chance to learn. If the response is poor, check whether the medication is actually being delivered before concluding that the disease is unresponsive.

A coherent improvement
After initial treatment the child speaks short sentences, respiratory rate falls to 30, and saturation reaches 95 percent on supplemental oxygen. Continued monitoring and reassessment guide the transfer; a single improvement does not establish sustained stability. The important feature is that several bedside findings move in the same direction. Better saturation alone would be less persuasive if the child were becoming less interactive or moving less air.

Severe asthma: follow the whole child
Case synopsis. A child with severe asthma remained alert but had marked obstruction. The useful trend was better air movement, speech and effort. Wheeze volume alone was an unreliable severity measure. Carry this into the disease lesson: severity and response are patterns over time. A noisy chest can be very sick, and a quieter chest needs a fresh examination rather than an automatic label of improvement.
