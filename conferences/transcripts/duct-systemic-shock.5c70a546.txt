A newborn becomes gray at home
Case conference. Critical coarctation of the aorta with duct-dependent systemic circulation and shock. This is a fictional teaching case, with clinical review pending. A 6-day-old term newborn weighs 3.2 kilograms. Feeding and urine output have declined over a day. The infant now looks gray and breathes rapidly. No congenital heart diagnosis was made before discharge. The parents call EMS because feeding has deteriorated and the newborn has become gray with very few wet diapers. The infant went home without a known cardiac diagnosis. EMS reports cooler legs and weaker femoral than arm pulses. The initial working problem is neonatal shock with suspected duct-dependent systemic blood flow; the final anatomical diagnosis will come from echocardiography. Discharge without a cardiac diagnosis can make the heart seem less likely to explain this presentation. The age and the changing circulation after birth keep it in the urgent differential.

The metabolic picture fits poor perfusion
Venous pH is 7.14. This pediatric peripheral venous pH reference is 7.30 to 7.40. Venous and arterial samples must be identified separately. This is substantial venous acidemia. With shock and elevated lactate, a metabolic component is likely; full chemistry and carbon dioxide define the complete disorder. Acidotic shock may present with rapid breathing, poor responsiveness and poor feeding; those findings are documented. Lactate is 7.1 millimoles per liter. For a plasma lactic-acid assay, this laboratory lists 1.1 to 4.4 millimoles per liter from three to ten days of age. Neonatal ranges differ from adult whole-blood ranges. This is elevated even against the age-specific reference and supports concern for poor systemic perfusion in context. Poor perfusion may cause gray color, weak femoral pulses, cold limbs and oliguria; lactate alone cannot identify coarctation. Glucose is 58 milligrams per deciliter. After forty-eight to seventy-two hours, the Pediatric Endocrine Society identifies glucose below 60 milligrams per deciliter as hypoglycemia. Early transitional newborn thresholds are different. This is low for an infant six days old and requires correction and repeat monitoring alongside shock care. It is not treated as normal early transition. Hypoglycemia can cause poor feeding, jitteriness, lethargy, apnea or seizures; lethargy has multiple possible causes here. The acidemia and lactate support the urgency already visible at the bedside. Keep glucose and temperature in the stabilization plan while the cause is being defined.

Suspect duct-dependent flow; define anatomy urgently
The combination of age, poor systemic perfusion and pulse differences raises concern for critical congenital heart disease as the duct closes. Definitive anatomy requires expert echocardiography. Plan for circulation and transport capability
A duct-dependent systemic lesion can present as shock rather than profound cyanosis. Sepsis and other causes must be addressed in parallel until the cause is established. Consult neonatal and cardiac specialists urgently; arrange definitive echocardiography and a capable destination. Discuss prostaglandin therapy for suspected duct dependence under protocol, anticipating apnea and an airway support requirement. Support perfusion, temperature and glucose while evaluating infection and other causes of neonatal shock. A medication plan also creates an operational plan. If prostaglandin is used, the team must be prepared for its potential effects and able to sustain the infusion and airway support during the journey.

Describe observations without inventing anatomy
The cardiac team directs stabilization and transfer. The handoff includes limb pressures, pulse findings, perfusion trends and any response to therapy. The anatomical diagnosis is not assumed from the examination alone. Neonatal and cardiac teams direct prostaglandin to maintain ductal patency while preparing for apnea, supporting ventilation and treating possible sepsis in parallel. Expert echocardiography shows critical coarctation with a constricting ductus and impaired systemic output. As ductal flow improves, leg perfusion and urine output recover and lactate falls. Cultures remain negative in this synthetic course, and the infant proceeds to cardiac repair after stabilization. The final diagnosis is critical coarctation, not an unspecified circulation theory. A precise handoff separates what we measured, what we suspect and what has been confirmed. That distinction prevents an early working diagnosis from becoming an unsupported anatomical label.
