You're listening to The electrically unstable ischemic heart. Coronary ischemia, conduction, dysrhythmia, electrolytes and sudden mechanical deterioration.

This is a level 5 critical-care session. The goal is not to memorize a list. The goal is to build a model that lets you predict what the patient will do next.

By the end, you should be able to integrate rhythm, ischemia and pump function in unstable cardiac patients; and recognize mechanical complications when shock cannot be explained by the ecg alone.

Now: Ischemia changes pump and electricity.

Acute coronary ischemia & complications. Acute myocardial ischemia can produce pump failure, dysrhythmia and mechanical complications whose physiology may dominate over the ECG diagnosis.

Build the mechanism first. Myocardial ischemia reduces contractility and electrical stability and can cause papillary muscle, septal or free-wall mechanical complications after infarction.

At the bedside, when a patient with ACS suddenly develops shock, pulmonary edema or a new murmur, broaden beyond “more ischemia” to mechanical failure and rhythm disturbance.

Two traps to actively avoid: Treating the ECG diagnosis while missing a mechanical complication. And: Using vasopressor escalation as a substitute for reperfusion or structural intervention.

The integration move is this: Reperfusion, rhythm and mechanical hemodynamics must be managed as one evolving syndrome.

Keep these terms available in your mental model: ischemia, reperfusion, papillary muscle, septal rupture.

What expert-level use of this concept looks like is this: Recognize ischemic mechanical complications in sudden shock. Integrate reperfusion urgency with hemodynamic and rhythm stabilization.

The relationship matters because Cardiogenic shock is inadequate forward flow from pump failure, often accompanied by elevated filling pressures and congestion. RV, LV and biventricular phenotypes behave differently. Cardiogenic shock. Pump failure where restoring perfusion must be balanced against congestion, myocardial oxygen demand, coronary perfusion and mechanical complications.

Build the mechanism first. Cardiogenic shock is inadequate forward flow from pump failure, often accompanied by elevated filling pressures and congestion. RV, LV and biventricular phenotypes behave differently.

At the bedside, track flow, pressure, congestion, coronary perfusion, rhythm and mechanical complications; escalate support when pharmacology cannot restore effective circulation.

Two traps to actively avoid: Treating all cardiogenic shock with the same inotrope/pressor pattern. And: Ignoring congestion while chasing blood pressure.

The integration move is this: The support strategy should match the failing ventricle and the cause of pump failure.

Keep these terms available in your mental model: cardiac power output, congestion, inotropy.

What expert-level use of this concept looks like is this: Phenotype left-, right- and biventricular pump failure. Choose support based on pressure, flow, congestion and the cause of pump failure.

Now connect Cardiogenic shock to Right ventricular failure. Failure of a thin-walled ventricle that is highly sensitive to acute afterload, volume, perfusion pressure and ventricular interaction.

The relationship matters because The RV is thin-walled and highly sensitive to acute afterload. When it dilates, wall stress rises, coronary perfusion can fall and septal shift can reduce LV filling. At the bedside, track systemic pressure, venous congestion, echo geometry, oxygenation, CO2, acid-base status and ventilator pressures together. Small changes in P V R or intrathoracic pressure can have large hemodynamic consequences.

Now connect Cardiogenic shock to Vasopressor & inotrope physiology. Vasoactive drugs change arterial tone, venous tone, heart rate, contractility and regional flow; the desired effect depends on the shock model.

The relationship matters because Vasoactive drugs alter arterial tone, venous tone, contractility and heart rate to different degrees. The same MAP response can hide very different effects on flow and myocardial demand. At the bedside, choose the agent from the dominant deficit and define what improvement and toxicity should look like before titration.

Clinical uncertainty & model revision. Expert reasoning explicitly tracks uncertainty, contradictory data and what finding would falsify the current working model.

Build the mechanism first. Clinical decisions are made with incomplete information. Calibrated reasoning assigns provisional probability, makes predictions and updates when new data contradict the model.

At the bedside, state confidence, name disconfirming evidence and define what response you expect from a treatment if the model is correct.

Two traps to actively avoid: Equating uncertainty with indecision. And: Becoming more confident simply because the same hypothesis has been repeated.

The integration move is this: A model that cannot be falsified is not useful clinical reasoning.

Keep these terms available in your mental model: calibration, falsification, diagnostic time-out.

What expert-level use of this concept looks like is this: State confidence, identify disconfirming evidence and update the model when the patient fails to respond as predicted.

Reasoning checkpoint. Do not just repeat the statement: State confidence, identify disconfirming evidence and update the model when the patient fails to respond as predicted. Now connect Clinical uncertainty & model revision to Cognitive bias & diagnostic error. Expert reasoning uses deliberate checks against anchoring, premature closure, confirmation bias and overconfidence while still making timely decisions.

The relationship matters because Anchoring, confirmation bias and premature closure arise because fast pattern recognition is useful but can overcommit to the first plausible model. At the bedside, name the finding that does not fit, generate at least one alternative mechanism and use treatment response as new diagnostic evidence.

Now connect Clinical uncertainty & model revision to Multisystem treatment tradeoffs. A therapy that helps one organ can worsen another; expert care weighs global benefit, reversibility and monitoring.

The relationship matters because Organ-support interventions frequently redistribute risk: peep can help oxygenation but worsen RV load; fluids can increase flow but worsen congestion; sedation can improve synchrony but obscure neurologic assessment. At the bedside, before intervening, name the intended benefit, the organ/system at risk and the signal that would tell you the tradeoff has become unfavorable.
