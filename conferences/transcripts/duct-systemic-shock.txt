A newborn becomes gray at home
Case conference. The newborn who deteriorates at home. This is a fictional teaching case, with clinical review pending. A 6-day-old term newborn weighs 3.2 kilograms. Feeding and urine output have declined over a day. The infant now looks gray and breathes rapidly. No congenital heart diagnosis was made before discharge. Discharge without a cardiac diagnosis can make the heart seem less likely to explain this presentation. The age and the changing circulation after birth keep it in the urgent differential.

Compare the upper and lower body
The infant is poorly responsive, with weak femoral pulses, cool legs and a prolonged refill time. Upper-extremity pulses are easier to feel. Sepsis remains an urgent competing diagnosis. The pulse difference is a clue, not an anatomical diagnosis. Check the quality of the examination while treating the infant’s poor perfusion as the immediate problem.

A saturation in the nineties can coexist with shock
Heart rate is 188 per minute. Right-arm blood pressure is 68 over 42. Leg blood pressure is 44 over 26; measurement quality is being checked. Respiratory rate is 72 per minute. Right-hand oxygen saturation is 94 percent. The right-hand saturation describes oxygenation in that location. It does not tell us that enough systemic flow is reaching the lower body or the kidneys.

The metabolic picture fits poor perfusion
Venous pH is 7.14. Lactate is 7.1 millimoles per liter. Glucose is 58 milligrams per deciliter. The acidemia and lactate support the urgency already visible at the bedside. Keep glucose and temperature in the stabilization plan while the cause is being defined.

Suspect duct-dependent flow; define anatomy urgently
The combination of age, poor systemic perfusion and pulse differences raises concern for critical congenital heart disease as the duct closes. Definitive anatomy requires expert echocardiography. Saturation alone cannot exclude poor systemic blood flow. We do not have to choose between acknowledging cardiac risk and evaluating infection. The infant needs urgent specialist input while those possibilities are addressed together.

Plan for circulation and transport capability
A duct-dependent systemic lesion can present as shock rather than profound cyanosis. Sepsis and other causes must be addressed in parallel until the cause is established. Consult neonatal and cardiac specialists urgently; arrange definitive echocardiography and a capable destination. Discuss prostaglandin therapy for suspected duct dependence under protocol, anticipating apnea and an airway support requirement. Support perfusion, temperature and glucose while evaluating infection and other causes of neonatal shock. A medication plan also creates an operational plan. If prostaglandin is used, the team must be prepared for its potential effects and able to sustain the infusion and airway support during the journey.

Describe observations without inventing anatomy
The cardiac team directs stabilization and transfer. The handoff includes limb pressures, pulse findings, perfusion trends and any response to therapy. The anatomical diagnosis is not assumed from the examination alone. A precise handoff separates what we measured, what we suspect and what has been confirmed. That distinction prevents an early working diagnosis from becoming an unsupported anatomical label.
