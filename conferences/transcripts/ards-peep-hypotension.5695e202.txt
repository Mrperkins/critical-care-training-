One adjustment, two opposing responses
Case conference. ARDS with hemodynamic intolerance after a PEEP increase. This is a fictional teaching case, with clinical review pending. A 62-year-old patient with ARDS is being prepared for an intensive care transfer. Following an increase in PEEP from 10 to 16, saturation rises but blood pressure falls. The change occurs immediately, before any new medication is given. The referring ICU raises PEEP during preparation for transfer because oxygenation remains poor. Saturation improves, but pressure falls immediately afterward. The timeline creates a specific question: did the intervention improve oxygenation at the expense of cardiac filling or right-ventricular performance? The sequence is unusually informative. One intervention is followed immediately by a better oxygen number and a worse circulation. That is a reason to reassess the net effect.

Find a tolerated ventilation strategy
PEEP can improve recruitment while impairing circulation in a susceptible patient. The treatment response must include perfusion, pressure and gas exchange rather than saturation alone. Reassess the recent ventilator change and restore an individually tolerated strategy with the critical care team. Evaluate alternative immediate causes of hypotension and verify pressure measurements. Trend both gas exchange and perfusion when making subsequent adjustments. The goal is an individualized strategy with a tolerable overall effect. Coordinate the respiratory and circulatory plan instead of letting separate numbers produce conflicting orders.

Handoff the opposing responses
The team revises the support plan and repeats perfusion and gas-exchange assessment. The final handoff records the PEEP change and its opposing oxygenation and blood-pressure effects. The team reassesses ventilator mechanics, circulation, sedation, tube position and possible pneumothorax. No pneumothorax or new tube problem is found in this synthetic course. Returning to a better tolerated pressure strategy improves blood pressure, and oxygenation is supported through an individualized ARDS plan including positioning and hemodynamic support. The team documents both oxygenation and perfusion responses before transfer. The event is intervention-associated hemodynamic compromise, not proof that all PEEP is harmful. The most useful handoff is the experiment the patient has already undergone: what was changed, what improved and what worsened. That prevents the next team from unknowingly repeating it.
