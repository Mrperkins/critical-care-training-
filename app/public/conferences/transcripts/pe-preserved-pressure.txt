Sudden breathlessness after surgery
Case conference. Breathlessness after surgery. This is a fictional teaching case, with clinical review pending. A 61-year-old patient is eight days after hip surgery. Sudden dyspnea and pleuritic discomfort develop at home. The patient is tachycardic and hypoxemic but not hypotensive. The postoperative history raises both thrombotic concern and treatment complexity. Keep the timing of surgery in view as we assess the severity and the options.

Clear lungs with a swollen calf
Breath sounds are clear and one calf is swollen. The team evaluates pulmonary embolism while considering other postoperative complications. The lung examination does not explain the degree of breathlessness. The leg finding adds a clue, while the broader postoperative differential still deserves attention.

Maintained pressure does not establish low risk
Heart rate is 120 per minute. Blood pressure is 118 over 74. Respiratory rate is 30 per minute. Saturation is 88 percent on room air. The pressure is preserved at this moment. The respiratory rate, hypoxemia and tachycardia show why the transfer still needs a careful risk assessment and deterioration plan.

Biomarkers contribute to risk assessment
High-sensitivity troponin is above the local decision cutoff. Lactate is 2.7 millimoles per liter. An elevated troponin contributes information about the cardiac response in this setting. It does not select reperfusion treatment on its own, especially with recent surgery in the history.

Confirmed PE with right-heart strain
A synthetic CT pulmonary angiography report confirms bilateral emboli and an enlarged right ventricle. Troponin and right-heart findings contribute to risk assessment but do not alone dictate reperfusion therapy. The CT establishes emboli in this case. The right-heart finding adds concern, but the treatment discussion still has to use the whole clinical risk profile rather than the appearance of the clot alone.

Would you call this a routine transfer?
Does preserved blood pressure make this a routine low-risk transfer? Think about what would change your monitoring and destination even without hypotension. Then consider how the recent operation affects the next treatment discussion.

Coordinate treatment and a deterioration plan
A patient may maintain pressure despite significant right-ventricular strain. Risk assessment includes clinical status, trajectory, imaging and biomarkers; recent surgery also changes bleeding considerations. Support oxygenation and coordinate anticoagulation and monitoring with the treating team. Discuss risk and destination with the pulmonary embolism response or critical care team. Prepare for deterioration and document recent surgery and bleeding risk. A stable reading is a moment, not a guarantee about the journey. The plan should describe what the team will do if the circulation changes and which center can provide the next level of care.

Stable pressure still needs surveillance
Pressure remains stable during observation, but the team maintains monitoring and a deterioration plan. Treatment is individualized rather than decided by clot appearance alone. The absence of deterioration during observation is useful. It supports continued assessment rather than a claim that the right-heart findings or postoperative treatment risks have disappeared.

PE: preserved pressure can coexist with important RV risk
Case synopsis. Preserved pressure did not erase right-heart risk. The transfer plan accounted for trajectory, strain and postoperative bleeding considerations. The case asks us to interpret stability precisely: what is maintained now, what remains abnormal and what could change during transfer.
