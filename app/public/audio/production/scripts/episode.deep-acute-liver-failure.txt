You're listening to Acute liver failure: the liver, brain and circulation fail together. Synthetic failure, ammonia, cerebral edema, vasodilation and transplant urgency.

This is a level 5 critical-care session. The goal is not to memorize a list. The goal is to build a model that lets you predict what the patient will do next.

By the end, you should be able to integrate neurologic, metabolic, coagulation and circulatory consequences of acute liver failure; and recognize why early transplant-center involvement is part of critical care, not an afterthought.

Now: Acute failure is not chronic cirrhosis.

Acute liver failure. Acute hepatic failure couples coagulopathy, ammonia handling, cerebral edema risk, hypoglycemia, vasodilation and multiorgan dysfunction.

Build the mechanism first. Abrupt loss of hepatic synthetic and metabolic function can cause hyperammonemia, hypoglycemia, vasodilation, coagulopathy, renal failure and cerebral edema.

At the bedside, trend neurologic status, glucose, acid-base, renal function and hemodynamics while involving a transplant-capable center early.

Two traps to actively avoid: Treating elevated INR as the only measure of severity. And: Assuming chronic-cirrhosis physiology applies directly to acute failure.

The integration move is this: Acute liver failure is a brain-circulation-metabolism emergency with a time-sensitive transplant decision.

Keep these terms available in your mental model: ammonia, synthetic failure, cerebral edema.

What expert-level use of this concept looks like is this: Recognize the physiology that makes acute liver failure different from stable chronic cirrhosis. Prioritize neuroprotection, glucose control, hemodynamics and early transplant-center escalation.

ICP, CPP & cerebral perfusion. Cerebral perfusion depends on the gradient across the cerebral circulation and is influenced by pressure, venous outflow, CO2 and intracranial compliance.

Build the mechanism first. CPP is the pressure gradient available to drive cerebral blood flow and is influenced by arterial pressure, ICP, venous pressure and autoregulation. Intracranial compliance falls as compensatory reserve is exhausted.

At the bedside, interpret ICP as a dynamic system with waveform, position, venous drainage, CO2 and sedation effects. A “normal” MAP does not guarantee adequate cerebral perfusion.

Two traps to actively avoid: Treating ICP and CPP as isolated targets. And: Ignoring neck position, intrathoracic pressure and venous outflow.

The integration move is this: Protect the brain by preserving oxygenation and perfusion while minimizing factors that increase intracranial volume or venous pressure.

Keep these terms available in your mental model: CPP, autoregulation, Monro-Kellie.

What expert-level use of this concept looks like is this: Predict how systemic pressure, CO2 and venous pressure affect cerebral perfusion. Recognize when a normal MAP does not guarantee adequate CPP.

Now connect ICP, CPP & cerebral perfusion to Brain herniation physiology. Rising intracranial pressure and compartment shifts distort brain structures, impair perfusion and can produce rapidly evolving focal and brainstem signs.

The relationship matters because When intracranial pressure gradients exceed compensatory reserve, brain tissue shifts across fixed boundaries, compressing cranial nerves, vessels and brainstem structures. At the bedside, recognize trajectory—declining consciousness, pupillary asymmetry, motor changes and evolving brainstem physiology—while protecting oxygenation and cerebral perfusion.

Now connect ICP, CPP & cerebral perfusion to E V D management. External ventricular drainage requires correct leveling, clamping during movement and interpretation of drainage and ICP.

The relationship matters because An external ventricular drain uses a hydrostatic height reference to define the pressure at which CSF drains while also allowing ICP monitoring depending on system configuration. At the bedside, level to the ordered reference point, manage clamping during movement according to policy, and re-level after position changes before interpreting pressure or drainage.

Now: Coagulopathy is not synonymous with spontaneous bleeding risk.

Critical-illness coagulopathy. Bleeding and thrombosis reflect platelets, fibrinogen, coagulation factors, fibrinolysis, temperature, calcium, dilution and disease-specific pathways.

Build the mechanism first. Hemostasis depends on platelets, fibrinogen, coagulation factors, fibrinolysis, calcium, pH and temperature. Critical illness can produce simultaneous bleeding and thrombosis risk.

At the bedside, interpret conventional labs and viscoelastic tests in the clinical context and replace the failing component rather than one arbitrary number.

Two traps to actively avoid: Equating INR with whole-blood clot quality. And: Ignoring fibrinogen, platelet function, temperature and calcium.

The integration move is this: Coagulation is a dynamic system, not a single laboratory pathway.

Keep these terms available in your mental model: fibrinolysis, fibrinogen, viscoelastic testing.

What expert-level use of this concept looks like is this: Reason from the failing component of clot formation rather than treating INR alone. Anticipate temperature, calcium and dilution effects during hemorrhagic resuscitation.

Now connect Critical-illness coagulopathy to Massive transfusion & hemorrhagic resuscitation. Hemorrhagic resuscitation integrates source control, balanced component replacement, calcium, temperature, coagulation and perfusion.

The relationship matters because Major hemorrhage produces loss of oxygen-carrying capacity, volume and hemostatic components while hypothermia, hypocalcemia, dilution and acidosis can worsen coagulation. At the bedside, activate the hemorrhage system early, warm the resuscitation, track calcium/coagulation/temperature and never lose sight of definitive source control.

Now: Vasodilation and multiorgan failure.

Shock phenotyping. Identify inadequate effective circulation by integrating flow, vascular tone, congestion, oxygen delivery and organ response.

Build the mechanism first. Shock is inadequate effective tissue perfusion and oxygen utilization, not merely hypotension. Flow, vascular tone, capacitance, congestion and extraction can fail in different combinations.

At the bedside, phenotype the circulation repeatedly. A patient can move from one dominant mechanism to another during resuscitation.

Two traps to actively avoid: Naming shock once and never revisiting the model. And: Treating MAP as the only endpoint.

The integration move is this: Choose interventions that both treat the suspected mechanism and generate information about whether the model was correct.

Keep these terms available in your mental model: flow, tone, congestion, perfusion.

What expert-level use of this concept looks like is this: Recognize mixed shock. Choose an intervention that tests a physiologic hypothesis and define the response expected if that hypothesis is correct.

Now connect Shock phenotyping to Septic shock physiology. Distributive shock with dynamic changes in vascular tone, venous capacitance, myocardial performance, microcirculation and oxygen extraction.

The relationship matters because Septic shock can combine vasoplegia, venous pooling, myocardial dysfunction, endothelial leak and disturbed microcirculation. Cardiac output may be low, normal or high. At the bedside, reassess vascular tone, flow, congestion and perfusion after each intervention rather than assuming a fixed distributive phenotype.

Now connect Shock phenotyping to Right ventricular failure. Failure of a thin-walled ventricle that is highly sensitive to acute afterload, volume, perfusion pressure and ventricular interaction.

The relationship matters because The RV is thin-walled and highly sensitive to acute afterload. When it dilates, wall stress rises, coronary perfusion can fall and septal shift can reduce LV filling. At the bedside, track systemic pressure, venous congestion, echo geometry, oxygenation, CO2, acid-base status and ventilator pressures together. Small changes in P V R or intrathoracic pressure can have large hemodynamic consequences.

Now: Support while moving toward transplant.

C R R T physiology & prescription literacy. Continuous renal replacement uses diffusion and/or convection to manage solute, acid-base and fluid balance in unstable patients.

Build the mechanism first. C R R T uses diffusion, convection and ultrafiltration to control solute, acid-base and volume continuously. Prescribed dose can differ substantially from delivered dose.

At the bedside, separate total effluent from net patient fluid removal and interpret access, filter and return pressures as clues to the physical circuit.

Two traps to actively avoid: Calling every effluent liter “fluid removed from the patient.” And: Ignoring downtime and clotting when assessing delivered therapy.

The integration move is this: C R R T is both renal support and a hemodynamic intervention.

Keep these terms available in your mental model: effluent dose, CVVH, CVVHD, CVVHDF, filtration fraction.

What expert-level use of this concept looks like is this: Explain diffusion versus convection and net ultrafiltration. Recognize how circuit, anticoagulation and prescribed versus delivered dose change therapy.
