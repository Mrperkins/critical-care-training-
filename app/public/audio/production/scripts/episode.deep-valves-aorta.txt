Now: Severe stenosis and fixed forward flow.

Critical valvular disease. Severe stenosis or regurgitation creates fixed or dynamic loading constraints that make usual shock interventions behave differently.

Build the mechanism first. Critical stenosis creates fixed outflow obstruction while acute regurgitation abruptly raises upstream pressure and reduces effective forward flow; loading interventions therefore have lesion-specific effects.

At the bedside, identify the lesion and dominant problem—fixed output, pulmonary edema, low coronary perfusion or regurgitant volume—before applying generic shock treatment.

Two traps to actively avoid: Aggressively reducing preload in severe fixed obstruction. And: Treating acute severe regurgitation like chronic compensated valve disease.

The integration move is this: Valve anatomy defines the permitted hemodynamic strategy.

Keep these terms available in your mental model: aortic stenosis, acute MR, afterload, forward flow.

What expert-level use of this concept looks like is this: Recognize hemodynamic priorities in severe AS versus acute MR/AR. Avoid interventions that worsen the lesion-specific loading problem.

Now: Dissection and aneurysmal catastrophe.

Aortic dissection & aneurysmal catastrophe. Acute aortic syndromes combine vascular-wall failure with risks of rupture, malperfusion, tamponade and acute valvular dysfunction.

Build the mechanism first. Aortic wall disruption or aneurysmal rupture can cause hemorrhage, branch-vessel malperfusion, acute aortic regurgitation or tamponade.

At the bedside, reduce shear/impulse when appropriate while preserving organ perfusion and accelerating definitive surgical/endovascular evaluation.

Two traps to actively avoid: Lowering blood pressure without controlling tachycardia/shear in dissection. And: Missing tamponade or malperfusion because chest pain has improved.

The integration move is this: The aorta can fail as both a conduit and a source of catastrophic secondary organ ischemia.

Keep these terms available in your mental model: impulse control, malperfusion, rupture.

What expert-level use of this concept looks like is this: Recognize branch-vessel and pericardial complications. Control shear stress while preserving end-organ perfusion and expediting definitive care.

Now: Malperfusion, tamponade and valve failure.

Cardiac tamponade physiology. Pericardial pressure constrains chamber filling, couples the ventricles and makes venous return highly sensitive to pressure gradients.

Build the mechanism first. When pericardial pressure approaches chamber filling pressure, diastolic filling becomes constrained and the ventricles compete within a fixed space.

At the bedside, interpret echo and hemodynamics together, especially respiratory variation, chamber collapse and venous congestion.

Two traps to actively avoid: Waiting for the complete classic triad. And: Ignoring how positive pressure can further reduce venous return.

The integration move is this: Tamponade is a pressure-gradient problem whose definitive solution is relief of pericardial constraint.

Keep these terms available in your mental model: pericardial pressure, respiratory variation, diastolic collapse.

What expert-level use of this concept looks like is this: Explain tamponade as a filling-pressure problem rather than a checklist of classic signs. Predict effects of positive pressure and volume changes on a preload-limited circulation.

Now connect Cardiac tamponade physiology to Ventricular interdependence. The ventricles share a septum and constrained pericardial space, so pressure or volume loading of one changes filling and geometry of the other.

The relationship matters because The ventricles share a septum and a constrained pericardial space. Acute RV pressure or volume loading can shift the septum and reduce LV diastolic filling. At the bedside, a falling LV stroke volume in RV failure may occur despite apparently adequate intravascular volume because geometry, not supply, is limiting filling.

Now: Support without worsening the lesion.

Cardiogenic shock. Pump failure where restoring perfusion must be balanced against congestion, myocardial oxygen demand, coronary perfusion and mechanical complications.

Build the mechanism first. Cardiogenic shock is inadequate forward flow from pump failure, often accompanied by elevated filling pressures and congestion. RV, LV and biventricular phenotypes behave differently.

At the bedside, track flow, pressure, congestion, coronary perfusion, rhythm and mechanical complications; escalate support when pharmacology cannot restore effective circulation.

Two traps to actively avoid: Treating all cardiogenic shock with the same inotrope/pressor pattern. And: Ignoring congestion while chasing blood pressure.

The integration move is this: The support strategy should match the failing ventricle and the cause of pump failure.

Keep these terms available in your mental model: cardiac power output, congestion, inotropy.

What expert-level use of this concept looks like is this: Phenotype left-, right- and biventricular pump failure. Choose support based on pressure, flow, congestion and the cause of pump failure.

Now connect Cardiogenic shock to Right ventricular failure. Failure of a thin-walled ventricle that is highly sensitive to acute afterload, volume, perfusion pressure and ventricular interaction.

The relationship matters because The RV is thin-walled and highly sensitive to acute afterload. When it dilates, wall stress rises, coronary perfusion can fall and septal shift can reduce LV filling. At the bedside, track systemic pressure, venous congestion, echo geometry, oxygenation, CO2, acid-base status and ventilator pressures together. Small changes in P V R or intrathoracic pressure can have large hemodynamic consequences.

Now connect Cardiogenic shock to Vasopressor & inotrope physiology. Vasoactive drugs change arterial tone, venous tone, heart rate, contractility and regional flow; the desired effect depends on the shock model.

The relationship matters because Vasoactive drugs alter arterial tone, venous tone, contractility and heart rate to different degrees. The same MAP response can hide very different effects on flow and myocardial demand. At the bedside, choose the agent from the dominant deficit and define what improvement and toxicity should look like before titration.
