Now connect Cardiogenic shock to Right ventricular failure. Failure of a thin-walled ventricle that is highly sensitive to acute afterload, volume, perfusion pressure and ventricular interaction.

The relationship matters because The RV is thin-walled and highly sensitive to acute afterload. When it dilates, wall stress rises, coronary perfusion can fall and septal shift can reduce LV filling. At the bedside, track systemic pressure, venous congestion, echo geometry, oxygenation, CO2, acid-base status and ventilator pressures together. Small changes in P V R or intrathoracic pressure can have large hemodynamic consequences.

Now connect Cardiogenic shock to Vasopressor & inotrope physiology. Vasoactive drugs change arterial tone, venous tone, heart rate, contractility and regional flow; the desired effect depends on the shock model.

The relationship matters because Vasoactive drugs alter arterial tone, venous tone, contractility and heart rate to different degrees. The same MAP response can hide very different effects on flow and myocardial demand. At the bedside, choose the agent from the dominant deficit and define what improvement and toxicity should look like before titration.

Stress-test the model. Imagine the patient changes in a way that makes treating all cardiogenic shock with the same inotrope/pressor pattern tempting. Before acting, state the mechanism you think is operating, predict what your intervention should change, and name the finding that would make you stop and reconsider. Now: Cardioversion, pacing, pharmacology or reperfusion?.

Rhythm & conduction emergencies. Tachy- and bradyarrhythmias become critical when they impair filling, output or perfusion; treatment depends on rhythm mechanism and patient stability.

Build the mechanism first. Rhythm affects cardiac output through rate, filling time, AV synchrony and ventricular activation; the same rate can be tolerated or catastrophic depending on reserve.

At the bedside, first ask whether the rhythm is causing instability or compensating for another problem, then identify the electrical mechanism and choose synchronization, pacing or medication accordingly.

Two traps to actively avoid: Treating compensatory sinus tachycardia as the primary disease. And: Using a rate target without considering filling, stroke volume and perfusion.

The integration move is this: Rhythm management is hemodynamic management performed through electrophysiology.

Keep these terms available in your mental model: AV conduction, reentry, wide complex, synchronization.

What expert-level use of this concept looks like is this: Distinguish electrical instability from compensatory tachycardia. Choose cardioversion, pacing or pharmacology based on rhythm and physiology.
