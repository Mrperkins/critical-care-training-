Now: HIT: low platelets, high thrombosis risk.

Heparin-induced thrombocytopenia. HIT is an immune prothrombotic disorder in which falling platelets signal thrombosis risk rather than simple bleeding risk.

Build the mechanism first. HIT antibodies activate platelets through PF4 complexes, making thrombocytopenia a marker of a strongly prothrombotic immune syndrome.

At the bedside, estimate pretest probability first, stop heparin and use appropriate non-heparin anticoagulation when probability warrants while confirmatory testing proceeds.

Two traps to actively avoid: Ordering HIT testing for every ICU platelet fall. And: Withholding anticoagulation simply because the platelet count is low in likely HIT.

The integration move is this: The feared complication is thrombosis, not the platelet number itself.

Keep these terms available in your mental model: 4Ts, PF4, non-heparin anticoagulation.

What expert-level use of this concept looks like is this: Estimate pretest probability before ordering confirmatory tests. Stop heparin and manage thrombosis risk when clinical probability is meaningful.

Now: Tumor lysis as metabolic catastrophe.

Oncologic critical-care emergencies. Tumor lysis, leukostasis, hyperviscosity, malignant airway/vascular obstruction and treatment toxicities can cause rapidly reversible organ failure.

Build the mechanism first. Cancer can cause abrupt metabolic, rheologic or compressive emergencies including tumor lysis, leukostasis and malignant airway/vascular obstruction.

At the bedside, recognize the syndrome early, stabilize organ physiology and coordinate disease-specific therapy such as cytoreduction or metabolic control.

Two traps to actively avoid: Treating tumor lysis electrolyte abnormalities one by one without controlling ongoing cell breakdown. And: Missing leukostasis because total leukocyte count is not extreme.

The integration move is this: The malignancy can be the direct mechanism of organ failure, not merely background history.

Keep these terms available in your mental model: tumor lysis, leukostasis, hyperviscosity.

What expert-level use of this concept looks like is this: Recognize metabolic and mechanical oncologic syndromes early. Coordinate disease-specific therapy with organ support.

Potassium disorders. Serum potassium reflects intake, excretion and transcellular shifts; dyskalemia becomes dangerous through membrane and cardiac electrophysiology.

Build the mechanism first. Potassium gradients determine membrane excitability. Serum concentration reflects total-body balance plus rapid transcellular shifts driven by insulin, catecholamines, acid-base state and cell injury.

At the bedside, when dangerous ECG or muscle findings are present, separate immediate membrane stabilization, temporary redistribution and definitive potassium removal.

Two traps to actively avoid: Assuming a normal ECG eliminates severe hyperkalemia risk. And: Confusing a transcellular shift with total-body potassium excess or depletion.

The integration move is this: Treat the membrane now while solving where the potassium came from and where it can go.

Keep these terms available in your mental model: transcellular shift, membrane potential, repolarization.

What expert-level use of this concept looks like is this: Separate redistribution from total-body gain/loss. Prioritize membrane stabilization, redistribution and elimination by mechanism.

Now connect Potassium disorders to Diabetic ketoacidosis physiology. Insulin deficiency produces ketogenesis, osmotic diuresis, electrolyte shifts and a high-anion-gap acidosis whose treatment changes potassium and water distribution.

The relationship matters because Insulin deficiency and counter-regulatory hormones drive ketogenesis, osmotic diuresis and major total-body electrolyte deficits even when serum levels initially appear normal or high. At the bedside, track volume status, potassium, sodium/osmolality, anion gap and acid-base trajectory through treatment.

Acute kidney injury. AKI is a syndrome of impaired filtration and homeostasis whose cause may be hemodynamic, intrinsic, obstructive or mixed.

Build the mechanism first. AKI represents impaired filtration and homeostasis from hemodynamic, tubular, interstitial, vascular, obstructive or mixed mechanisms; creatinine is delayed and does not identify cause.

At the bedside, use trajectory, urine output, hemodynamics, nephrotoxins, urinalysis and obstruction assessment to build the phenotype, then support volume/electrolyte/acid-base physiology.

Two traps to actively avoid: Calling all oliguria “pre-renal.” And: Starting or withholding renal support based on creatinine alone.

The integration move is this: Kidney injury is a syndrome; the treatment target is the mechanism and the homeostatic consequence.

Keep these terms available in your mental model: filtration, renal perfusion, tubular injury, obstruction.

What expert-level use of this concept looks like is this: Build an AKI differential from perfusion, nephrotoxins, urinalysis and obstruction. Recognize when kidney support is needed for physiology rather than a creatinine threshold.

Now: Bleeding and clotting can coexist.

Critical-illness coagulopathy. Bleeding and thrombosis reflect platelets, fibrinogen, coagulation factors, fibrinolysis, temperature, calcium, dilution and disease-specific pathways.

Build the mechanism first. Hemostasis depends on platelets, fibrinogen, coagulation factors, fibrinolysis, calcium, pH and temperature. Critical illness can produce simultaneous bleeding and thrombosis risk.

At the bedside, interpret conventional labs and viscoelastic tests in the clinical context and replace the failing component rather than one arbitrary number.

Two traps to actively avoid: Equating INR with whole-blood clot quality. And: Ignoring fibrinogen, platelet function, temperature and calcium.

The integration move is this: Coagulation is a dynamic system, not a single laboratory pathway.

Keep these terms available in your mental model: fibrinolysis, fibrinogen, viscoelastic testing.

Now connect Critical-illness coagulopathy to Massive transfusion & hemorrhagic resuscitation. Hemorrhagic resuscitation integrates source control, balanced component replacement, calcium, temperature, coagulation and perfusion.

The relationship matters because Major hemorrhage produces loss of oxygen-carrying capacity, volume and hemostatic components while hypothermia, hypocalcemia, dilution and acidosis can worsen coagulation. At the bedside, activate the hemorrhage system early, warm the resuscitation, track calcium/coagulation/temperature and never lose sight of definitive source control.
