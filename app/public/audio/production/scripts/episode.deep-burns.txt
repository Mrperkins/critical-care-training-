Now: Estimate injury, then stop worshiping the formula.

Burn shock & resuscitation. Major burns create capillary leak, evaporative loss, inflammatory shock and evolving fluid requirements that cannot be managed by formula alone.

Build the mechanism first. Major burns create inflammatory capillary leak, evaporative loss and changing vascular permeability. Formula estimates cannot account for individual response or fluid creep.

At the bedside, use a formula as an initial estimate, then titrate to perfusion and organ endpoints while watching for over-resuscitation.

Two traps to actively avoid: Treating the formula as a fixed prescription. And: Chasing urine output with unlimited crystalloid despite worsening edema/compartment physiology.

The integration move is this: Burn resuscitation is dynamic shock management, not arithmetic.

Keep these terms available in your mental model: TBSA, burn shock, urine output.

What expert-level use of this concept looks like is this: Use formulas only as a starting estimate and titrate to physiologic response. Recognize both under-resuscitation and fluid creep.

Shock phenotyping. Identify inadequate effective circulation by integrating flow, vascular tone, congestion, oxygen delivery and organ response.

Build the mechanism first. Shock is inadequate effective tissue perfusion and oxygen utilization, not merely hypotension. Flow, vascular tone, capacitance, congestion and extraction can fail in different combinations.

At the bedside, phenotype the circulation repeatedly. A patient can move from one dominant mechanism to another during resuscitation.

Two traps to actively avoid: Naming shock once and never revisiting the model. And: Treating MAP as the only endpoint.

The integration move is this: Choose interventions that both treat the suspected mechanism and generate information about whether the model was correct.

Keep these terms available in your mental model: flow, tone, congestion, perfusion.

What expert-level use of this concept looks like is this: Recognize mixed shock. Choose an intervention that tests a physiologic hypothesis and define the response expected if that hypothesis is correct.

Now connect Shock phenotyping to Septic shock physiology. Distributive shock with dynamic changes in vascular tone, venous capacitance, myocardial performance, microcirculation and oxygen extraction.

The relationship matters because Septic shock can combine vasoplegia, venous pooling, myocardial dysfunction, endothelial leak and disturbed microcirculation. Cardiac output may be low, normal or high. At the bedside, reassess vascular tone, flow, congestion and perfusion after each intervention rather than assuming a fixed distributive phenotype.

Now connect Shock phenotyping to Right ventricular failure. Failure of a thin-walled ventricle that is highly sensitive to acute afterload, volume, perfusion pressure and ventricular interaction.

The relationship matters because The RV is thin-walled and highly sensitive to acute afterload. When it dilates, wall stress rises, coronary perfusion can fall and septal shift can reduce LV filling. At the bedside, track systemic pressure, venous congestion, echo geometry, oxygenation, CO2, acid-base status and ventilator pressures together. Small changes in P V R or intrathoracic pressure can have large hemodynamic consequences.
