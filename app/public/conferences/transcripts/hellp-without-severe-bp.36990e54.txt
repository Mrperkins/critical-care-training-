Pain persists after delivery
Case conference. Postpartum HELLP syndrome with nonsevere hypertension. This is a fictional teaching case, with clinical review pending. A 26-year-old patient is 18 hours postpartum. Persistent upper abdominal pain, nausea and profound malaise continue after delivery. The blood pressure has not reached the severe range, and the initial concern is postoperative discomfort. The bedside nurse requests review because upper abdominal pain and profound malaise persist after delivery. Petechiae appear at recent puncture sites. The pressure is only mildly elevated, but it does not explain or dismiss the severe platelet and hemolysis abnormalities. This conference concerns postpartum HELLP, not routine postoperative discomfort. The delivery is complete and the pressure does not look dramatic. Those two facts can make continued pain sound less urgent than it is. Let the examination and laboratory pattern have their own voice.

The examination adds a bleeding clue
The patient is pale with right upper quadrant tenderness and new petechiae around venipuncture sites. Mental status and breathing are currently normal. The petechiae are a small visible finding that connects to a much larger laboratory problem. Do not let the patient’s current ability to talk comfortably substitute for a systemic assessment.

Nonsevere pressure cannot clear organ injury
Heart rate is 108 per minute. A resting adult heart rate is commonly 60 to 100 beats per minute. Pregnancy, pain and medications can shift the baseline. This is mild tachycardia, consistent with pain or systemic illness and worth trending with bleeding and perfusion findings. Tachycardia may accompany pain, anxiety or reduced circulating volume; weak pulses, cool skin and oliguria would add evidence of poor perfusion. Blood pressure is 142 over 90. In pregnancy, a pressure below 140 over 90 is below the hypertension threshold. Severe hypertension is a systolic pressure of at least 160 or a diastolic pressure of at least 110; either component qualifies. This is hypertension but does not meet the severe threshold. HELLP can still be severe despite this pressure. Abdominal pain, malaise, nausea and bruising can be the dominant presentation without extreme hypertension. Saturation is 98 percent. At sea level, oxygen saturation is usually 95 to 100 percent in a person without a special oxygen target. Verify signal quality and the oxygen support in use. This is normal measured oxygenation. It does not reduce the urgency of hemolysis and severe thrombocytopenia. Hypoxemia can cause restlessness, tachycardia or cyanosis; oxygen saturation alone does not measure perfusion or cardiac output. The pressure is information, but it is not an exclusion test for HELLP. We need to see whether the symptoms and other evidence point to significant disease despite that reading.

The laboratory pattern carries the urgency
Platelet count is 48 billion per liter. An adult platelet count is commonly about 150 to 400 times ten to the ninth per liter. In preeclampsia, a count below 100 is a severe-feature threshold. This is severe thrombocytopenia, matching the petechiae and creating bleeding and procedural concerns. Thrombocytopenia may cause petechiae, bruising or mucosal bleeding, but significant depletion can exist without visible bleeding. A S T is 340 units per liter. The adult AST teaching reference is 12 to 38 units per liter; local assay limits vary. More than twice the upper limit is relevant to preeclampsia assessment. This is markedly elevated AST, supporting hepatic injury rather than isolated postpartum discomfort. Liver injury in this setting may accompany right upper quadrant pain, nausea or malaise; severe escalating pain warrants evaluation for hepatic complications. L D H is 1080 units per liter. The adult LDH teaching reference is 45 to 200 units per liter, with substantial assay variation. In HELLP, an LDH of at least 600 is commonly used as evidence supporting hemolysis. This is markedly elevated. Alongside low haptoglobin it supports hemolysis, while other causes of tissue injury must still be considered. Hemolysis may cause fatigue, pallor, jaundice or dark urine; pallor and malaise are observed. Haptoglobin is low, supporting hemolysis in this context. Haptoglobin is normally detectable within an assay-specific interval; no universal qualitative normal number is supplied here. A low result supports consumption as haptoglobin binds free hemoglobin during intravascular hemolysis. Liver disease and other causes can also lower it. Hemolysis may cause pallor, jaundice and dark urine; interpret this qualitative result alongside LDH, smear and the blood count. These results are markedly abnormal in a way that fits the ongoing symptoms. The hemolysis evidence, liver injury and platelet count deserve urgent interpretation even without a severe pressure reading.

Postpartum HELLP and its mimics
HELLP can present postpartum and without severe hypertension. Other thrombotic microangiopathies and liver disorders must also be considered by the specialist team.  There is still diagnostic work to do. The appropriate response to that uncertainty is expert evaluation and close monitoring, rather than waiting for the blood pressure to become more convincing.

Escalate on the evidence already present
The absence of severe hypertension does not negate major platelet, liver and hemolysis abnormalities. Delivery also does not guarantee immediate resolution. Obtain urgent obstetric and critical care review and trend the laboratory abnormalities. Assess for active bleeding and evolving hepatic or neurological complications. Plan monitored transfer and specialist management rather than waiting for severe hypertension to develop. The clinical risk comes from the syndrome and its trajectory. The handoff should make clear why a relatively modest pressure reading has not reduced the required level of care.

Follow what happens after the first panel
The platelet count is repeated and the clinical course is monitored closely. The handoff emphasizes the postpartum timing and the trajectory of symptoms and laboratories. Obstetrics and critical care repeat blood counts, liver and coagulation tests, assess for bleeding and consider the broader postpartum thrombotic microangiopathy differential. Blood-bank and anesthesia teams are involved according to procedural and bleeding needs. The synthetic course shows no focal hepatic hemorrhage; the platelet count and hemolysis markers recover over the next several days with specialist management. Failure to improve would trigger reassessment for conditions such as TTP or complement-mediated hemolytic uremic syndrome rather than preserving the HELLP label at all costs. Delivery does not promise an immediate end to the illness. Reassessment must be able to detect persistence or progression instead of treating postpartum status as a conclusion.
