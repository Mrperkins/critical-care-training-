Breathlessness after surgery
Case conference. Breathlessness after surgery. This is a fictional teaching case, with clinical review pending. A 61-year-old patient is eight days after hip surgery. Sudden dyspnea and pleuritic discomfort develop at home. The patient is tachycardic and hypoxemic but not hypotensive.

What do you notice?
At the bedside. Breath sounds are clear and one calf is swollen. The team evaluates pulmonary embolism while considering other postoperative complications.

Vital signs
The initial vital signs are as follows. Heart rate is 120 per minute. Blood pressure is 118 over 74. Respiratory rate is 30 per minute. Saturation is 88 percent on room air. These are observations at this point in the case, rather than treatment targets.

Laboratory findings
Laboratory findings. High-sensitivity troponin is above the local decision cutoff. Lactate is 2.7 millimoles per liter. Interpret these results alongside the examination and the timing of sampling.

Diagnostic findings
Diagnostic findings. A synthetic CT pulmonary angiography report confirms bilateral emboli and an enlarged right ventricle. Troponin and right-heart findings contribute to risk assessment but do not alone dictate reperfusion therapy.

What would you prioritize?
Think it through. Does preserved blood pressure make this a routine low-risk transfer? Consider your answer while the case continues. No screen interaction is needed.

Clinical reasoning & priorities
Conference discussion. A patient may maintain pressure despite significant right-ventricular strain. Risk assessment includes clinical status, trajectory, imaging and biomarkers; recent surgery also changes bleeding considerations. Priorities. 1. Support oxygenation and coordinate anticoagulation and monitoring with the treating team. 2. Discuss risk and destination with the pulmonary embolism response or critical care team. 3. Prepare for deterioration and document recent surgery and bleeding risk.

Reassessment & handoff
Reassessment and handoff. Pressure remains stable during observation, but the team maintains monitoring and a deterioration plan. Treatment is individualized rather than decided by clot appearance alone. This is an authored fictional course; improvement is not guaranteed in a real patient.
