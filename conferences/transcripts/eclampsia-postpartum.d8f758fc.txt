A 32-year-old patient is five days postpartum after an apparently uncomplicated delivery. A severe headache and visual disturbance are followed by a generalized seizure. The family did not realize a pregnancy-related emergency could occur after discharge. A partner calls EMS after headache and flashing lights are followed by a generalized convulsion. The baby was delivered five days earlier, and the family initially omits that history because the pregnancy is over. EMS protects the patient from injury and finds severe hypertension after the seizure. The working diagnosis is postpartum eclampsia while intracranial hemorrhage and venous thrombosis remain active alternatives. Start with the detail that can be missed in a generic seizure handoff: delivery was five days ago. The pregnancy history is part of the present emergency even though the patient is home.

A seizure with severe postpartum hypertension
Heart rate is 118 per minute. A resting adult heart rate is commonly 60 to 100 beats per minute. Pregnancy, pain and medications can shift the baseline. This is tachycardia after a seizure. Adrenergic stress is plausible, but perfusion and rhythm are reassessed rather than attributed automatically to the postictal state. Tachycardia may accompany pain, anxiety or reduced circulating volume; weak pulses, cool skin and oliguria would add evidence of poor perfusion. Blood pressure is 182 over 116. In pregnancy, a pressure below 140 over 90 is below the hypertension threshold. Severe hypertension is a systolic pressure of at least 160 or a diastolic pressure of at least 110; either component qualifies. Both components are severe, including in this postpartum setting. The pressure raises the risks of stroke and further neurological injury. Headache, visual symptoms, seizures and altered consciousness can accompany hypertensive obstetric disease; the first three preceded this assessment. Respiratory rate is 24 per minute. This resting adult respiratory-rate reference is 12 to 18 breaths per minute; assess depth and effort as well as the count. This is tachypnea after the seizure. Assess airway patency, aspiration risk and ventilation rather than assuming every postictal patient ventilates effectively. Seizures may be followed by obstruction, hypoventilation or aspiration; gurgling, poor chest movement or new crackles would be important. Saturation is 93 percent on room air. At sea level, oxygen saturation is usually 95 to 100 percent in a person without a special oxygen target. Verify signal quality and the oxygen support in use. This is below the usual oxygenation reference on room air. Seizure-associated hypoventilation or aspiration may contribute and need reassessment. Hypoxemia can cause restlessness, tachycardia or cyanosis; oxygen saturation alone does not measure perfusion or cardiac output. The pressure supports an urgent obstetric concern in this setting. At the same time, the respiratory assessment after the seizure remains an immediate bedside task.

Normal-looking results do not close the case
Glucose is 106 milligrams per deciliter. For a random sample after the first month, this laboratory lists glucose from 65 to 139 milligrams per deciliter. Fasting reference limits and treatment thresholds differ. This random value is in range. Measured hypoglycemia is not the explanation for this seizure. Hypoglycemia may cause sweating, confusion or convulsions, which is why a bedside glucose is checked promptly. Platelet count is 156 billion per liter. An adult platelet count is commonly about 150 to 400 times ten to the ninth per liter. In preeclampsia, a count below 100 is a severe-feature threshold. This count is in the adult reference. A normal count does not exclude eclampsia or remove the need to treat the seizure and hypertension. Thrombocytopenia may cause petechiae, bruising or mucosal bleeding, but significant depletion can exist without visible bleeding. Creatinine is 1.0 milligrams per deciliter. A general adult creatinine reference is 0.6 to 1.2 milligrams per deciliter. Pregnancy usually lowers creatinine; a value above 1.1 or doubling from baseline is a preeclampsia severe-feature criterion without another renal cause. This is within the general adult interval and below the stated obstetric severe-feature cutoff. Its postpartum trend and urine output still matter. Renal dysfunction can reduce magnesium clearance, producing reduced reflexes and respiratory depression during excess exposure; monitor clinically and by the pathway. A glucose check addresses one immediate alternative. A platelet count that is not markedly low does not remove eclampsia from the differential. The symptom sequence still carries major weight.

Postpartum eclampsia remains possible
Postpartum timing does not exclude eclampsia. Atypical features, focal deficits or persistent altered mental status increase the need for urgent neurological evaluation and imaging after stabilization.  The diagnosis is approached through timing and clinical features, while other causes are evaluated. Persistent altered consciousness or focal findings should change how urgently we pursue additional neurological assessment.

Support breathing, treat seizure risk and pressure
Eclampsia can occur postpartum and without prior recognition of preeclampsia. Simultaneous evaluation of alternative seizure causes prevents premature closure. Protect airway and ventilation, prevent injury and treat suspected eclampsia with magnesium under protocol. Treat persistent severe hypertension and arrange immediate obstetric consultation. Reassess neurological recovery and pursue urgent imaging or additional diagnostics when indicated. Parallel care is the key.
