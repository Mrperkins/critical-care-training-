You're listening to The patient survived the ICU—now they cannot stand. Critical-illness myopathy, neuropathy, sedation, immobility and recovery.

This is a level 3 critical-care session. The goal is not to memorize a list. The goal is to build a model that lets you predict what the patient will do next.

By the end, you should be able to recognize icu-acquired weakness as a predictable organ-system consequence; and connect daily icu choices to ventilator liberation and long-term recovery.

Now: Why weakness develops.

ICU-acquired weakness & recovery. Critical illness, immobility, inflammation, sedation and organ failure can produce profound weakness and long-term functional loss.

Build the mechanism first. Inflammation, immobility, neuropathy, myopathy, sedation and undernutrition can cause profound ICU-acquired weakness and prolonged post-ICU disability.

At the bedside, build mobility, delirium prevention, nutrition and sedation minimization into daily care rather than starting “rehab” only after organ failure resolves.

Two traps to actively avoid: Interpreting generalized weakness only as deconditioning. And: Separating recovery planning from ventilator liberation.

The integration move is this: Survival is not the only outcome; function is a critical-care endpoint.

Keep these terms available in your mental model: critical illness myopathy, critical illness neuropathy, PICS.

What expert-level use of this concept looks like is this: Recognize weakness as an ICU outcome, not merely a discharge problem. Connect sedation, mobility, nutrition and ventilator liberation to recovery.

Tie this back to the patient. Ask what variable is changing, what downstream effect you expect, and what bedside finding would support or contradict that model.

Now: Weakness can become a ventilation problem.

Liberation from mechanical ventilation. Liberation requires separating readiness, spontaneous breathing performance, airway protection and post-extubation risk.

Build the mechanism first. A spontaneous breathing trial tests whether the patient can assume the work of breathing, but extubation also requires airway protection and manageable post-extubation risk.

At the bedside, when a trial fails, distinguish respiratory muscle load, cardiac dysfunction, weakness, sedation, airway issues and unresolved disease.

Two traps to actively avoid: Repeating failed SBTs without diagnosing why. And: Equating SBT success with guaranteed extubation success.

The integration move is this: Liberation is a diagnostic stress test followed by an airway decision.

Keep these terms available in your mental model: SBT, RSBI, airway protection.

What expert-level use of this concept looks like is this: Distinguish failure of respiratory mechanics from cardiac, neurologic or airway causes of failed liberation. Plan extubation support based on risk rather than a single breathing-trial number.

Tie this back to the patient. Ask what variable is changing, what downstream effect you expect, and what bedside finding would support or contradict that model.

Now: Sedation, mobility and delirium.

Delirium, sleep & mobility. Brain dysfunction in critical illness is shaped by disease, drugs, sleep disruption, immobility and environment.

Build the mechanism first. Delirium reflects acute brain dysfunction shaped by illness, medications, sleep disruption, sensory deprivation, immobility and environmental stressors.

At the bedside, identify reversible drivers, lighten unnecessary sedation, restore sleep-wake cues, mobilize when feasible and involve family/orientation strategies.

Two traps to actively avoid: Calling hypoactive delirium “calm.” And: Treating delirium exclusively with sedating medication.

The integration move is this: Brain recovery depends on the entire ICU environment, not one drug.

Keep these terms available in your mental model: CAM-ICU, ABCDE/ABCDEF bundle.

What expert-level use of this concept looks like is this: Recognize modifiable drivers of delirium. Integrate sedation reduction, sleep, mobility and family engagement into daily ICU care.

Tie this back to the patient. Ask what variable is changing, what downstream effect you expect, and what bedside finding would support or contradict that model.

Now: Nutrition and rehabilitation.

ICU nutrition & refeeding risk. Nutrition in critical illness is a metabolic therapy with timing, route, protein, glycemic and electrolyte consequences.

Build the mechanism first. Critical illness changes protein turnover, insulin sensitivity and energy use. Nutrition cannot fully reverse catabolism but route, timing and protein strategy influence recovery.

At the bedside, prefer enteral feeding when feasible, assess aspiration/hemodynamic risk and monitor tolerance rather than chasing an exact calorie number immediately.

Two traps to actively avoid: Equating calories with adequate nutrition. And: Overfeeding unstable patients or missing refeeding risk.

The integration move is this: Nutrition, mobility and ventilator liberation are connected recovery interventions.

Keep these terms available in your mental model: enteral nutrition, protein target, caloric debt.

What expert-level use of this concept looks like is this: Explain why early enteral nutrition is often preferred when feasible. Recognize when feeding strategy, aspiration risk or hemodynamic instability changes the plan.

Tie this back to the patient. Ask what variable is changing, what downstream effect you expect, and what bedside finding would support or contradict that model.
