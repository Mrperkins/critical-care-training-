Now: Four questions for any lesion.

Critical congenital heart disease physiology. Critical congenital lesions are best understood by whether systemic or pulmonary blood flow depends on shunts, mixing or ductal patency.

Build the mechanism first. Critical congenital lesions can be organized by whether systemic flow, pulmonary flow or mixing depends on a shunt/ductus and by how Qp and Qs respond to resistance changes.

At the bedside, think topology before lesion names: where can blood go, where must it mix, and what changes if P V R or S V R moves?

Two traps to actively avoid: Treating oxygen as universally beneficial without considering shunt balance. And: Memorizing lesions without understanding circulation geometry.

Now: Oxygen and ventilation can change shunt balance.

Pulmonary vascular resistance. Resistance faced by the RV, influenced by lung volume, oxygenation, CO2, pH, pulmonary vascular disease and mechanical ventilation.

Build the mechanism first. Pulmonary vascular resistance reflects the combined behavior of alveolar and extra-alveolar vessels and is affected by lung volume, oxygen, CO2, pH and pulmonary vascular disease.

At the bedside, both severe derecruitment and overdistension can raise RV afterload. Hypoxemia, hypercapnia and acidosis can compound the problem.

Two traps to actively avoid: Assuming more peep always lowers P V R by recruiting lung. And: Ignoring gas chemistry as an RV afterload intervention.

The integration move is this: Interpret ventilator changes through both lung mechanics and the pulmonary circulation.

Keep these terms available in your mental model: RV afterload, West zones.

What expert-level use of this concept looks like is this: Explain the U-shaped relationship between lung volume and pulmonary vascular resistance. Identify reversible contributors to acute RV afterload.

Now connect Pulmonary vascular resistance to Right ventricular failure. Failure of a thin-walled ventricle that is highly sensitive to acute afterload, volume, perfusion pressure and ventricular interaction.

The relationship matters because The RV is thin-walled and highly sensitive to acute afterload. When it dilates, wall stress rises, coronary perfusion can fall and septal shift can reduce LV filling. At the bedside, track systemic pressure, venous congestion, echo geometry, oxygenation, CO2, acid-base status and ventilator pressures together. Small changes in P V R or intrathoracic pressure can have large hemodynamic consequences.

Now connect Pulmonary vascular resistance to Positive-pressure ventilation & circulation. Positive intrathoracic pressure changes venous return, ventricular transmural pressures and pulmonary vascular resistance.

The relationship matters because Positive intrathoracic pressure can reduce the gradient for venous return, change transmural ventricular pressures and alter P V R depending on lung volume and recruitability. At the bedside, post-intubation hypotension may reflect vasodilation, reduced venous return, increased RV afterload, auto-peep or previously compensated shock becoming unmasked.
