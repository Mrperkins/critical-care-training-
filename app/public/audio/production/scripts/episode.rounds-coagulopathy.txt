You're listening to Coagulopathy: stop treating INR as the clot. Platelets, fibrinogen, factors, fibrinolysis, calcium and temperature work as a system.

This is a level 4 critical-care session. The goal is not to memorize a list. The goal is to build a model that lets you predict what the patient will do next.

By the end, you should be able to reason from the failing component of hemostasis; and anticipate resuscitation-induced coagulopathy before a single conventional lab value declares it.

Now: Build a clot in your head.

Critical-illness coagulopathy. Bleeding and thrombosis reflect platelets, fibrinogen, coagulation factors, fibrinolysis, temperature, calcium, dilution and disease-specific pathways.

Build the mechanism first. Hemostasis depends on platelets, fibrinogen, coagulation factors, fibrinolysis, calcium, pH and temperature. Critical illness can produce simultaneous bleeding and thrombosis risk.

At the bedside, interpret conventional labs and viscoelastic tests in the clinical context and replace the failing component rather than one arbitrary number.

Two traps to actively avoid: Equating INR with whole-blood clot quality. And: Ignoring fibrinogen, platelet function, temperature and calcium.

The integration move is this: Coagulation is a dynamic system, not a single laboratory pathway.

Keep these terms available in your mental model: fibrinolysis, fibrinogen, viscoelastic testing.

What expert-level use of this concept looks like is this: Reason from the failing component of clot formation rather than treating INR alone. Anticipate temperature, calcium and dilution effects during hemorrhagic resuscitation.

Tie this back to the patient. Ask what variable is changing, what downstream effect you expect, and what bedside finding would support or contradict that model.

Now: Temperature, calcium and pH.

Massive transfusion & hemorrhagic resuscitation. Hemorrhagic resuscitation integrates source control, balanced component replacement, calcium, temperature, coagulation and perfusion.

Build the mechanism first. Major hemorrhage produces loss of oxygen-carrying capacity, volume and hemostatic components while hypothermia, hypocalcemia, dilution and acidosis can worsen coagulation.

At the bedside, activate the hemorrhage system early, warm the resuscitation, track calcium/coagulation/temperature and never lose sight of definitive source control.

Two traps to actively avoid: Running product delivery as a logistics task disconnected from physiology. And: Allowing source control to become secondary to transfusion mechanics.

The integration move is this: Resuscitation buys time; source control ends the hemorrhage.

Keep these terms available in your mental model: M T P, fibrinogen, ionized calcium, lethal triad.

What expert-level use of this concept looks like is this: Run the resuscitation as a system rather than as isolated blood-unit administration. Recognize dilutional/coagulopathic, hypocalcemic and hypothermic deterioration early.

Tie this back to the patient. Ask what variable is changing, what downstream effect you expect, and what bedside finding would support or contradict that model.
