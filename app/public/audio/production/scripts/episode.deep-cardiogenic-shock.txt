This is a level 5 critical-care session. The goal is not to memorize a list. The goal is to build a model that lets you predict what the patient will do next.

By the end, you should be able to phenotype pump failure by flow, congestion and ventricle; and choose monitoring and support based on the failing physiology rather than a generic shock label.

Now: LV, RV and biventricular failure.

Cardiogenic shock. Pump failure where restoring perfusion must be balanced against congestion, myocardial oxygen demand, coronary perfusion and mechanical complications.

Build the mechanism first. Cardiogenic shock is inadequate forward flow from pump failure, often accompanied by elevated filling pressures and congestion. RV, LV and biventricular phenotypes behave differently.

At the bedside, track flow, pressure, congestion, coronary perfusion, rhythm and mechanical complications; escalate support when pharmacology cannot restore effective circulation.

Two traps to actively avoid: Treating all cardiogenic shock with the same inotrope/pressor pattern. And: Ignoring congestion while chasing blood pressure.

The integration move is this: The support strategy should match the failing ventricle and the cause of pump failure.

Keep these terms available in your mental model: cardiac power output, congestion, inotropy.

What expert-level use of this concept looks like is this: Phenotype left-, right- and biventricular pump failure. Choose support based on pressure, flow, congestion and the cause of pump failure.

Right ventricular failure. Failure of a thin-walled ventricle that is highly sensitive to acute afterload, volume, perfusion pressure and ventricular interaction.

Build the mechanism first. The RV is thin-walled and highly sensitive to acute afterload. When it dilates, wall stress rises, coronary perfusion can fall and septal shift can reduce LV filling.

At the bedside, track systemic pressure, venous congestion, echo geometry, oxygenation, CO2, acid-base status and ventilator pressures together. Small changes in P V R or intrathoracic pressure can have large hemodynamic consequences.

Two traps to actively avoid: Reflexively giving fluid to every hypotensive RV patient. And: Focusing on systemic pressure while ignoring worsening RV dilation and congestion.

The integration move is this: Every intervention should be judged by its effects on RV preload, RV afterload, contractility and coronary perfusion.

Keep these terms available in your mental model: RV-PA coupling, septal shift, transmural pressure.

What expert-level use of this concept looks like is this: Recognize RV shock physiology before systemic hypotension becomes profound. Predict how peep, hypercapnia, hypoxemia, fluids and vasoactives may change RV performance.

Tie this back to the patient. Ask what variable is changing, what downstream effect you expect, and what bedside finding would support or contradict that model.

Now: Pressor versus inotrope tradeoffs.

Vasopressor & inotrope physiology. Vasoactive drugs change arterial tone, venous tone, heart rate, contractility and regional flow; the desired effect depends on the shock model.

Build the mechanism first. Vasoactive drugs alter arterial tone, venous tone, contractility and heart rate to different degrees. The same MAP response can hide very different effects on flow and myocardial demand.

At the bedside, choose the agent from the dominant deficit and define what improvement and toxicity should look like before titration.

Two traps to actively avoid: Treating MAP increase as proof of improved perfusion. And: Ignoring venous effects and cardiac workload.

The integration move is this: Use vasoactive drugs to correct a physiologic mechanism, not to normalize a monitor number.

Keep these terms available in your mental model: alpha-1, beta-1, inotropy, chronotropy, venoconstriction.

What expert-level use of this concept looks like is this: Choose a vasoactive strategy from the physiologic deficit rather than blood pressure alone. State the expected benefit, predictable tradeoff and reassessment endpoint.

Tie this back to the patient. Ask what variable is changing, what downstream effect you expect, and what bedside finding would support or contradict that model.

Now: When invasive hemodynamics clarify the model.

Pulmonary artery catheter interpretation. A PA catheter is useful only when waveforms, zero/level, thermodilution assumptions and the clinical model are integrated correctly.

Build the mechanism first. A pulmonary artery catheter samples pressures through right heart and pulmonary circulation and can estimate flow and oxygen extraction. Every derived variable depends on valid waveforms and measurement assumptions.

At the bedside, level and zero first, recognize RA/RV/PA/wedge waveforms, validate wedge and interpret serial pressures with cardiac output and SvO2.

Two traps to actively avoid: Treating a wedge number without validating the waveform. And: Using one hemodynamic snapshot as a diagnosis.

The integration move is this: Use invasive hemodynamics to test and refine a model, not to collect isolated targets.

Keep these terms available in your mental model: RAP, PAP, PAOP, thermodilution, P V R, S V R.

What expert-level use of this concept looks like is this: Recognize chamber waveforms during insertion and interpret pressures in physiologic context. Use serial values to test a hemodynamic model rather than treating targets in isolation.

Tie this back to the patient. Ask what variable is changing, what downstream effect you expect, and what bedside finding would support or contradict that model.

Now: When drugs are no longer enough.

Mechanical circulatory support. Temporary circulatory support devices unload or bypass different parts of the circulation and must be matched to the failing ventricle and oxygenation problem.

Build the mechanism first. Temporary MCS devices unload or bypass different portions of the circulation. Device selection depends on LV, RV, biventricular and/or oxygenation failure.

At the bedside, understand where blood is removed, where it returns, what ventricle is unloaded and how native circulation interacts with the device.

Two traps to actively avoid: Choosing a device by shock severity without matching anatomy/physiology. And: Ignoring hemolysis, limb ischemia, ventricular distension and device-device interactions.

The integration move is this: Follow the blood and ask what pressure/flow relationship the device is changing.

Keep these terms available in your mental model: LV unloading, VA E C M O, axial flow.

What expert-level use of this concept looks like is this: Match support strategy to LV, RV, biventricular or cardiopulmonary failure. Anticipate loading, limb, hemolysis and device-interaction complications.

Tie this back to the patient. Ask what variable is changing, what downstream effect you expect, and what bedside finding would support or contradict that model.
