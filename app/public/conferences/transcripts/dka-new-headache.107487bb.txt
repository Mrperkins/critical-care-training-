A new symptom changes the case
Case conference. DKA complicated by suspected cerebral injury during treatment. This is a fictional teaching case, with clinical review pending. A 7-year-old child, measured weight 22 kilograms, is three hours into treatment for severe DKA. The original presentation included marked dehydration and acidosis. During transfer preparation the child develops headache, vomiting and irritability, then becomes drowsy. Glucose has been falling, which initially distracts the team from the neurological change. At handoff, the referring nurse says the child was conversing normally before the new headache, vomiting and irritability began. The parent now struggles to keep the child awake. We are treating a clinical diagnosis of DKA-associated cerebral injury; imaging has not confirmed its mechanism, and a normal glucose trend would not exclude it. The metabolic numbers are moving in a direction the team expected. Then the child develops a new problem. This case asks us to notice when the original treatment story is no longer the only story.

The neurological baseline has changed
The child opens eyes to voice, has a new decline in interaction and a heart rate that is falling despite continued illness. Pupils are equal. No sedative has been given. Equal pupils can be a powerful distraction when we are hoping the child is simply tired. The comparison that matters is with this child’s earlier interaction, especially without a medication explanation.

A clinical emergency before confirmation
Suspected DKA-associated cerebral injury is a clinical emergency. Equal pupils and a reasonable glucose do not exclude it. Treatment should not wait for confirmatory cranial imaging.  The important decision is about timing. A later image may help clarify the course, but a request for proof should not become the reason a deteriorating child waits for treatment.

Treat the new syndrome and reassess
The new headache, vomiting, change in behavior and relative slowing of heart rate raise concern for cerebral injury. Hypoglycemia and other causes still require immediate assessment, but do not delay syndrome-directed treatment. Call pediatric critical care and begin the protocol for suspected cerebral injury, including indicated hyperosmolar therapy. Reassess airway protection, perfusion and glucose immediately; avoid unnecessary interruptions in care for imaging. If intubation is unavoidable, use expert planning to preserve appropriate compensation and avoid an abrupt rise in carbon dioxide. Two tasks run together: act on suspected cerebral injury and check for another reversible cause of deterioration. If an airway intervention becomes unavoidable, the metabolic compensation needs explicit planning.
