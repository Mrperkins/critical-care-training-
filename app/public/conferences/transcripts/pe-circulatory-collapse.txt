Syncope followed by shock
Case conference. Syncope followed by shock. This is a fictional teaching case, with clinical review pending. A 57-year-old patient develops sudden dyspnea and collapses briefly. On recovery the patient remains confused and hypotensive. There is no reported major trauma or external bleeding.

What do you notice?
At the bedside. There are weak pulses, cool extremities and elevated neck veins. The lungs are relatively clear. The team considers obstructive, cardiac and other shock causes.

Vital signs
The initial vital signs are as follows. Heart rate is 138 per minute. Blood pressure is 70 over 42. Respiratory rate is 36 per minute. Saturation is 84 percent on supplemental oxygen. These are observations at this point in the case, rather than treatment targets.

Laboratory findings
Laboratory findings. Lactate is 7.2 millimoles per liter. Arterial pH is 7.27. Interpret these results alongside the examination and the timing of sampling.

Diagnostic findings
Diagnostic findings. Bedside echocardiography shows a dilated, poorly functioning right ventricle. This supports a shock assessment but is not specific for acute pulmonary embolism. The instability may limit safe transport to CT before initial resuscitation.

What would you prioritize?
Think it through. What is the risk of taking a profoundly unstable patient away for confirmatory imaging? Consider your answer while the case continues. No screen interaction is needed.

Clinical reasoning & priorities
Conference discussion. Shock requires immediate support and an urgent diagnostic and reperfusion strategy. Airway induction and positive pressure can further destabilize right-ventricular failure. Priorities. 1. Activate critical care and the pulmonary embolism response team for an urgent individualized plan. 2. Support circulation and oxygenation, anticipating the hemodynamic risk of airway interventions. 3. Coordinate bedside diagnostics and reperfusion decisions with explicit assessment of contraindications and alternative causes.

Reassessment & handoff
Reassessment and handoff. The receiving team selects a definitive pathway based on the evolving evidence and risk. The handoff distinguishes confirmed observations from the working diagnosis. This is an authored fictional course; improvement is not guaranteed in a real patient.
