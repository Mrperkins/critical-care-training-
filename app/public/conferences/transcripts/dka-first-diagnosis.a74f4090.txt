The vomiting has a longer story
Case conference. New-onset type 1 diabetes presenting with diabetic ketoacidosis. This is a fictional teaching case, with clinical review pending. A 10-year-old child, measured weight 30 kilograms, has no known diabetes. The family describes two weeks of thirst, frequent urination and weight loss, followed by vomiting and abdominal pain. The child is transferred after initial assessment at a community hospital. EMS was called for repeated vomiting and fast breathing. The father describes a previously well child who has been taking water to bed and waking to urinate for two weeks. The referring examination finds dehydration and a clear chest rather than a primary wheezing illness. The diagnosis is DKA; the deep breathing is part of the metabolic presentation. If the handoff begins with vomiting, ask what happened before the vomiting. The two-week history changes the meaning of the final few hours and points us toward a metabolic process.

Deep breathing is part of the compensation
The child is alert but tired, with dry mucosa and deep, regular breathing. Capillary refill is three seconds. There are no focal neurological findings, headache or signs of pulmonary edema. The breathing can draw our attention toward a primary lung problem. Listen to its regular depth and connect it with the history. We also need a neurological baseline before the treatment course evolves.

Oxygenation and ventilation answer different questions
Heart rate is 128 per minute. For a ten-year-old, the RCH acceptable heart-rate range for unwell children is 60 to 130 beats per minute. This is broader than a healthy resting range; trends and examination still determine severity. This rate is toward the upper end of the broad unwell-child range. With dry mucosa and delayed refill, volume depletion is clinically important even without crossing that upper limit. Dehydration commonly causes thirst, dry mucosa, reduced urine output and tachycardia; polyuria can precede the later fall in output. Blood pressure is 104 over 64. For a ten-year-old, the RCH acceptable systolic-pressure range for unwell children is 85 to 120 millimeters of mercury. It is an observation reference, not a shock rule-out threshold. The systolic pressure is within range. Children can preserve pressure despite substantial dehydration, so this does not cancel the refill and mucosal findings. Worsening volume loss may add weak pulses, cool skin or lethargy before overt hypotension. Respiratory rate is 32 with deep breaths. For a ten-year-old, the RCH acceptable respiratory-rate range for unwell children is 15 to 25 breaths per minute. A count in range does not establish adequate ventilation. This is tachypnea with deep compensatory breathing, often called Kussmaul respiration in DKA. Carbon dioxide elimination partially compensates for metabolic acidosis. Deep regular breaths, acetone odor, abdominal pain and vomiting often accompany this presentation; wheeze is not required. Oxygen saturation is 98 percent on room air. At sea level, oxygen saturation is usually 95 to 100 percent in a person without a special oxygen target. Verify signal quality and the oxygen support in use. Oxygenation is within the usual reference on room air. The rapid deep breathing is driven by acidosis, not demonstrated hypoxemia. Acidotic respiratory effort can be substantial without cyanosis; normal saturation does not exclude a metabolic emergency. Normal saturation tells us about oxygenation at this moment. It does not explain away the deep breathing or the metabolic illness. The respiratory pattern is doing useful compensatory work.

Glucose, ketones and acidosis
Glucose is 480 milligrams per deciliter. For a random sample after the first month, this laboratory lists glucose from 65 to 139 milligrams per deciliter. Fasting reference limits and treatment thresholds differ. This is marked hyperglycemia. Glucose-driven osmotic diuresis explains the thirst, frequent urination and dehydration. Common associated symptoms are polyuria, polydipsia, weight loss and blurred vision; the family has already supplied the first three. Venous pH is 7.12. This pediatric peripheral venous pH reference is 7.30 to 7.40. Venous and arterial samples must be identified separately. This is acidemia. Together with ketonemia and low bicarbonate it supports the metabolic acidosis of DKA. Acidosis commonly produces deep breathing, nausea, abdominal pain and fatigue; neurological deterioration requires separate urgent evaluation. Bicarbonate is 8 millimoles per liter. The serum bicarbonate teaching reference is 22 to 28 millimoles per liter. This is markedly reduced bicarbonate, consumed buffering ketoacids. It explains why the child is trying to lower carbon dioxide through deep breathing. Low bicarbonate from metabolic acidosis may accompany Kussmaul breathing and vomiting; it is not evidence of an asthma attack. Beta hydroxybutyrate is 5.8 millimoles per liter. In a fed child, significant ketonemia is not expected. In the pediatric DKA pathway, beta hydroxybutyrate of at least 3 millimoles per liter is a diagnostic ketosis threshold, not a normal limit. This is significant ketonemia above the DKA threshold. Insulin deficiency is allowing fat-derived ketone production. Ketosis may be associated with acetone odor, nausea and abdominal pain; odor alone is neither required nor diagnostic. Potassium is 4.8 millimoles per liter. The serum potassium teaching reference is 3.5 to 5.0 millimoles per liter; use the reporting laboratory and exclude sample hemolysis. The serum value is within range, but total-body potassium is commonly depleted in DKA. Insulin and correction of acidosis can lower the measured value rapidly. A subsequent potassium deficit may cause weakness or arrhythmias; a normal initial result does not eliminate that risk. Now the history and the laboratory pattern meet. Keep the potassium value in view: its initial position in the reference range is not a measure of how much potassium remains in the body.

DKA: establish the pathway
Hyperglycemia, ketonemia and metabolic acidosis support DKA. Deep breathing is compensatory. Routine cranial imaging is not indicated solely because DKA is present. Plan a monitored metabolic correction
Serum potassium may appear normal despite depletion. Fluid, insulin, electrolyte and dextrose decisions depend on repeated assessment and a pediatric DKA pathway, including neurological monitoring. Verify measured weight, fluid history, urine output, electrolyte results and timing of insulin. Use the pediatric DKA protocol for fluids and insulin; avoid an insulin bolus. Arrange serial glucose, electrolytes and neurological examinations and prepare for deterioration. The pathway links several moving parts. A change in glucose may alter dextrose needs while ketosis still requires treatment. That is why isolated numbers are a poor substitute for a shared plan.

Glucose improves; treatment continues
Two hours later glucose is 330, potassium is 3.7 and the child remains alert. The team adjusts dextrose and electrolytes under its pathway while continuing treatment of ketosis. Glucose improvement alone does not mean DKA has resolved. After the initial circulation assessment, the team uses a pediatric DKA pathway for measured isotonic fluid, followed by insulin infusion without an insulin bolus and potassium management based on repeated chemistry and urine output. Dextrose is added as glucose falls so insulin can continue treating ketogenesis. The later glucose of 330 remains hyperglycemic compared with the stated reference. Potassium of 3.7 is still in range but falling, which makes replacement and surveillance important. In this synthetic hospital course, ketones and acidosis resolve with continued monitored treatment, mental status remains normal, and diabetes education begins before discharge. Glucose alone is never used to declare DKA resolved. This is the result we were preparing to interpret. Potassium has moved even though the child looks better.
