You're listening to ICU nutrition is not just calories. Route, protein, aspiration, hemodynamics and the metabolic cost of critical illness.

This is a level 3 critical-care session. The goal is not to memorize a list. The goal is to build a model that lets you predict what the patient will do next.

By the end, you should be able to frame nutrition as organ-support and recovery therapy; and recognize refeeding risk before escalating calories.

Now: What feeding is trying to accomplish.

ICU nutrition & refeeding risk. Nutrition in critical illness is a metabolic therapy with timing, route, protein, glycemic and electrolyte consequences.

Build the mechanism first. Critical illness changes protein turnover, insulin sensitivity and energy use. Nutrition cannot fully reverse catabolism but route, timing and protein strategy influence recovery.

At the bedside, prefer enteral feeding when feasible, assess aspiration/hemodynamic risk and monitor tolerance rather than chasing an exact calorie number immediately.

Two traps to actively avoid: Equating calories with adequate nutrition. And: Overfeeding unstable patients or missing refeeding risk.

The integration move is this: Nutrition, mobility and ventilator liberation are connected recovery interventions.

Keep these terms available in your mental model: enteral nutrition, protein target, caloric debt.

What expert-level use of this concept looks like is this: Explain why early enteral nutrition is often preferred when feasible. Recognize when feeding strategy, aspiration risk or hemodynamic instability changes the plan.

Tie this back to the patient. Ask what variable is changing, what downstream effect you expect, and what bedside finding would support or contradict that model.

Now: When feeding itself becomes dangerous.

Refeeding syndrome. Insulin-driven intracellular shifts after refeeding can rapidly lower phosphate, potassium and magnesium and precipitate cardiopulmonary or neurologic deterioration.

Build the mechanism first. Reintroducing carbohydrate after prolonged undernutrition raises insulin and shifts phosphate, potassium and magnesium intracellularly while increasing thiamine demand.

At the bedside, identify high-risk patients before feeding escalation and monitor electrolytes and cardiopulmonary/neurologic response.

Two traps to actively avoid: Waiting for severe hypophosphatemia before recognizing risk. And: Increasing calories rapidly because initial feeding was tolerated.

The integration move is this: The dangerous part is the metabolic transition, not the food itself.

Keep these terms available in your mental model: phosphate, thiamine, intracellular shift.

What expert-level use of this concept looks like is this: Identify high-risk patients before feeding escalates. Connect electrolyte shifts to respiratory, cardiac and neurologic failure.

Tie this back to the patient. Ask what variable is changing, what downstream effect you expect, and what bedside finding would support or contradict that model.

Now: Protein, mobility and recovery.

ICU-acquired weakness & recovery. Critical illness, immobility, inflammation, sedation and organ failure can produce profound weakness and long-term functional loss.

Build the mechanism first. Inflammation, immobility, neuropathy, myopathy, sedation and undernutrition can cause profound ICU-acquired weakness and prolonged post-ICU disability.

At the bedside, build mobility, delirium prevention, nutrition and sedation minimization into daily care rather than starting “rehab” only after organ failure resolves.

Two traps to actively avoid: Interpreting generalized weakness only as deconditioning. And: Separating recovery planning from ventilator liberation.

The integration move is this: Survival is not the only outcome; function is a critical-care endpoint.

Keep these terms available in your mental model: critical illness myopathy, critical illness neuropathy, PICS.

What expert-level use of this concept looks like is this: Recognize weakness as an ICU outcome, not merely a discharge problem. Connect sedation, mobility, nutrition and ventilator liberation to recovery.

Tie this back to the patient. Ask what variable is changing, what downstream effect you expect, and what bedside finding would support or contradict that model.
