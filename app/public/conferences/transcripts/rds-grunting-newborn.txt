Grunting after a preterm delivery
Case conference. Grunting after a preterm delivery. This is a fictional teaching case, with clinical review pending. A newborn delivered at 30 weeks gestation weighs 1.4 kilograms. Within minutes of birth there is persistent grunting and increasing oxygen need. The infant continues to breathe spontaneously. The team is preparing neonatal transport.

What do you notice?
At the bedside. The infant has retractions and reduced air entry with equal chest movement. Temperature control and minimizing heat loss are active priorities.

Vital signs
The initial vital signs are as follows. Heart rate is 162 per minute. Respiratory rate is 74 per minute. At 25 minutes of age, saturation is 89 percent on respiratory support. Temperature is 36.1 degrees Celsius. These are observations at this point in the case, rather than treatment targets.

Laboratory findings
Laboratory findings. Arterial pH is 7.25. Arterial carbon dioxide is 55 millimeters of mercury. Glucose is 61 milligrams per deciliter. Interpret these results alongside the examination and the timing of sampling.

Diagnostic findings
Diagnostic findings. A synthetic radiology report describes low lung volumes, diffuse granular opacities and air bronchograms, consistent with surfactant-deficiency RDS in context. Clinical reasoning & priorities
Conference discussion. Spontaneous breathing permits noninvasive support in selected preterm infants, but increasing oxygen need, apnea, poor ventilation or exhaustion require escalation. Oxygen targets depend on postnatal age and the neonatal pathway. Priorities. 1. Maintain warmth and use neonatal respiratory support appropriate to the infant’s effort and gas exchange. 2. Obtain neonatal specialist guidance on surfactant and airway escalation. 3. Check glucose, interface fit, pressure delivery and supplies before departure.

Reassessment & handoff
Reassessment and handoff. After respiratory and thermal support, effort improves and temperature reaches 36.7 degrees. The team continues to assess oxygen requirement and gases rather than assuming the early response will persist. This is an authored fictional course; improvement is not guaranteed in a real patient.
