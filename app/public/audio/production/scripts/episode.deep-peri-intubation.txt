This is a level 5 critical-care session. The goal is not to memorize a list. The goal is to build a model that lets you predict what the patient will do next.

By the end, you should be able to identify physiologic risk before induction; and run the post-intubation period as active resuscitation rather than a checklist afterthought.

Now: Why the patient may not tolerate apnea or induction.

R S I preparation & peri-intubation physiology. R S I is a physiologic resuscitation that includes preoxygenation, hemodynamic preparation, drug strategy, positioning, backup planning and post-intubation care.

Build the mechanism first. R S I changes oxygenation, ventilation, sympathetic tone and intrathoracic pressure abruptly. Physiologically difficult airways fail because the circulation or gas exchange cannot tolerate that transition.

At the bedside, prepare oxygenation, hemodynamics, suction, primary/backup devices, rescue ventilation and post-intubation support before induction.

Two traps to actively avoid: Treating R S I as laryngoscopy plus drugs. And: Ignoring severe RV failure, metabolic acidosis or shock until after apnea begins.

The integration move is this: The airway procedure includes the minutes before and after tube passage.

Keep these terms available in your mental model: preoxygenation, apneic oxygenation, first-pass success, peri-intubation arrest.

What expert-level use of this concept looks like is this: Identify physiologically difficult airways before induction. Build an airway plan that includes hemodynamics and the first five minutes after tube placement.

Right ventricular failure. Failure of a thin-walled ventricle that is highly sensitive to acute afterload, volume, perfusion pressure and ventricular interaction.

Build the mechanism first. The RV is thin-walled and highly sensitive to acute afterload. When it dilates, wall stress rises, coronary perfusion can fall and septal shift can reduce LV filling.

At the bedside, track systemic pressure, venous congestion, echo geometry, oxygenation, CO2, acid-base status and ventilator pressures together. Small changes in P V R or intrathoracic pressure can have large hemodynamic consequences.

Two traps to actively avoid: Reflexively giving fluid to every hypotensive RV patient. And: Focusing on systemic pressure while ignoring worsening RV dilation and congestion.

The integration move is this: Every intervention should be judged by its effects on RV preload, RV afterload, contractility and coronary perfusion.

Keep these terms available in your mental model: RV-PA coupling, septal shift, transmural pressure.

What expert-level use of this concept looks like is this: Recognize RV shock physiology before systemic hypotension becomes profound. Predict how peep, hypercapnia, hypoxemia, fluids and vasoactives may change RV performance.

Tie this back to the patient. Ask what variable is changing, what downstream effect you expect, and what bedside finding would support or contradict that model.

Now: The circulation changes when positive pressure starts.

Positive-pressure ventilation & circulation. Positive intrathoracic pressure changes venous return, ventricular transmural pressures and pulmonary vascular resistance.

Build the mechanism first. Positive intrathoracic pressure can reduce the gradient for venous return, change transmural ventricular pressures and alter P V R depending on lung volume and recruitability.

At the bedside, post-intubation hypotension may reflect vasodilation, reduced venous return, increased RV afterload, auto-peep or previously compensated shock becoming unmasked.

Two traps to actively avoid: Attributing every post-intubation pressure drop to induction medication. And: Assuming positive pressure has a single predictable effect on both ventricles.

The integration move is this: Think preload, RV afterload and LV transmural afterload separately.

Keep these terms available in your mental model: transmural pressure, mean airway pressure.

What expert-level use of this concept looks like is this: Predict why induction and positive pressure can precipitate hypotension. Distinguish effects on RV preload, RV afterload and LV transmural afterload.

Tie this back to the patient. Ask what variable is changing, what downstream effect you expect, and what bedside finding would support or contradict that model.

Now: Confirmation is only the midpoint.

Post-intubation stabilization. Tube confirmation is the beginning of post-intubation care: oxygenation, ventilation, hemodynamics, sedation, ventilator setup and diagnosis must be reassessed together.

Build the mechanism first. Intubation creates a new physiologic state: airway resistance, mean intrathoracic pressure, sedation, ventilation and venous return all change at once.

At the bedside, confirm tube with waveform capnography, set disease-appropriate ventilation, establish analgesia/sedation and reassess hypotension or hypoxemia mechanistically.

Two traps to actively avoid: Declaring success at tube confirmation. And: Forgetting analgesia/sedation in a paralyzed patient.

The integration move is this: The first five minutes determine whether airway control becomes physiologic rescue or a new source of instability.

Keep these terms available in your mental model: waveform capnography, post-intubation hypotension, analgosedation.

What expert-level use of this concept looks like is this: Run a reproducible first-five-minutes stabilization sequence. Treat hypotension or hypoxemia as a physiology problem rather than simply changing one ventilator or medication setting.

Now: Initial ventilation from disease physiology.

Ventilator emergency troubleshooting. A structured patient-to-device approach to acute hypoxemia, high pressure, low pressure, dyssynchrony and post-intubation deterioration.

Build the mechanism first. Acute ventilator deterioration can originate from the patient, tube, circuit or machine. Peak and plateau pressures help separate resistance from compliance when measurements are trustworthy.

At the bedside, look at the patient first, rescue oxygenation/ventilation if the device cannot be trusted, then trace from patient to tube to circuit to machine.

Two traps to actively avoid: Staring at the ventilator while the patient deteriorates. And: Treating every high-pressure alarm as bronchospasm.

The integration move is this: Use pressure pattern, capnography, exam and circuit inspection to localize the problem quickly.

Keep these terms available in your mental model: DOPES, peak pressure, plateau pressure, auto-peep.

What expert-level use of this concept looks like is this: Separate resistance, compliance, circuit and patient problems using waveforms and bedside examination. Prioritize oxygenation and manual ventilation when the device or circuit cannot be trusted.

Tie this back to the patient. Ask what variable is changing, what downstream effect you expect, and what bedside finding would support or contradict that model.

Now: Analgesia, sedation and paralysis awareness.

ICU analgesia, sedation & synchrony. Sedation strategy balances analgesia, comfort, ventilator synchrony, neurologic assessment, hemodynamics and delirium risk.

Build the mechanism first. Analgesics and sedatives alter pain, awareness, respiratory drive, vascular tone and neurologic assessment. Deep sedation is a physiologic intervention with downstream consequences.

At the bedside, separate pain, anxiety, delirium, dyssynchrony and hypoxemia before escalating sedation; use an explicit target and reassess it.

Two traps to actively avoid: Using paralysis as evidence of comfort. And: Treating agitation as a medication deficiency without finding the cause.

The integration move is this: Sedation strategy should serve a clinical goal, not simply silence movement.

Keep these terms available in your mental model: analgosedation, RASS, daily awakening.

What expert-level use of this concept looks like is this: Separate pain, anxiety, delirium and dyssynchrony before simply deepening sedation. Predict major hemodynamic and respiratory tradeoffs of sedative strategies.

Tie this back to the patient. Ask what variable is changing, what downstream effect you expect, and what bedside finding would support or contradict that model.
