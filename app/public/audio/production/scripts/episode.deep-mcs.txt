Now: Counterpulsation.

Intra-aortic balloon pump physiology. I A B P timing uses diastolic augmentation and presystolic deflation to alter coronary perfusion and LV afterload.

Build the mechanism first. I A B P inflates in diastole to augment aortic diastolic pressure and deflates before systole to reduce LV afterload. Benefit depends on timing and native rhythm/hemodynamics.

At the bedside, use the arterial waveform to judge inflation near the dicrotic notch and presystolic deflation; compare assisted and unassisted beats.

Two traps to actively avoid: Treating augmentation number alone as success. And: Allowing late deflation to increase LV afterload.

The integration move is this: The waveform should show you the mechanism the device is trying to create.

Keep these terms available in your mental model: diastolic augmentation, deflation, trigger.

What expert-level use of this concept looks like is this: Identify correct inflation/deflation timing from the arterial waveform. Predict the hemodynamic consequence of early or late timing errors.

Tie this back to the patient. Ask what variable is changing, what downstream effect you expect, and what bedside finding would support or contradict that model.

Now: VA E C M O flow and LV loading.

E C M O circuit & physiology literacy. Extracorporeal support changes preload, afterload, gas exchange and oxygen delivery through a circuit whose configuration determines what it can support.

Build the mechanism first. E C M O drains venous blood, pumps it through a membrane lung and returns it to the circulation. VV supports gas exchange; VA additionally supports systemic flow and changes cardiac loading conditions.

At the bedside, follow blood through drainage, pump, oxygenator and return while considering native heart/lung function and cannula position.

Two traps to actively avoid: Equating pump RPM with effective support. And: Ignoring recirculation, LV distension or differential hypoxemia.

The integration move is this: The circuit and the native circulation are one coupled system.

Keep these terms available in your mental model: VV E C M O, VA E C M O, sweep gas, recirculation, differential hypoxemia.

What expert-level use of this concept looks like is this: Distinguish VV from VA support goals and limitations. Reason from circuit flow, native heart/lung function and cannula position to common failure patterns.

Tie this back to the patient. Ask what variable is changing, what downstream effect you expect, and what bedside finding would support or contradict that model.

Now: Right-sided failure changes the answer.

Right ventricular failure. Failure of a thin-walled ventricle that is highly sensitive to acute afterload, volume, perfusion pressure and ventricular interaction.

Build the mechanism first. The RV is thin-walled and highly sensitive to acute afterload. When it dilates, wall stress rises, coronary perfusion can fall and septal shift can reduce LV filling.

At the bedside, track systemic pressure, venous congestion, echo geometry, oxygenation, CO2, acid-base status and ventilator pressures together. Small changes in P V R or intrathoracic pressure can have large hemodynamic consequences.

Two traps to actively avoid: Reflexively giving fluid to every hypotensive RV patient. And: Focusing on systemic pressure while ignoring worsening RV dilation and congestion.

The integration move is this: Every intervention should be judged by its effects on RV preload, RV afterload, contractility and coronary perfusion.

Keep these terms available in your mental model: RV-PA coupling, septal shift, transmural pressure.

What expert-level use of this concept looks like is this: Recognize RV shock physiology before systemic hypotension becomes profound. Predict how peep, hypercapnia, hypoxemia, fluids and vasoactives may change RV performance.

Tie this back to the patient. Ask what variable is changing, what downstream effect you expect, and what bedside finding would support or contradict that model.
