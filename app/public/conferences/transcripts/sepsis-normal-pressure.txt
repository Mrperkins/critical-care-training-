The blood pressure is still normal
Case conference. The blood pressure is still normal. This is a fictional teaching case, with clinical review pending. A 4-year-old child, measured weight 17 kilograms, has fever and poor intake. The caregiver reports rapidly reduced interaction and no urine for eight hours. A clinic recorded a normal blood pressure and sent the child for further assessment. There is no known heart disease.

What do you notice?
At the bedside. The child is lethargic but rousable, with cool mottled extremities, weak peripheral pulses and capillary refill of five seconds. Breathing is rapid without focal wheeze.

Vital signs
The initial vital signs are as follows. Heart rate is 166 per minute. Blood pressure is 96 over 58. Respiratory rate is 40 per minute. Temperature is 39.4 degrees Celsius. Oxygen saturation is 95 percent on room air. These are observations at this point in the case, rather than treatment targets.

Laboratory findings
Laboratory findings. Venous lactate is 4.7 millimoles per liter. Glucose is 72 milligrams per deciliter. Venous pH is 7.28. Interpret these results alongside the examination and the timing of sampling.

Diagnostic findings
Diagnostic findings. Suspected infection with poor perfusion raises concern for septic shock despite preserved blood pressure. Cultures and source evaluation should be coordinated without delaying time-critical treatment.

What would you prioritize?
Think it through. Would you wait for hypotension before recognizing shock? Consider your answer while the case continues. No screen interaction is needed.

Clinical reasoning & priorities
Conference discussion. Children can maintain blood pressure through compensatory vasoconstriction. Perfusion, interaction and urine output reveal deterioration earlier. Lactate supports the assessment but cannot establish or exclude shock on its own. Priorities. 1. Activate pediatric sepsis care and give timely antimicrobials under the local pathway. 2. Use measured, reassessed fluid therapy appropriate to the setting and physiology, watching for overload. 3. Prepare vasoactive support when indicated and escalate early to pediatric critical care.

Reassessment & handoff
Reassessment and handoff. After an initial measured fluid intervention, pulses and capillary refill improve only partly. The team reassesses lungs and cardiac function before further fluid, and prepares vasoactive support rather than repeating boluses automatically. This is an authored fictional course; improvement is not guaranteed in a real patient.
