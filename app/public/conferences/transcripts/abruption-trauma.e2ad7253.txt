Maternal pressure and fetal findings differ
Maternal heart rate is 112 per minute. A resting adult heart rate is commonly 60 to 100 beats per minute. Pregnancy, pain and medications can shift the baseline. This is mild tachycardia. Pain, anxiety and pregnancy may contribute, but bleeding and evolving maternal injury must still be assessed. Tachycardia may accompany pain, anxiety or reduced circulating volume; weak pulses, cool skin and oliguria would add evidence of poor perfusion. Maternal blood pressure is 116 over 72. An adult pressure is commonly around 90 over 60 to below 120 over 80 at rest. A systolic pressure below 90 is concerning in an acutely ill patient; perfusion and baseline matter. This pressure is in the usual resting adult interval. It does not exclude placental injury or early concealed bleeding. Hemorrhagic shock commonly causes pallor, diaphoresis, weak pulses, confusion and reduced urine output; external blood loss may underestimate total loss. Fetal heart rate is 170 with recurrent decelerations on the referring monitor. The usual fetal baseline heart rate is 110 to 160 beats per minute. Baseline duration, variability and decelerations must also be reviewed. A baseline near 170 is fetal tachycardia if sustained, and recurrent decelerations independently add concern. A single spot rate cannot characterize the full tracing. Placental compromise may cause recurrent decelerations, altered variability and reduced movement; the recurrent decelerations are documented here. The maternal pressure is maintained, while the fetal monitor reports recurrent decelerations with tachycardia. This difference is a reason to integrate the assessments rather than choose one as the whole story.

A baseline panel needs a clinical trend
Hemoglobin is 11.2 grams per deciliter. The nonpregnant adult female hemoglobin teaching reference is 12 to 16 grams per deciliter. Gestation and fluid shifts change the baseline, and an early sample can underestimate acute blood loss. This is slightly below the nonpregnant reference and can occur with pregnancy dilution. It is not a reliable early exclusion test for acute blood loss. Major hemorrhage may first show tachycardia, weakness or reduced perfusion before a large measured hemoglobin fall. Fibrinogen is 250 milligrams per deciliter and will be interpreted with its trend and clinical context. Fibrinogen normally rises in late pregnancy. During postpartum hemorrhage, less than 200 milligrams per deciliter is a replacement threshold in the WHO guideline; 200 is a treatment threshold, not a normal pregnancy value. This exceeds the postpartum 200 replacement threshold but may still be concerning relative to expected late-pregnancy elevation, especially if falling. It neither confirms nor excludes abruption. A developing coagulopathy may later cause persistent bleeding or oozing; trend the value with the uterine and fetal findings. These initial values provide a starting point. They do not overrule the pain, contractions and fetal findings. Repeated assessment is how we learn whether the situation is evolving.
