The deterioration begins during movement
Case conference. Displaced endotracheal tube causing acute ventilation failure in a preterm infant with RDS. This is a fictional teaching case, with clinical review pending. A 29-week newborn, weight 1.2 kilograms, is intubated for respiratory distress. During movement into the transport incubator, saturation abruptly falls and the ventilator alarms. The infant had been stable on the same settings immediately before movement. During movement from the warmer to the transport incubator, an infant intubated for surfactant-deficiency RDS suddenly loses chest excursion and the carbon dioxide waveform. The crew reports the tube marking has changed at the lip. This is an acute equipment and airway event superimposed on a known lung disease, not simply worsening RDS. The timing is the first clue. A sudden event during movement asks a different question from a gradual increase in oxygen requirement over several hours.

Is ventilation reaching the infant?
Chest movement is now reduced, the capnography trace disappears and the heart rate falls. The team checks the infant, tube and circuit together. Start at the patient and work through the support system. A ventilator can display familiar settings while little useful ventilation is reaching the lungs.

Bradycardia with loss of effective ventilation
Heart rate has fallen to 88 per minute. The RCH term-newborn observation reference is 120 to 170 beats per minute. In a preterm infant, gestation, sleep and respiratory support also affect the expected rate. This is neonatal bradycardia during sudden ventilation loss. The immediate intervention is effective ventilation with reassessment under neonatal resuscitation guidance. Hypoxic bradycardia can accompany poor tone, cyanosis and absent chest movement; the event timing links these findings. Oxygen saturation is 68 percent. After initial stabilization, NICE recommends a preterm oxygen saturation target of 91 to 95 percent. That is a treatment target, not a delivery-room first-minute reference. This is severe desaturation, far below the post-stabilization preterm target. The abrupt change coincides with ineffective ventilation. Cyanosis, limpness and bradycardia may accompany ventilation failure; a monitor lag must not delay checking chest movement. No exhaled carbon dioxide waveform is currently detected. With effective ventilation through a correctly positioned tracheal tube and adequate pulmonary blood flow, a sustained exhaled carbon dioxide waveform is expected. A lost waveform is an emergency clue to displacement, obstruction, disconnection or very low pulmonary blood flow. Here the tube marking and absent chest movement support displacement. No chest rise, absent breath sounds or sudden bradycardia may accompany tube displacement; waveform loss alone does not distinguish every cause. The oxygen saturation and heart rate are falling together. The absent carbon dioxide waveform is important, with the limitations of small tidal volumes and poor circulation kept in mind.

A new sample is not the next useful action
Glucose before the event was 79 milligrams per deciliter. Glucose targets depend on postnatal age and risk. The Pediatric Endocrine Society suggests maintaining above 50 milligrams per deciliter during the first forty-eight hours in high-risk infants without a suspected congenital disorder; neonatal pathways can use different operational thresholds. The earlier value exceeded the early maintenance target. It does not establish the current glucose or explain the abrupt airway-linked event. Hypoglycemia can cause apnea or reduced tone, but the lost waveform and changed tube marking require immediate airway correction. No new blood gas is available; immediate ventilation takes priority over sampling. No repeat value has yet been measured. A normal blood gas must never be assumed from a blank result. Sampling follows restoration of effective ventilation; it must not become a reason to delay rescue. The observed bradycardia, desaturation and absent chest movement are sufficient to act before the laboratory result. We already know enough to act on the ventilation problem. The earlier glucose does not explain away a change that began during movement, and sampling must not interrupt resuscitation.

A structured patient-to-device assessment
Abrupt deterioration during movement raises concern for displacement, obstruction, equipment failure or pneumothorax. Absence of a capnography signal requires assessment in context, including circulation and small tidal volumes. Restore ventilation and confirm the response
A sudden change needs a structured patient-to-device assessment. Previously appropriate settings cannot compensate for a displaced tube or disconnected circuit. Establish effective ventilation immediately using the neonatal rescue approach. Check tube position and patency, circuit connections and oxygen delivery; assess for pneumothorax if indicated. Escalate airway management with neonatal expertise and reconfirm ventilation after every movement. The confirmation has to be physiological. Equipment checks matter, and the infant’s chest movement, heart rate and carbon dioxide trace tell us whether the repair is effective.

A displaced tube is identified
The team identifies a displaced tube and restores effective ventilation with the neonatal airway plan. Heart rate rises and a consistent carbon dioxide waveform returns. Tube position and securement are rechecked before transport resumes. The team calls for help, checks the circuit and tube, and finds that the endotracheal tube has displaced. Ineffective tube ventilation is stopped and effective mask ventilation is established immediately while an experienced neonatal airway clinician restores the airway. Chest movement, bilateral air entry and sustained exhaled carbon dioxide confirm ventilation. The heart rate returns to its prior baseline and saturation returns to the neonatal target, with a repeat gas obtained after stabilization. The tube is secured and the marking is rechecked during every subsequent move. This synthetic event resolves with restored ventilation rather than a blind increase in oxygen or ventilator pressure. The response helps connect the identified problem with the deterioration. Before transport resumes, the team needs a new confirmation of ventilation and securement after the rescue.
