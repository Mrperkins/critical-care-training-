Headache and flashing lights
Case conference. Preeclampsia with severe features at 33 weeks. This is a fictional teaching case, with clinical review pending. A 28-year-old patient is pregnant at 33 weeks and 2 days, with a singleton pregnancy. Persistent headache, flashing lights and right upper quadrant discomfort develop over several hours. There is no seizure, vaginal bleeding or known chronic hypertension. EMS brings the patient from home after a persistent headache is joined by flashing lights. The partner says these symptoms are new, and prenatal records show no chronic hypertension. The repeat pressure and organ-injury pattern establish the conference diagnosis early: preeclampsia with severe features, before any seizure occurs. The symptom story already asks us to consider more than an isolated blood pressure problem. Keep the headache, vision change and abdominal discomfort connected as the observations arrive.

Repeat the pressure; keep examining
The patient is alert with brisk reflexes and mild dependent edema. The lungs are clear. Severe blood pressure readings persist when repeated with an appropriate cuff. Confirming a reading matters, but confirmation should lead to action. The repeat severe pressure and the neurological symptoms belong together in the escalation call.

Two patients, one immediate stabilization plan
Maternal heart rate is 104 per minute. A resting adult heart rate is commonly 60 to 100 beats per minute. Pregnancy, pain and medications can shift the baseline. This is mild tachycardia against the nonpregnant resting reference. Pregnancy and distress may contribute; it is not the criterion establishing severe preeclampsia. Tachycardia may accompany pain, anxiety or reduced circulating volume; weak pulses, cool skin and oliguria would add evidence of poor perfusion. Blood pressure is 174 over 114 and remains severe on repeat assessment. In pregnancy, a pressure below 140 over 90 is below the hypertension threshold. Severe hypertension is a systolic pressure of at least 160 or a diastolic pressure of at least 110; either component qualifies. Both components are in the severe range and remain severe on repeat. This requires urgent treatment rather than waiting for proteinuria or a seizure. Severe preeclampsia may cause headache, visual symptoms, hyperreflexia, epigastric pain or pulmonary edema; headache and visual disturbance are present. Maternal saturation is 97 percent on room air. At sea level, oxygen saturation is usually 95 to 100 percent in a person without a special oxygen target. Verify signal quality and the oxygen support in use. This is within the usual reference, and the lungs are currently clear. There is no documented hypoxemia at this assessment. Hypoxemia can cause restlessness, tachycardia or cyanosis; oxygen saturation alone does not measure perfusion or cardiac output. Fetal heart rate is 150 per minute at this assessment. The usual fetal baseline heart rate is 110 to 160 beats per minute. Baseline duration, variability and decelerations must also be reviewed. This spot rate is in the usual baseline interval. It cannot establish a normal full tracing or override the maternal emergency. Fetal compromise can appear through recurrent decelerations or abnormal variability even when a single rate is in range. The fetal rate is one observation at one moment. It cannot clear the mother’s severe hypertension or symptoms. Stabilizing maternal physiology is central to the care of both patients.

Look for organ involvement
Platelet count is 118 billion per liter. An adult platelet count is commonly about 150 to 400 times ten to the ninth per liter. In preeclampsia, a count below 100 is a severe-feature threshold. This is thrombocytopenia, but above the below-100 severe-feature threshold. Its trend matters, and other severe features are already present. Thrombocytopenia may cause petechiae, bruising or mucosal bleeding, but significant depletion can exist without visible bleeding. A S T is 85 units per liter. The adult AST teaching reference is 12 to 38 units per liter; local assay limits vary. More than twice the upper limit is relevant to preeclampsia assessment. This is more than twice the stated upper reference limit, supporting hepatic involvement in the setting of severe hypertension. Liver injury in this setting may accompany right upper quadrant pain, nausea or malaise; severe escalating pain warrants evaluation for hepatic complications. Creatinine is 1.2 milligrams per deciliter. A general adult creatinine reference is 0.6 to 1.2 milligrams per deciliter. Pregnancy usually lowers creatinine; a value above 1.1 or doubling from baseline is a preeclampsia severe-feature criterion without another renal cause. This exceeds the obstetric 1.1 criterion even though it sits at the upper end of a general adult interval. Gestational physiology changes its meaning. Renal involvement may produce oliguria or fluid accumulation; monitor urine output and magnesium clearance rather than assuming symptoms must be present. The laboratories add to a syndrome we already suspected from the bedside. Avoid waiting for one particular urine result before recognizing the severe clinical features already present.

The findings support preeclampsia with severe features. Proteinuria testing contributes to evaluation but is not required to recognize severe disease when other qualifying features are present. Control pressure and prevent seizures
Seizure prevention and treatment of severe blood pressure serve different purposes. Maternal stabilization protects both patients, and the fetus requires ongoing assessment rather than a single reassuring number. Begin urgent obstetric escalation and treat persistent severe hypertension under the obstetric protocol. Give indicated magnesium for seizure prophylaxis and monitor breathing, reflexes, renal function and infusion delivery. Coordinate maternal and fetal monitoring and the specialist plan for delivery after stabilization. A good handoff names the goal of each intervention. It also carries the exact administration times and the monitoring findings, so a new team can continue treatment without guessing what has already happened.

Improved pressure is a response, not discharge criteria
Blood pressure improves under treatment and headache lessens, but the patient still requires specialist obstetric management. The handoff includes repeat pressures, symptoms, laboratory trends and exact medication times. The obstetric team treats persistent severe hypertension with a pathway-selected antihypertensive and gives magnesium for seizure prophylaxis as a separate intervention. Respiratory rate, reflexes, urine output and infusion delivery are monitored. After maternal stabilization, the obstetric team recommends delivery because of the severe maternal disease; gestational age, symptoms and fetal assessment inform its timing and route. In this synthetic course, mother and infant receive inpatient care and the mother leaves hospital with pressure follow-up and postpartum warning signs. Better headache alone does not mean the disease has resolved. The early improvement is useful, but it does not erase the underlying pregnancy emergency. We still need a coordinated destination, monitoring plan and specialist decision about delivery.
