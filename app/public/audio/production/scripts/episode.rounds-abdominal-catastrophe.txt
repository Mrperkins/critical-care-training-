Now: When physiology exceeds the abdominal exam.

Mesenteric ischemia & abdominal catastrophe. Mesenteric ischemia and perforation can produce severe pain, lactate elevation and shock before dramatic examination or imaging changes.

Build the mechanism first. Mesenteric hypoperfusion or arterial/venous occlusion causes intestinal ischemia, barrier failure and necrosis; examination can lag behind tissue injury.

At the bedside, maintain suspicion when pain, acidosis/lactate and shock exceed abdominal findings and accelerate definitive imaging/surgical evaluation.

Two traps to actively avoid: Waiting for peritoneal signs before considering ischemia. And: Attributing rising lactate entirely to global shock.

The integration move is this: Disproportion between physiology and examination is itself a diagnostic clue.

Now: Intra-abdominal pressure becomes multisystem failure.

Intra-abdominal hypertension & compartment syndrome. Elevated intra-abdominal pressure impairs venous return, renal perfusion, lung mechanics and organ blood flow.

Build the mechanism first. Elevated intra-abdominal pressure compresses venous return, renal perfusion and diaphragm excursion while increasing thoracic pressures and reducing organ blood flow.

At the bedside, consider bladder pressure/abdominal tension when ventilation, urine output and hemodynamics worsen together after resuscitation or edema.

Two traps to actively avoid: Treating oliguria with more fluid when pressure is the cause. And: Escalating ventilator pressure without recognizing abdominal mechanics.

The integration move is this: One pressure compartment can simultaneously create renal, respiratory and circulatory failure.

Keep these terms available in your mental model: intra-abdominal pressure, abdominal perfusion pressure.

What expert-level use of this concept looks like is this: Recognize abdominal pressure as a multisystem cause of deterioration. Connect decompression or fluid strategy to renal, respiratory and hemodynamic effects.

Now: Kidney, lung and circulation move together.

Acute kidney injury. AKI is a syndrome of impaired filtration and homeostasis whose cause may be hemodynamic, intrinsic, obstructive or mixed.

Build the mechanism first. AKI represents impaired filtration and homeostasis from hemodynamic, tubular, interstitial, vascular, obstructive or mixed mechanisms; creatinine is delayed and does not identify cause.

At the bedside, use trajectory, urine output, hemodynamics, nephrotoxins, urinalysis and obstruction assessment to build the phenotype, then support volume/electrolyte/acid-base physiology.

Two traps to actively avoid: Calling all oliguria “pre-renal.” And: Starting or withholding renal support based on creatinine alone.

The integration move is this: Kidney injury is a syndrome; the treatment target is the mechanism and the homeostatic consequence.

Keep these terms available in your mental model: filtration, renal perfusion, tubular injury, obstruction.

What expert-level use of this concept looks like is this: Build an AKI differential from perfusion, nephrotoxins, urinalysis and obstruction. Recognize when kidney support is needed for physiology rather than a creatinine threshold.

Now connect Acute kidney injury to C R R T physiology & prescription literacy. Continuous renal replacement uses diffusion and/or convection to manage solute, acid-base and fluid balance in unstable patients.

The relationship matters because C R R T uses diffusion, convection and ultrafiltration to control solute, acid-base and volume continuously. Prescribed dose can differ substantially from delivered dose. At the bedside, separate total effluent from net patient fluid removal and interpret access, filter and return pressures as clues to the physical circuit.

Now connect Acute kidney injury to Antimicrobial PK/PD in critical illness. Critical illness changes volume of distribution, clearance and target attainment, so dose strategy must match drug and patient physiology.

The relationship matters because Critical illness alters volume of distribution, protein binding and clearance. Drug efficacy depends on whether exposure matches the relevant PK/PD target. At the bedside, account for augmented renal clearance, AKI, C R R T, fluid shifts and obesity rather than assuming standard dosing produces standard exposure.

Ventilator emergency troubleshooting. A structured patient-to-device approach to acute hypoxemia, high pressure, low pressure, dyssynchrony and post-intubation deterioration.

Build the mechanism first. Acute ventilator deterioration can originate from the patient, tube, circuit or machine. Peak and plateau pressures help separate resistance from compliance when measurements are trustworthy.

At the bedside, look at the patient first, rescue oxygenation/ventilation if the device cannot be trusted, then trace from patient to tube to circuit to machine.

Two traps to actively avoid: Staring at the ventilator while the patient deteriorates. And: Treating every high-pressure alarm as bronchospasm.

The integration move is this: Use pressure pattern, capnography, exam and circuit inspection to localize the problem quickly.

Now connect Ventilator emergency troubleshooting to Dead space. Ventilation that does not effectively participate in CO2 exchange, from conducting airways or ventilated but poorly perfused alveoli.

The relationship matters because Dead-space ventilation reaches regions that do not effectively eliminate CO2 because perfusion is absent or inadequate, or because gas remains in conducting airways. At the bedside, rising minute-ventilation requirement, widening PaCO2–EtCO2 gap or unexpectedly low EtCO2 can signal increasing dead space when ventilation is otherwise unchanged.

Now connect Ventilator emergency troubleshooting to peep. Positive end-expiratory pressure used to preserve recruitment and oxygenation, with important mechanical and hemodynamic effects.

The relationship matters because peep can prevent end-expiratory collapse and recruit unstable lung units, but can also overdistend already-open units and transmit pressure to the thorax. At the bedside, judge a peep change by oxygenation, compliance, driving pressure, hemodynamics and the disease’s recruitability rather than by oxygen saturation alone.
