Now: Small radius, large resistance consequences.

Pediatric airway & ventilation. Smaller airways, higher metabolic demand and lower reserve make pediatric respiratory deterioration faster and equipment sizing more consequential.

Build the mechanism first. Small airway radius magnifies resistance, metabolic demand is higher and functional residual capacity is smaller, so pediatric respiratory reserve is limited.

At the bedside, anticipate rapid desaturation, apparatus dead-space effects and resistance from small tubes or secretions; size equipment thoughtfully.

Two traps to actively avoid: Using adult ventilator assumptions on small patients. And: Ignoring dead space added by connectors/sensors.

The integration move is this: Tiny geometric changes can create large physiologic consequences.

Keep these terms available in your mental model: airway resistance, dead space, uncuffed/cuffed.

What expert-level use of this concept looks like is this: Anticipate rapid desaturation and pressure consequences in small airways. Use age/size-appropriate airway and ventilation strategy.

Tie this back to the patient. Ask what variable is changing, what downstream effect you expect, and what bedside finding would support or contradict that model.

Now: Dead space and equipment scale.

Dead space. Ventilation that does not effectively participate in CO2 exchange, from conducting airways or ventilated but poorly perfused alveoli.

Build the mechanism first. Dead-space ventilation reaches regions that do not effectively eliminate CO2 because perfusion is absent or inadequate, or because gas remains in conducting airways.

At the bedside, rising minute-ventilation requirement, widening PaCO2–EtCO2 gap or unexpectedly low EtCO2 can signal increasing dead space when ventilation is otherwise unchanged.

Two traps to actively avoid: Equating all hypercapnia with low minute ventilation. And: Using EtCO2 as a fixed conversion to PaCO2 in shock.

The integration move is this: CO2 is a ventilation-perfusion signal as well as a ventilation signal.

Keep these terms available in your mental model: VD/VT, alveolar dead space.

What expert-level use of this concept looks like is this: Use PaCO2–EtCO2 behavior and minute ventilation requirements to reason about changing dead space.

Tie this back to the patient. Ask what variable is changing, what downstream effect you expect, and what bedside finding would support or contradict that model.

Now: Troubleshooting after intubation.

Ventilator emergency troubleshooting. A structured patient-to-device approach to acute hypoxemia, high pressure, low pressure, dyssynchrony and post-intubation deterioration.

Build the mechanism first. Acute ventilator deterioration can originate from the patient, tube, circuit or machine. Peak and plateau pressures help separate resistance from compliance when measurements are trustworthy.

At the bedside, look at the patient first, rescue oxygenation/ventilation if the device cannot be trusted, then trace from patient to tube to circuit to machine.

Two traps to actively avoid: Staring at the ventilator while the patient deteriorates. And: Treating every high-pressure alarm as bronchospasm.

The integration move is this: Use pressure pattern, capnography, exam and circuit inspection to localize the problem quickly.

Keep these terms available in your mental model: DOPES, peak pressure, plateau pressure, auto-peep.

What expert-level use of this concept looks like is this: Separate resistance, compliance, circuit and patient problems using waveforms and bedside examination. Prioritize oxygenation and manual ventilation when the device or circuit cannot be trusted.
