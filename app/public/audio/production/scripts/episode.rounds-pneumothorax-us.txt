This is a level 3 critical-care session. The goal is not to memorize a list. The goal is to build a model that lets you predict what the patient will do next.

By the end, you should be able to use multiple pleural signs rather than absent sliding alone; and integrate ultrasound findings with ventilator and hemodynamic physiology.

Now: Find the pleural line first.

Pneumothorax ultrasound. Pleural ultrasound combines sliding, B-lines, M-mode patterns and lung point rather than relying on a single sign.

Build the mechanism first. Pleural ultrasound infers apposition or separation of pleural layers through sliding, B-lines, lung pulse, M-mode patterns and lung point.

At the bedside, absent sliding raises suspicion but is nonspecific; lung point is more specific when present in the appropriate context.

Two traps to actively avoid: Diagnosing pneumothorax from absent sliding alone. And: Forgetting apnea, pleurodesis, mainstem intubation or severe lung disease as alternatives.

The integration move is this: Use multiple signs and the patient’s physiology rather than one binary image feature.

Keep these terms available in your mental model: lung sliding, lung point, seashore, barcode.

What expert-level use of this concept looks like is this: Use multiple pleural signs to support or weaken the diagnosis. Recognize situations where absent sliding is not pneumothorax.

Now: The ultrasound answers a bedside question.

Ventilator emergency troubleshooting. A structured patient-to-device approach to acute hypoxemia, high pressure, low pressure, dyssynchrony and post-intubation deterioration.

Build the mechanism first. Acute ventilator deterioration can originate from the patient, tube, circuit or machine. Peak and plateau pressures help separate resistance from compliance when measurements are trustworthy.

At the bedside, look at the patient first, rescue oxygenation/ventilation if the device cannot be trusted, then trace from patient to tube to circuit to machine.

Two traps to actively avoid: Staring at the ventilator while the patient deteriorates. And: Treating every high-pressure alarm as bronchospasm.

The integration move is this: Use pressure pattern, capnography, exam and circuit inspection to localize the problem quickly.

Keep these terms available in your mental model: DOPES, peak pressure, plateau pressure, auto-peep.

What expert-level use of this concept looks like is this: Separate resistance, compliance, circuit and patient problems using waveforms and bedside examination. Prioritize oxygenation and manual ventilation when the device or circuit cannot be trusted.

Now connect Ventilator emergency troubleshooting to Dead space. Ventilation that does not effectively participate in CO2 exchange, from conducting airways or ventilated but poorly perfused alveoli.

The relationship matters because Dead-space ventilation reaches regions that do not effectively eliminate CO2 because perfusion is absent or inadequate, or because gas remains in conducting airways. At the bedside, rising minute-ventilation requirement, widening PaCO2–EtCO2 gap or unexpectedly low EtCO2 can signal increasing dead space when ventilation is otherwise unchanged.

Now connect Ventilator emergency troubleshooting to peep. Positive end-expiratory pressure used to preserve recruitment and oxygenation, with important mechanical and hemodynamic effects.

The relationship matters because peep can prevent end-expiratory collapse and recruit unstable lung units, but can also overdistend already-open units and transmit pressure to the thorax. At the bedside, judge a peep change by oxygenation, compliance, driving pressure, hemodynamics and the disease’s recruitability rather than by oxygen saturation alone.
