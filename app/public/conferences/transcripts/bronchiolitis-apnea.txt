The infant who stops breathing
Case conference. The infant who stops breathing. This is a fictional teaching case, with clinical review pending. A 6-week-old infant, measured weight 3.6 kilograms, was born at 34 weeks gestation. The caregiver reports nasal congestion and two episodes of pallor with absent breathing. Another episode occurs during assessment.

What do you notice?
At the bedside. Between episodes there is only mild recession. During the observed apnea the infant becomes limp and the heart rate falls. Mild visible effort between episodes is misleading.

Vital signs
The initial vital signs are as follows. Heart rate falls to 76 during apnea. Oxygen saturation falls to 72 percent during the episode. Temperature is 36.5 degrees Celsius. These are observations at this point in the case, rather than treatment targets.

Laboratory findings
Laboratory findings. Glucose is 68 milligrams per deciliter. After stabilization, venous pH is 7.27. Venous carbon dioxide is 59 millimeters of mercury. Interpret these results alongside the examination and the timing of sampling.

Diagnostic findings
Diagnostic findings. Apnea requires stabilization and evaluation for causes beyond bronchiolitis, including infection and metabolic or neurological disease. A viral prodrome does not close the differential.

What would you prioritize?
Think it through. Can you judge safety from the infant’s appearance between events? Consider your answer while the case continues. No screen interaction is needed.

Clinical reasoning & priorities
Conference discussion. Young age and prematurity increase concern when apnea is present. Intermittent loss of ventilation may be more urgent than the work of breathing observed between episodes. Priorities. 1. Provide immediate airway positioning and effective ventilation when breathing is inadequate. 2. Use continuous cardiorespiratory monitoring and prepare a reliable escalation plan. 3. Discuss neonatal or pediatric critical care destination and evaluation for alternative causes.

Reassessment & handoff
Reassessment and handoff. Assisted ventilation restores the heart rate and saturation. Recurrent episodes make unmonitored transfer unsafe. The team documents event timing, interventions and recovery. This is an authored fictional course; improvement is not guaranteed in a real patient.
