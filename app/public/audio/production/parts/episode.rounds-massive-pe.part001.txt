Now: Pulmonary vascular obstruction.

Pulmonary embolism physiology. Acute pulmonary vascular obstruction can abruptly raise RV afterload, impair LV filling and cause shock despite structurally normal lungs.

Build the mechanism first. Large pulmonary embolic obstruction abruptly raises RV afterload, causing RV dilation, reduced LV filling and potential obstructive shock; gas exchange often reflects increased dead space.

At the bedside, look for RV strain, low-flow signs and widening PaCO2–EtCO2 gap; be cautious with induction and excessive positive pressure in a preload- and RV-dependent circulation.

Two traps to actively avoid: Assuming clear lungs mean respiratory physiology is benign. And: Intubating without preparing for hemodynamic collapse.

The integration move is this: Massive PE is primarily a circulation problem expressed through the pulmonary vasculature.

Keep these terms available in your mental model: obstructive shock, RV strain, dead space.

Now connect Pulmonary embolism physiology to Right ventricular failure. Failure of a thin-walled ventricle that is highly sensitive to acute afterload, volume, perfusion pressure and ventricular interaction.

The relationship matters because The RV is thin-walled and highly sensitive to acute afterload. When it dilates, wall stress rises, coronary perfusion can fall and septal shift can reduce LV filling. At the bedside, track systemic pressure, venous congestion, echo geometry, oxygenation, CO2, acid-base status and ventilator pressures together. Small changes in P V R or intrathoracic pressure can have large hemodynamic consequences.

Now connect Pulmonary embolism physiology to Dead space. Ventilation that does not effectively participate in CO2 exchange, from conducting airways or ventilated but poorly perfused alveoli.

The relationship matters because Dead-space ventilation reaches regions that do not effectively eliminate CO2 because perfusion is absent or inadequate, or because gas remains in conducting airways. At the bedside, rising minute-ventilation requirement, widening PaCO2–EtCO2 gap or unexpectedly low EtCO2 can signal increasing dead space when ventilation is otherwise unchanged.

Stress-test the model. Imagine the patient changes in a way that makes assuming clear lungs mean respiratory physiology is benign tempting.
