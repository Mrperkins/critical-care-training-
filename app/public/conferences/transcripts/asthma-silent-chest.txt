When the wheeze disappears
Case conference. When the wheeze disappears. This is a fictional teaching case, with clinical review pending. A 12-year-old child, measured weight 42 kilograms, is being transferred for a severe asthma attack. Repeated bronchodilators have been delivered. During preparation for transport the child becomes quiet and stops holding the nebulizer. Staff initially describe the quieter breathing as improvement.

What do you notice?
At the bedside. Air entry is now minimal, the chest is nearly silent, and respiratory effort is shallow. The child responds slowly to voice and cannot complete a sentence. There is no unilateral loss of breath sounds.

Vital signs
The initial vital signs are as follows. Heart rate is 154 per minute. Blood pressure is 94 over 58. Respiratory rate has fallen from 42 to 18, with shallow breaths. Oxygen saturation is 88 percent despite supplemental oxygen. These are observations at this point in the case, rather than treatment targets.

Laboratory findings
Laboratory findings. Arterial pH is 7.19. Arterial carbon dioxide is 68 millimeters of mercury. Arterial oxygen tension is 57 millimeters of mercury on the current support. Interpret these results alongside the examination and the timing of sampling.

Diagnostic findings
Diagnostic findings. The blood gas and examination indicate failing ventilation. The priority is bedside resuscitation, rather than delaying support for a radiograph. After stabilization, imaging can assess suspected complications. In visual mode, an optional real reference image can be opened. It belongs to a different patient and is not the scan from this fictional case.

What would you prioritize?
Think it through. Is the fall in respiratory rate a favorable response? What risks must be anticipated if assisted ventilation becomes necessary? Consider your answer while the case continues. No screen interaction is needed.

Clinical reasoning & priorities
Conference discussion. Reduced respiratory effort with altered alertness and rising carbon dioxide indicates fatigue. Positive pressure can worsen dynamic hyperinflation and reduce venous return in severe obstruction. Priorities. 1. Escalate airway and ventilatory support immediately with the most experienced available team. 2. Continue disease-directed therapy and plan adequate expiratory time if positive-pressure ventilation is used. 3. Track blood pressure and ventilation; investigate sudden deterioration for air trapping, tube problems and pneumothorax.

Reassessment & handoff
Reassessment and handoff. After controlled airway management, the receiving team uses an obstruction-focused ventilation strategy and follows pressure, expiratory flow, gas exchange and perfusion. A gas is repeated after stabilization rather than assuming the initial settings are adequate. This is an authored fictional course; improvement is not guaranteed in a real patient.
