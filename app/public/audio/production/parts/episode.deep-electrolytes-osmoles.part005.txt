The relationship matters because Cortisol deficiency impairs vascular responsiveness and stress metabolism; mineralocorticoid deficiency can add sodium loss and hyperkalemia depending on the cause. Now connect Critical hypoglycemia to Acute liver failure. Acute hepatic failure couples coagulopathy, ammonia handling, cerebral edema risk, hypoglycemia, vasodilation and multiorgan dysfunction.

The relationship matters because Abrupt loss of hepatic synthetic and metabolic function can cause hyperammonemia, hypoglycemia, vasodilation, coagulopathy, renal failure and cerebral edema. At the bedside, trend neurologic status, glucose, acid-base, renal function and hemodynamics while involving a transplant-capable center early.

Now: When electrolyte physiology collides.

Electrolytes & cardiac electrophysiology. Potassium, magnesium, calcium and acid-base changes alter depolarization, repolarization and conduction.

Build the mechanism first. Potassium, magnesium and calcium alter resting membrane potential, channel function and action-potential duration, changing conduction and repolarization.

At the bedside, use ECG morphology as evidence of membrane toxicity and correct the responsible electrolyte while monitoring for rapid electrical change.

Two traps to actively avoid: Waiting for a numeric threshold despite toxic ECG findings. And: Correcting potassium without magnesium when refractory ectopy or QT-related risk persists.

The integration move is this: The ECG is a real-time readout of electrolyte physiology.

Keep these terms available in your mental model: QT, QRS, repolarization.

What expert-level use of this concept looks like is this: Use the ECG as physiologic evidence of electrolyte toxicity. Correct the membrane problem while treating the underlying electrolyte mechanism.

Now connect Electrolytes & cardiac electrophysiology to Rhythm & conduction emergencies. Tachy- and bradyarrhythmias become critical when they impair filling, output or perfusion; treatment depends on rhythm mechanism and patient stability.

The relationship matters because Rhythm affects cardiac output through rate, filling time, AV synchrony and ventricular activation; the same rate can be tolerated or catastrophic depending on reserve. At the bedside, first ask whether the rhythm is causing instability or compensating for another problem, then identify the electrical mechanism and choose synchronization, pacing or medication accordingly.
