This is a level 4 critical-care session. The goal is not to memorize a list. The goal is to build a model that lets you predict what the patient will do next.

By the end, you should be able to choose access strategy from urgency, therapy requirements and procedural risk; and recognize the failure modes unique to ultrasound piv, io and central venous access.

Now: Access is a clinical objective, not a device preference.

Ultrasound-guided peripheral IV. Ultrasound-guided peripheral access combines vessel selection, needle-tip tracking, catheter length and post-placement assessment.

Build the mechanism first. Ultrasound-guided peripheral access depends on selecting a vessel whose depth, diameter and course match the catheter and on tracking the actual needle tip.

At the bedside, scan before puncture, use multiple planes, advance enough catheter into the lumen and confirm function without soft-tissue infiltration.

Two traps to actively avoid: Following the needle shaft while losing the tip. And: Using a catheter too short for a deep vessel.

The integration move is this: Success is durable intraluminal catheter position, not merely blood return.

Keep these terms available in your mental model: short axis, long axis, tip tracking, catheter-to-vein ratio.

What expert-level use of this concept looks like is this: Choose an appropriate target by depth, diameter and course. Keep the true needle tip accounted for and anticipate failure from a catheter that is too short for the vessel depth.

Intraosseous access. Rapid vascular access through the medullary space when conventional access is delayed or unreliable.

Build the mechanism first. Intraosseous access reaches the medullary venous network through bone cortex, providing rapid vascular access when conventional access is delayed.

At the bedside, use the approved landmark and device-specific technique, then confirm stability, flush behavior and surrounding tissue integrity repeatedly.

Two traps to actively avoid: Assuming every device that enters bone is intramedullary and usable. And: Forgetting ongoing extravasation/compartment-risk checks.

The integration move is this: Speed matters, but landmark discipline and confirmation remain part of the procedure.

Keep these terms available in your mental model: cortex, medullary space, extravasation.

What expert-level use of this concept looks like is this: Identify the correct landmark for the chosen approved site and device. Confirm functional placement and recognize extravasation or compartment-risk signs.

Ultrasound-guided central venous access. Safe venous access requires anatomy, real-time tip awareness, vessel confirmation, wire control, sterile technique and complication recognition.

Build the mechanism first. Central venous access uses a wire-guided pathway from needle to catheter; the highest-risk errors occur when vessel identity, needle-tip position or wire location is assumed rather than confirmed.

At the bedside, map anatomy before puncture, keep the true needle tip accounted for, confirm venous placement by reliable methods and maintain continuous wire control.

Two traps to actively avoid: Using blood color alone to distinguish vein from artery. And: Dilating before confirming wire position in the intended vessel.

The integration move is this: Each stage should contain an explicit safety checkpoint before moving to the next irreversible step.

Keep these terms available in your mental model: Seldinger technique, compressibility, guidewire.

What expert-level use of this concept looks like is this: Distinguish vein from artery using more than color. Rehearse seeing the wire in the intended vessel before dilation and keeping control of the wire throughout.

High-risk medication safety. Use standardized concentration, labeling, independent verification, closed-loop communication and context checks for high-risk drugs.

Build the mechanism first. High-risk medication harm often arises from system failures: concentration mismatch, unlabeled syringes, look-alike products, route errors and communication breakdowns.

At the bedside, standardize concentration, isolate the preparation task, label immediately and use closed-loop verification before administration.

Two traps to actively avoid: Relying on expertise to replace a safety system. And: Assuming urgency makes verification optional.

The integration move is this: Design the workflow so a single cognitive slip is unlikely to reach the patient.

What expert-level use of this concept looks like is this: Catch concentration and route errors before administration. Use local protocol and pharmacy-standard concentrations rather than improvised memory.
