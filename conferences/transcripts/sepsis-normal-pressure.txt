A normal pressure in a sick child
Case conference. The blood pressure is still normal. This is a fictional teaching case, with clinical review pending. A 4-year-old child, measured weight 17 kilograms, has fever and poor intake. The caregiver reports rapidly reduced interaction and no urine for eight hours. A clinic recorded a normal blood pressure and sent the child for further assessment. There is no known heart disease. The clinic’s blood pressure reading is accurate information, but it is an incomplete summary. Before accepting the word stable, ask how the child has been behaving and whether the kidneys are producing urine.

The periphery tells a different story
The child is lethargic but rousable, with cool mottled extremities, weak peripheral pulses and capillary refill of five seconds. Breathing is rapid without focal wheeze. These are direct clues about perfusion. Notice the mismatch between a maintained pressure and the examination. That mismatch should sharpen our assessment rather than reassure us.

Compensation can preserve pressure
Heart rate is 166 per minute. Blood pressure is 96 over 58. Respiratory rate is 40 per minute. Temperature is 39.4 degrees Celsius. Oxygen saturation is 95 percent on room air. The child is using a fast heart rate and compensatory physiology to maintain the measured pressure. Waiting for that pressure to fall would mean waiting for a later stage of deterioration.

Lactate supports the concern
Venous lactate is 4.7 millimoles per liter. Glucose is 72 milligrams per deciliter. Venous pH is 7.28. The lactate fits the clinical picture of impaired perfusion, but we did not need a laboratory permission slip to recognize a sick child. We still need repeated clinical assessment as treatment begins.

Suspected infection with impaired perfusion
Suspected infection with poor perfusion raises concern for septic shock despite preserved blood pressure. Cultures and source evaluation should be coordinated without delaying time-critical treatment. A working diagnosis is enough to organize urgent care while we continue to investigate. The source may become clearer later; the perfusion problem is already in front of us.

Would you wait for hypotension?
Would you wait for hypotension before recognizing shock? Consider what threshold you would use to act if the monitor were covered. The history and examination should still lead you to a resuscitation plan.

Intervene and reassess each step
Children can maintain blood pressure through compensatory vasoconstriction. Perfusion, interaction and urine output reveal deterioration earlier. Lactate supports the assessment but cannot establish or exclude shock on its own. Activate pediatric sepsis care and give timely antimicrobials under the local pathway. Use measured, reassessed fluid therapy appropriate to the setting and physiology, watching for overload. Prepare vasoactive support when indicated and escalate early to pediatric critical care. Resuscitation is a series of decisions informed by response. After each intervention, ask whether perfusion improved and whether the lungs or heart are showing a cost from the treatment.

A partial response is useful information
After an initial measured fluid intervention, pulses and capillary refill improve only partly. The team reassesses lungs and cardiac function before further fluid, and prepares vasoactive support rather than repeating boluses automatically. A partial response is neither a reason to abandon the diagnosis nor a reason to repeat the same action indefinitely. It tells us to re-examine the physiology before choosing the next intervention.
