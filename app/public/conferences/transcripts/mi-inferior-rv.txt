Inferior ST elevation with clear lungs and low pressure
Case conference. Inferior ST elevation with clear lungs and low pressure. This is a fictional teaching case, with clinical review pending. A 58-year-old patient develops crushing chest pain and nausea. The first ECG shows an inferior injury pattern. Before any nitrate is given, blood pressure is already low. The transfer request is for urgent coronary intervention.

What do you notice?
At the bedside. The patient is pale and diaphoretic with elevated neck veins and clear lung fields. The team considers right-ventricular involvement and conduction complications.

Vital signs
The initial vital signs are as follows. Heart rate is 52 per minute. Blood pressure is 84 over 56. Saturation is 96 percent on room air. These are observations at this point in the case, rather than treatment targets.

Laboratory findings
Laboratory findings. The initial high-sensitivity troponin is below the laboratory’s decision cutoff; this early sample does not exclude an acute occlusion. Potassium is 4.1 millimoles per liter. Interpret these results alongside the examination and the timing of sampling.

Diagnostic findings
Diagnostic findings. The synthetic ECG report describes ST elevation in leads two, three and a V F, reciprocal change in one and a V L, and ST elevation in right-sided lead V four R. These findings support inferior infarction with right-ventricular involvement in context.

What would you prioritize?
Think it through. Should a negative early troponin delay the reperfusion pathway? What does the hypotension mean for vasodilator decisions? Consider your answer while the case continues. No screen interaction is needed.

Clinical reasoning & priorities
Conference discussion. A time-critical ECG pattern must be acted on in its clinical context. Right-ventricular involvement and hypotension require careful attention to preload, rhythm and medication effects. Priorities. 1. Activate the appropriate acute coronary occlusion and reperfusion pathway. 2. Avoid treatments that worsen existing hypotension; reassess perfusion and volume responsiveness individually. 3. Monitor for bradyarrhythmia and conduction block and prepare indicated support.

Reassessment & handoff
Reassessment and handoff. The receiving catheterization team is notified of the ECG pattern, right-sided findings and unstable pressure. Serial ECGs and perfusion checks continue during stabilization. This is an authored fictional course; improvement is not guaranteed in a real patient.
