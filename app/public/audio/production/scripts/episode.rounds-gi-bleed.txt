This is a level 4 critical-care session. The goal is not to memorize a list. The goal is to build a model that lets you predict what the patient will do next.

By the end, you should be able to run hemorrhage resuscitation and definitive-control planning in parallel; and recognize physiology and airway features that make severe gi bleeding uniquely dangerous.

Now: Resuscitate while locating the source.

Severe GI hemorrhage. Upper and lower GI bleeding require simultaneous hemorrhagic resuscitation, airway judgment, coagulopathy assessment and definitive source control.

Build the mechanism first. Severe GI bleeding combines blood loss with source-specific physiology such as portal hypertension and can rapidly create airway, perfusion and coagulation problems.

At the bedside, resuscitate while mobilizing definitive endoscopic/interventional control and managing anticoagulant/coagulopathy issues.

Two traps to actively avoid: Delaying source control until every vital sign normalizes. And: Intubating reflexively without considering the hemodynamic cost.

The integration move is this: Resuscitation and source control are simultaneous workflows.

Keep these terms available in your mental model: variceal, nonvariceal, endoscopy.

What expert-level use of this concept looks like is this: Run resuscitation and definitive-control planning in parallel. Recognize when airway, portal-hypertension or anticoagulation issues change the strategy.

Now: Blood, calcium, temperature and clot.

Massive transfusion & hemorrhagic resuscitation. Hemorrhagic resuscitation integrates source control, balanced component replacement, calcium, temperature, coagulation and perfusion.

Build the mechanism first. Major hemorrhage produces loss of oxygen-carrying capacity, volume and hemostatic components while hypothermia, hypocalcemia, dilution and acidosis can worsen coagulation.

At the bedside, activate the hemorrhage system early, warm the resuscitation, track calcium/coagulation/temperature and never lose sight of definitive source control.

Two traps to actively avoid: Running product delivery as a logistics task disconnected from physiology. And: Allowing source control to become secondary to transfusion mechanics.

The integration move is this: Resuscitation buys time; source control ends the hemorrhage.

Keep these terms available in your mental model: M T P, fibrinogen, ionized calcium, lethal triad.

What expert-level use of this concept looks like is this: Run the resuscitation as a system rather than as isolated blood-unit administration. Recognize dilutional/coagulopathic, hypocalcemic and hypothermic deterioration early.

Now: Endoscopic/interventional control is the endpoint.

Source control. Definitive management of infected material, devitalized tissue or an infected device can be as important as antimicrobial therapy.

Build the mechanism first. Antibiotics cannot sterilize every infected collection, necrotic tissue or infected device. Persistent nidus can sustain inflammatory and microbial burden despite apparently appropriate antimicrobial therapy.

At the bedside, ask early whether drainage, debridement, device removal or surgery is required and whether it is actually happening.

Two traps to actively avoid: Escalating antibiotic breadth when the real failure is lack of source control. And: Waiting for complete hemodynamic normalization before addressing a time-sensitive source.

The integration move is this: Sepsis treatment is host support plus antimicrobials plus definitive control of the source.

Keep these terms available in your mental model: drainage, debridement, device removal.

What expert-level use of this concept looks like is this: Identify when antibiotics alone are unlikely to succeed. Escalate procedural or surgical source control as part of the resuscitation plan.

Now connect Source control to Septic shock physiology. Distributive shock with dynamic changes in vascular tone, venous capacitance, myocardial performance, microcirculation and oxygen extraction.

The relationship matters because Septic shock can combine vasoplegia, venous pooling, myocardial dysfunction, endothelial leak and disturbed microcirculation. Cardiac output may be low, normal or high. At the bedside, reassess vascular tone, flow, congestion and perfusion after each intervention rather than assuming a fixed distributive phenotype.

Now connect Source control to Antimicrobial PK/PD in critical illness. Critical illness changes volume of distribution, clearance and target attainment, so dose strategy must match drug and patient physiology.

The relationship matters because Critical illness alters volume of distribution, protein binding and clearance. Drug efficacy depends on whether exposure matches the relevant PK/PD target. At the bedside, account for augmented renal clearance, AKI, C R R T, fluid shifts and obesity rather than assuming standard dosing produces standard exposure.
