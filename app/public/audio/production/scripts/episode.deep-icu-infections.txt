Now: CNS infection: treat while diagnosing.

CNS infection. Meningitis and encephalitis can rapidly produce cerebral edema, seizure and shock; treatment often precedes definitive microbiologic diagnosis.

Build the mechanism first. Meningeal or parenchymal infection can trigger edema, seizures, altered cerebral perfusion and systemic shock; antimicrobial delay can be harmful.

At the bedside, stabilize airway/perfusion and initiate appropriate empiric therapy promptly while deciding whether imaging/instability alters lumbar-puncture timing.

Two traps to actively avoid: Delaying antimicrobials for an unsafe or logistically delayed LP. And: Missing encephalitis because CSF findings are initially subtle.

The integration move is this: Diagnostic sequencing must never obstruct time-sensitive treatment of a high-risk CNS infection.

Keep these terms available in your mental model: meningitis, encephalitis, CSF.

What expert-level use of this concept looks like is this: Start time-sensitive empiric therapy while managing airway, seizure and ICP concerns. Recognize when imaging or instability changes lumbar-puncture timing.

Now connect CNS infection to Status epilepticus. Ongoing seizures become a time-dependent systemic and neurologic emergency requiring rapid recognition, medication sequencing and search for cause.

The relationship matters because Persistent seizure activity becomes increasingly self-sustaining while causing metabolic demand, hypoxemia, acidosis, hyperthermia and neuronal injury. At the bedside, treat promptly in phases, support physiology and consider ongoing nonconvulsive seizure when convulsions stop but mental status does not recover.

Now connect CNS infection to ICP, CPP & cerebral perfusion. Cerebral perfusion depends on the gradient across the cerebral circulation and is influenced by pressure, venous outflow, CO2 and intracranial compliance.

The relationship matters because CPP is the pressure gradient available to drive cerebral blood flow and is influenced by arterial pressure, ICP, venous pressure and autoregulation. Intracranial compliance falls as compensatory reserve is exhausted. At the bedside, interpret ICP as a dynamic system with waveform, position, venous drainage, CO2 and sedation effects. A “normal” MAP does not guarantee adequate cerebral perfusion.

Now: Biofilm and device source control.

Catheter & device-associated infection. Intravascular lines, urinary catheters and implanted devices can become infection sources whose management may require device removal.

Build the mechanism first. Foreign material permits biofilm and persistent microbial adherence, making infection harder to clear without removing or exchanging the source device when feasible.

At the bedside, assess every invasive device for necessity and source potential, obtain appropriate cultures and pair antimicrobial therapy with device/source decisions.

Two traps to actively avoid: Broadening antibiotics indefinitely while leaving an infected device in place. And: Removing essential access without a replacement plan in an unstable patient.

The integration move is this: A device can be both lifesaving infrastructure and the uncontrolled source.

Keep these terms available in your mental model: CLABSI, biofilm, device removal.

What expert-level use of this concept looks like is this: Recognize when the device itself is the source-control problem. Balance culture strategy, empiric therapy and device necessity.

Now: Intra-abdominal infection and surgery/drainage.

Intra-abdominal infection. Perforation, abscess, ischemia and biliary infection often require urgent source control in addition to antimicrobial therapy.

Build the mechanism first. Perforation, abscess, biliary obstruction and ischemic bowel create ongoing contamination or necrotic source that antibiotics alone may not control.

At the bedside, in persistent shock, ask whether drainage, decompression or surgery is delayed and whether anatomy makes the source inaccessible to medical therapy.

Two traps to actively avoid: Escalating vasopressors while source control stalls. And: Attributing persistent lactate to sepsis alone despite possible ischemic bowel.

The integration move is this: For many abdominal infections, the definitive antimicrobial is a procedure.

Keep these terms available in your mental model: perforation, abscess, biliary sepsis.

What expert-level use of this concept looks like is this: Recognize when persistent shock reflects uncontrolled abdominal source. Coordinate antimicrobial therapy with procedural or operative control.

Now: PK/PD still matters after the drug is chosen.

Antimicrobial PK/PD in critical illness. Critical illness changes volume of distribution, clearance and target attainment, so dose strategy must match drug and patient physiology.

Build the mechanism first. Critical illness alters volume of distribution, protein binding and clearance. Drug efficacy depends on whether exposure matches the relevant PK/PD target.

At the bedside, account for augmented renal clearance, AKI, C R R T, fluid shifts and obesity rather than assuming standard dosing produces standard exposure.

Two traps to actively avoid: Reducing doses reflexively in early sepsis because creatinine is high. And: Ignoring extracorporeal clearance.

The integration move is this: The antimicrobial choice and the exposure strategy are separate decisions.

Keep these terms available in your mental model: time above MIC, AUC/MIC, augmented renal clearance.

What expert-level use of this concept looks like is this: Explain why standard dosing can underdose a shocked or hyperdynamic patient. Integrate renal support and changing clearance into antimicrobial strategy.

Now: Ask whether the source is actually controlled.

Now connect Source control to Septic shock physiology. Distributive shock with dynamic changes in vascular tone, venous capacitance, myocardial performance, microcirculation and oxygen extraction.

The relationship matters because Septic shock can combine vasoplegia, venous pooling, myocardial dysfunction, endothelial leak and disturbed microcirculation. Cardiac output may be low, normal or high. At the bedside, reassess vascular tone, flow, congestion and perfusion after each intervention rather than assuming a fixed distributive phenotype.
