A new symptom changes the case
Case conference. A new headache during DKA treatment. This is a fictional teaching case, with clinical review pending. A 7-year-old child, measured weight 22 kilograms, is three hours into treatment for severe DKA. The original presentation included marked dehydration and acidosis. During transfer preparation the child develops headache, vomiting and irritability, then becomes drowsy. Glucose has been falling, which initially distracts the team from the neurological change. The metabolic numbers are moving in a direction the team expected. Then the child develops a new problem. This case asks us to notice when the original treatment story is no longer the only story.

The neurological baseline has changed
The child opens eyes to voice, has a new decline in interaction and a heart rate that is falling despite continued illness. Pupils are equal. No sedative has been given. Equal pupils can be a powerful distraction when we are hoping the child is simply tired. The comparison that matters is with this child’s earlier interaction, especially without a medication explanation.

Read the change in heart rate
Heart rate is 82, down from 132. Blood pressure is now 128 over 82. Respiratory rate is 28 per minute. Oxygen saturation is 99 percent on room air. A lower heart rate can look like recovery from dehydration. Here it arrives with headache and declining interaction. Pair the trend with those new symptoms before deciding what it means.

Metabolic improvement does not settle neurological risk
Glucose is 290 milligrams per deciliter. Venous pH is 7.18. Measured sodium is 140 millimoles per liter. Potassium is 3.6 millimoles per liter. These values help us assess immediate alternatives, including glucose abnormalities. They do not cancel a new neurological examination. We can investigate competing explanations while acting on a time-critical concern.

A clinical emergency before confirmation
Suspected DKA-associated cerebral injury is a clinical emergency. Equal pupils and a reasonable glucose do not exclude it. Treatment should not wait for confirmatory cranial imaging. The important decision is about timing. A later image may help clarify the course, but a request for proof should not become the reason a deteriorating child waits for treatment.

Which change matters more here: the falling glucose or the new neurological deterioration? Put yourself at the transfer bedside. What would make you stop departure preparation and call for an immediate change in the resuscitation plan? Hold that answer as we move to management.

Treat the new syndrome and reassess
The new headache, vomiting, change in behavior and relative slowing of heart rate raise concern for cerebral injury. Hypoglycemia and other causes still require immediate assessment, but do not delay syndrome-directed treatment. Call pediatric critical care and begin the protocol for suspected cerebral injury, including indicated hyperosmolar therapy. Reassess airway protection, perfusion and glucose immediately; avoid unnecessary interruptions in care for imaging. If intubation is unavoidable, use expert planning to preserve appropriate compensation and avoid an abrupt rise in carbon dioxide. Two tasks run together: act on suspected cerebral injury and check for another reversible cause of deterioration. If an airway intervention becomes unavoidable, the metabolic compensation needs explicit planning.

Follow recovery, then clarify the cause
The team treats suspected cerebral injury and transfers with frequent documented neurological checks. A later scan is used to evaluate complications and alternatives after initial resuscitation; it is not the prerequisite for treatment. A handoff should describe the exact sequence of headache, behavior change, intervention and response. That sequence helps the next team understand a patient whose examination may already have changed again.

DKA: reassessment can reveal a second emergency
Case synopsis. Metabolic improvement did not ensure neurological safety. A new headache and reduced interaction during pediatric DKA required immediate assessment and treatment for possible cerebral injury. The teaching point lives in the change from baseline. A patient can improve in one physiological domain while becoming critically worse in another, and our plan must be able to change with that.
