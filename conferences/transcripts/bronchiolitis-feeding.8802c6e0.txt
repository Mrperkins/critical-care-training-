Breathing interrupts feeding
Case conference. Acute viral bronchiolitis with hypoxemia and inadequate oral intake. This is a fictional teaching case, with clinical review pending. A 5-month-old infant, measured weight 6.4 kilograms, was born at term. After three days of coryza, the infant has increasing cough, reduced feeds and fewer wet diapers. There has been no witnessed apnea. The father calls EMS because the infant takes only brief feeds before pulling away to breathe. A sibling has a cold. The infant has diffuse bilateral findings and no focal asymmetry or choking history. The disease process is viral bronchiolitis; the specific virus is not identified because the initial management does not require that result. For an infant, a feeding history is also a functional respiratory history. Ask the caregiver what happens when the bottle or breast is offered, and how that compares with yesterday.

Effort and intake are linked
The infant is alert with subcostal recession, diffuse crackles and wheeze, and copious nasal secretions. Feeding attempts increase distress. The child has to coordinate breathing with feeding. When that ordinary task becomes difficult, it adds information that a spot saturation cannot capture.

A clinical bronchiolitis pattern
This is a clinical presentation of bronchiolitis. Routine chest radiographs and viral testing are not necessary for every typical case; testing depends on the clinical question and setting.  The wheeze can tempt us to borrow an asthma treatment story. Start instead with the infant’s age, course and overall pattern, and build supportive care around the problems actually present.

What does the feeding history change?

Support breathing and hydration together
Respiratory distress can compromise both ventilation and hydration. Supportive care and reassessment are central; wheeze in an infant does not automatically imply asthma. Support oxygenation and clear obstructing nasal secretions when needed. Plan hydration that the infant can tolerate safely. Escalate respiratory support for persistent distress, hypoxemia, apnea or fatigue under the local pediatric pathway. A useful plan describes how we will know supportive care is enough. That means naming the signs that would trigger a change in respiratory support, rather than only naming a saturation goal.

The saturation improves first
With support, saturation reaches 94 percent, but respiratory rate remains high. The team continues to assess effort and hydration rather than declaring success from saturation alone. Oxygen and gentle nasal suction improve oxygenation, but feeding still produces distress. The team arranges hydration through a route selected by the pediatric service according to respiratory effort and aspiration risk rather than forcing oral feeds. Because effort persists, the infant is admitted and respiratory support is escalated as clinically indicated. In this synthetic course, breathing and feeding improve over two hospital days; the infant returns to adequate oral intake and maintains the pathway discharge saturation without supplemental oxygen. No bacterial pneumonia is diagnosed. This is a real improvement in one domain. The continuing respiratory rate and feeding problem explain why the team keeps watching and adjusting rather than closing the case.
