You're listening to Stroke, ICH and SAH: perfusion goals change with the lesion and the clock. LVO, hemorrhage, hydrocephalus, rebleeding, ICP and delayed cerebral ischemia.

This is a level 5 critical-care session. The goal is not to memorize a list. The goal is to build a model that lets you predict what the patient will do next.

By the end, you should be able to choose pressure/perfusion priorities according to ischemic versus hemorrhagic pathology and treatment phase; and recognize hydrocephalus, herniation and delayed ischemia as evolving neurologic emergencies.

Now: LVO and the salvageable penumbra.

Acute ischemic stroke & LVO. Large-vessel occlusion threatens salvageable tissue over time; perfusion depends on collateral circulation, pressure and rapid reperfusion.

Build the mechanism first. Large-vessel occlusion abruptly reduces regional cerebral blood flow; collateral circulation temporarily sustains penumbral tissue until reperfusion or infarction occurs.

At the bedside, recognize cortical/LVO syndromes, protect oxygenation and perfusion, establish last-known-well and move rapidly toward imaging/reperfusion pathways.

Two traps to actively avoid: Lowering blood pressure reflexively before reperfusion strategy is defined. And: Letting transport/airway procedures unnecessarily delay thrombectomy-capable care.

The integration move is this: Time, collaterals and perfusion determine how much brain remains salvageable.

Keep these terms available in your mental model: penumbra, large-vessel occlusion, thrombectomy.

What expert-level use of this concept looks like is this: Recognize LVO syndromes and time-sensitive reperfusion pathways. Avoid secondary hypoxemia/hypotension while protecting access to definitive therapy.

Now: Hematoma, expansion and cerebral perfusion.

ICH & subarachnoid hemorrhage. Hemorrhagic stroke combines hematoma/aneurysm-related injury with risks of expansion, hydrocephalus, ICP crisis, seizure and delayed ischemia.

Build the mechanism first. Intracranial hemorrhage causes direct tissue injury and mass effect; SAH adds aneurysm rebleeding, hydrocephalus and later delayed ischemic complications.

At the bedside, control blood pressure thoughtfully, reverse relevant anticoagulation, watch ICP/hydrocephalus and preserve cerebral perfusion while neurosurgical/aneurysm treatment proceeds.

Two traps to actively avoid: Driving pressure so low that CPP is compromised. And: Missing evolving hydrocephalus in a patient whose mental status declines.

The integration move is this: Hemorrhage management is simultaneously bleeding control, pressure control and brain perfusion management.

Keep these terms available in your mental model: hematoma expansion, aneurysm, hydrocephalus.

What expert-level use of this concept looks like is this: Balance blood-pressure control with cerebral perfusion. Recognize hydrocephalus, herniation and rebleeding risk early.

Now: The SAH problem changes days later.

SAH vasospasm & delayed cerebral ischemia. After aneurysmal SAH, delayed cerebral ischemia may reflect vasospasm and broader perfusion disturbances days after the initial bleed.

Build the mechanism first. Delayed cerebral ischemia after aneurysmal SAH involves vasospasm and broader microcirculatory/perfusion dysfunction, typically separated in time from the initial bleed.

At the bedside, track new focal deficits or reduced consciousness after the aneurysm is secured, maintain nimodipine when appropriate and escalate perfusion/vascular evaluation.

Two traps to actively avoid: Assuming every late decline is sedation or hydrocephalus. And: Confusing rebleeding risk management with later DCI hemodynamic goals.

The integration move is this: SAH changes phase over days; the correct hemodynamic concern changes with it.

Keep these terms available in your mental model: nimodipine, vasospasm, DCI.

What expert-level use of this concept looks like is this: Distinguish early rebleeding/ICP issues from later DCI risk. Use neurologic change and perfusion monitoring to escalate evaluation.
