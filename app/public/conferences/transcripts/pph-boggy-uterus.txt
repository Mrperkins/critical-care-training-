The measured loss is still rising
Case conference. The bleeding continues after birth. This is a fictional teaching case, with clinical review pending. A 29-year-old patient is 20 minutes postpartum after vaginal delivery at term. Measured blood loss is approximately 1,200 milliliters and rising. The placenta has been delivered. The patient feels faint and nauseated. The measured volume and the patient’s symptoms tell us this is active resuscitation. Waiting for the next laboratory result would leave the source continuing to remove circulating volume.

Atony is a source clue
The uterus is boggy and enlarged, with ongoing brisk bleeding. The patient is pale with cool extremities. The team evaluates tone, trauma, tissue and coagulation causes together. The boggy uterus gives us an immediate treatment direction. It should focus the first actions while we continue to check whether another cause is contributing.

Hemorrhage with impaired perfusion
Heart rate is 132 per minute. Blood pressure is 88 over 52. Respiratory rate is 28 per minute. Saturation is 97 percent. Tachycardia and hypotension fit the observed loss. The oxygen saturation is not a measure of how much blood remains in circulation or whether tissue delivery is adequate.

The early hemoglobin can lag behind the event
Early hemoglobin is 10.4 grams per deciliter; this does not exclude major acute blood loss. Fibrinogen is 190 milligrams per deciliter. Lactate is 4.9 millimoles per liter. An early hemoglobin may sound less alarming than the bedside picture. Here the measured bleeding, perfusion findings and coagulation results carry more weight than an isolated initial concentration.

Control hemorrhage while assessing the cause
The bedside finding of a boggy uterus supports atony, while continued assessment looks for additional causes. Imaging must not delay hemorrhage control and resuscitation. This diagnosis is being made through direct examination during treatment. The source assessment and the resuscitation are parallel tasks, and both need a clear team lead.

Is the hemoglobin a reason to wait?
Would you wait for hemoglobin to fall before activating a hemorrhage response? Decide what evidence is enough to activate the hemorrhage response. Think about what can change in the time it would take to wait for another sample.

Treat the source and restore circulation
Acute bleeding can initially leave a deceptively preserved hemoglobin. The observed blood loss and shock require action, and a relatively low fibrinogen is concerning in obstetric hemorrhage. Activate the obstetric hemorrhage pathway, including source control, uterine measures and indicated uterotonics. Coordinate blood products and timely tranexamic acid when indicated, with warming and monitoring under protocol. Quantify ongoing loss and escalate promptly for mechanical or surgical control if bleeding continues. The treatments work toward complementary goals. Source control limits continued loss; resuscitation supports the patient while control is achieved. Neither task should wait for the other to be completely finished.

Slowing bleeding still needs measurement
Bleeding slows after treatment but the team continues quantification and perfusion monitoring. Total blood loss, uterotonic times, blood products and coagulation results accompany the handoff. A visual impression that bleeding is slowing is welcome, but the trend still needs to be measured. The timeline helps the next team judge both the remaining deficit and the response.
