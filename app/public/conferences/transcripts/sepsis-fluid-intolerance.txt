Shock with new crackles
Case conference. Shock with new crackles. This is a fictional teaching case, with clinical review pending. A 9-year-old child, measured weight 29 kilograms, has suspected bacterial infection. The referring team has given initial fluids and antimicrobials. The child remains poorly perfused and now needs more oxygen. The transport team must decide whether additional fluid is likely to help.

What do you notice?
At the bedside. The child is confused, with weak pulses, prolonged refill and new bilateral crackles. The liver edge is more prominent than in the earlier examination.

Vital signs
The initial vital signs are as follows. Heart rate is 158 per minute. Blood pressure is 78 over 42. Respiratory rate is 36 per minute. Oxygen saturation is 90 percent on supplemental oxygen. These are observations at this point in the case, rather than treatment targets.

Laboratory findings
Laboratory findings. Lactate is 6.2 millimoles per liter. Venous pH is 7.23. Glucose is 108 milligrams per deciliter. Interpret these results alongside the examination and the timing of sampling.

Diagnostic findings
Diagnostic findings. Bedside ultrasound shows reduced global ventricular function and bilateral lung B-lines. These findings inform tolerance of further fluid but do not independently prove the infection source.

What would you prioritize?
Think it through. Does ongoing hypotension justify unlimited fluid? Which new findings change your next intervention? Consider your answer while the case continues. No screen interaction is needed.

Clinical reasoning & priorities
Conference discussion. Persistent shock can coexist with myocardial dysfunction and fluid accumulation. The next step must reflect both perfusion and fluid tolerance; cold or warm extremities alone should not dictate a vasoactive drug. Priorities. 1. Escalate vasoactive and respiratory support under pediatric critical care guidance. 2. Pause automatic fluid repetition and reassess perfusion, cardiac function and lung findings. 3. Recheck the infection source, antimicrobial delivery and access while preparing a high-acuity handoff.

Reassessment & handoff
Reassessment and handoff. With vasoactive support and respiratory assistance, perfusion begins to improve. The handoff includes total fluid volume, response to each intervention, ultrasound limitations and the evolving oxygen requirement. This is an authored fictional course; improvement is not guaranteed in a real patient.
