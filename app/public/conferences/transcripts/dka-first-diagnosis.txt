Vomiting, thirst and deep breathing
Case conference. Vomiting, thirst and deep breathing. This is a fictional teaching case, with clinical review pending. A 10-year-old child, measured weight 30 kilograms, has no known diabetes. The family describes two weeks of thirst, frequent urination and weight loss, followed by vomiting and abdominal pain. The child is transferred after initial assessment at a community hospital.

What do you notice?
At the bedside. The child is alert but tired, with dry mucosa and deep, regular breathing. Capillary refill is three seconds. There are no focal neurological findings, headache or signs of pulmonary edema.

Vital signs
The initial vital signs are as follows. Heart rate is 128 per minute. Blood pressure is 104 over 64. Respiratory rate is 32 with deep breaths. Oxygen saturation is 98 percent on room air. These are observations at this point in the case, rather than treatment targets.

Laboratory findings
Laboratory findings. Glucose is 480 milligrams per deciliter. Venous pH is 7.12. Bicarbonate is 8 millimoles per liter. Beta hydroxybutyrate is 5.8 millimoles per liter. Potassium is 4.8 millimoles per liter. Interpret these results alongside the examination and the timing of sampling.

Diagnostic findings
Diagnostic findings. Hyperglycemia, ketonemia and metabolic acidosis support DKA. Deep breathing is compensatory. Routine cranial imaging is not indicated solely because DKA is present.

What would you prioritize?
Think it through. Does a normal serum potassium exclude total-body depletion? What should accompany the handoff before starting a long transfer? Consider your answer while the case continues. No screen interaction is needed.

Clinical reasoning & priorities
Conference discussion. Serum potassium may appear normal despite depletion. Fluid, insulin, electrolyte and dextrose decisions depend on repeated assessment and a pediatric DKA pathway, including neurological monitoring. Priorities. 1. Verify measured weight, fluid history, urine output, electrolyte results and timing of insulin. 2. Use the pediatric DKA protocol for fluids and insulin; avoid an insulin bolus. 3. Arrange serial glucose, electrolytes and neurological examinations and prepare for deterioration.

Reassessment & handoff
Reassessment and handoff. Two hours later glucose is 330, potassium is 3.7 and the child remains alert. The team adjusts dextrose and electrolytes under its pathway while continuing treatment of ketosis. Glucose improvement alone does not mean DKA has resolved. This is an authored fictional course; improvement is not guaranteed in a real patient.
