The vomiting has a longer story
Case conference. Vomiting, thirst and deep breathing. This is a fictional teaching case, with clinical review pending. A 10-year-old child, measured weight 30 kilograms, has no known diabetes. The family describes two weeks of thirst, frequent urination and weight loss, followed by vomiting and abdominal pain. The child is transferred after initial assessment at a community hospital. If the handoff begins with vomiting, ask what happened before the vomiting. The two-week history changes the meaning of the final few hours and points us toward a metabolic process.

Deep breathing is part of the compensation
The child is alert but tired, with dry mucosa and deep, regular breathing. Capillary refill is three seconds. There are no focal neurological findings, headache or signs of pulmonary edema. The breathing can draw our attention toward a primary lung problem. Listen to its regular depth and connect it with the history. We also need a neurological baseline before the treatment course evolves.

Oxygenation and ventilation answer different questions
Heart rate is 128 per minute. Blood pressure is 104 over 64. Respiratory rate is 32 with deep breaths. Oxygen saturation is 98 percent on room air. Normal saturation tells us about oxygenation at this moment. It does not explain away the deep breathing or the metabolic illness. The respiratory pattern is doing useful compensatory work.

Glucose, ketones and acidosis
Glucose is 480 milligrams per deciliter. Venous pH is 7.12. Bicarbonate is 8 millimoles per liter. Beta hydroxybutyrate is 5.8 millimoles per liter. Potassium is 4.8 millimoles per liter. Now the history and the laboratory pattern meet. Keep the potassium value in view: its initial position in the reference range is not a measure of how much potassium remains in the body.

DKA: establish the pathway
Hyperglycemia, ketonemia and metabolic acidosis support DKA. Deep breathing is compensatory. Routine cranial imaging is not indicated solely because DKA is present. We have enough information to organize a pediatric DKA handoff. The next task is to reconstruct what fluids and medications have already been given, so the receiving team is continuing one coherent course.

What must travel with this child?
Does a normal serum potassium exclude total-body depletion? What should accompany the handoff before starting a long transfer? Imagine a handoff that only says the glucose is improving. What would the next team still need to safely continue treatment? Think about the timeline as well as the latest laboratory panel.

Plan a monitored metabolic correction
Serum potassium may appear normal despite depletion. Fluid, insulin, electrolyte and dextrose decisions depend on repeated assessment and a pediatric DKA pathway, including neurological monitoring. Verify measured weight, fluid history, urine output, electrolyte results and timing of insulin. Use the pediatric DKA protocol for fluids and insulin; avoid an insulin bolus. Arrange serial glucose, electrolytes and neurological examinations and prepare for deterioration. The pathway links several moving parts. A change in glucose may alter dextrose needs while ketosis still requires treatment. That is why isolated numbers are a poor substitute for a shared plan.

Glucose improves; treatment continues
Two hours later glucose is 330, potassium is 3.7 and the child remains alert. The team adjusts dextrose and electrolytes under its pathway while continuing treatment of ketosis. Glucose improvement alone does not mean DKA has resolved. This is the result we were preparing to interpret. Potassium has moved even though the child looks better. Reassessment changes the next treatment adjustment rather than simply confirming the first diagnosis.

New-onset DKA: the correction needs a timeline
