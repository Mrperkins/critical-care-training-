This is a level 4 critical-care session. The goal is not to memorize a list. The goal is to build a model that lets you predict what the patient will do next.

By the end, you should be able to predict major ways critical illness changes drug exposure; and reassess dosing as organ function, fluid state and extracorporeal support change.

Now: Fluid shifts change distribution.

Critical-care pharmacokinetics & interactions. Critical illness alters absorption, distribution, protein binding, hepatic metabolism and renal/extracorporeal clearance.

Build the mechanism first. Critical illness changes volume of distribution, protein binding, hepatic metabolism and renal/extracorporeal clearance, so dose-exposure relationships become dynamic.

At the bedside, reassess dosing after fluid shifts, organ failure, C R R T/E C M O or interacting drugs instead of treating the admission dose as permanent.

Two traps to actively avoid: Assuming creatinine alone predicts drug clearance. And: Ignoring protein binding and circuit sequestration for highly bound/lipophilic drugs.

The integration move is this: Drug dosing is a moving physiology problem.

Keep these terms available in your mental model: volume of distribution, clearance, protein binding, drug interaction.

What expert-level use of this concept looks like is this: Predict when standard dosing is unlikely to produce standard exposure. Recognize organ support and interacting drugs as moving pharmacokinetic variables.

Now: Extracorporeal support changes exposure.

C R R T physiology & prescription literacy. Continuous renal replacement uses diffusion and/or convection to manage solute, acid-base and fluid balance in unstable patients.

Build the mechanism first. C R R T uses diffusion, convection and ultrafiltration to control solute, acid-base and volume continuously. Prescribed dose can differ substantially from delivered dose.

At the bedside, separate total effluent from net patient fluid removal and interpret access, filter and return pressures as clues to the physical circuit.

Two traps to actively avoid: Calling every effluent liter “fluid removed from the patient.” And: Ignoring downtime and clotting when assessing delivered therapy.

The integration move is this: C R R T is both renal support and a hemodynamic intervention.

Keep these terms available in your mental model: effluent dose, CVVH, CVVHD, CVVHDF, filtration fraction.

What expert-level use of this concept looks like is this: Explain diffusion versus convection and net ultrafiltration. Recognize how circuit, anticoagulation and prescribed versus delivered dose change therapy.

Now connect C R R T physiology & prescription literacy to Shock phenotyping. Identify inadequate effective circulation by integrating flow, vascular tone, congestion, oxygen delivery and organ response.

The relationship matters because Shock is inadequate effective tissue perfusion and oxygen utilization, not merely hypotension. Flow, vascular tone, capacitance, congestion and extraction can fail in different combinations. At the bedside, phenotype the circulation repeatedly. A patient can move from one dominant mechanism to another during resuscitation.

E C M O circuit & physiology literacy. Extracorporeal support changes preload, afterload, gas exchange and oxygen delivery through a circuit whose configuration determines what it can support.

Build the mechanism first. E C M O drains venous blood, pumps it through a membrane lung and returns it to the circulation. VV supports gas exchange; VA additionally supports systemic flow and changes cardiac loading conditions.

At the bedside, follow blood through drainage, pump, oxygenator and return while considering native heart/lung function and cannula position.

Two traps to actively avoid: Equating pump RPM with effective support. And: Ignoring recirculation, LV distension or differential hypoxemia.

The integration move is this: The circuit and the native circulation are one coupled system.

Keep these terms available in your mental model: VV E C M O, VA E C M O, sweep gas, recirculation, differential hypoxemia.

What expert-level use of this concept looks like is this: Distinguish VV from VA support goals and limitations. Reason from circuit flow, native heart/lung function and cannula position to common failure patterns.

Now connect E C M O circuit & physiology literacy to Right ventricular failure. Failure of a thin-walled ventricle that is highly sensitive to acute afterload, volume, perfusion pressure and ventricular interaction.

The relationship matters because The RV is thin-walled and highly sensitive to acute afterload. When it dilates, wall stress rises, coronary perfusion can fall and septal shift can reduce LV filling. At the bedside, track systemic pressure, venous congestion, echo geometry, oxygenation, CO2, acid-base status and ventilator pressures together. Small changes in P V R or intrathoracic pressure can have large hemodynamic consequences.

Now connect E C M O circuit & physiology literacy to A R D S physiology. Diffuse inflammatory lung injury producing heterogeneity, shunt, low aerated lung volume and risk from ventilator-induced injury.

The relationship matters because A R D S produces heterogeneous inflammatory lung injury, shunt, edema and a reduced aerated “baby lung,” making regional overdistension possible even at seemingly modest tidal volumes. At the bedside, integrate gas exchange, compliance, recruitability, prone response, driving pressure and hemodynamics rather than treating the P/F ratio alone.
