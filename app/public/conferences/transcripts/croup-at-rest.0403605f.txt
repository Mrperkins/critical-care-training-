Noisy breathing persists while calm
Case conference. Moderate viral croup with stridor at rest. This is a fictional teaching case, with clinical review pending. A 2-year-old child, measured weight 12 kilograms, has a viral prodrome. The child wakes with a barking cough and noisy inspiration. Stridor persists while calm on a caregiver’s lap. EMS finds the child upright on the mother’s lap after a cold is followed by a barking cough overnight. The mother says the noisy inspiration continues even after crying stops. The child can swallow saliva and has no abrupt choking episode. This is a typical clinical croup presentation, caused by inflammatory narrowing below the vocal cords. The caregiver’s lap gives us a useful low-distress observation. Stridor that persists there has a different meaning from a sound heard only while the child is crying.

Assess without adding distress
The child is alert, with inspiratory stridor and moderate sternal recession. There is no drooling, tripod posture or reported choking. The voice is hoarse. The examination is partly about restraint. A normal saturation can coexist with obstruction
Heart rate is 142 per minute. For a two-year-old, the RCH acceptable heart-rate range for unwell children is 95 to 150 beats per minute. This is broader than a healthy resting range; trends and examination still determine severity. This is toward the upper end of the broad unwell-child reference. Distress and the mildly raised temperature can contribute without establishing shock. A distressed toddler may be frightened and tachycardic; poor perfusion would add weak pulses, delayed refill or altered interaction. Respiratory rate is 34 per minute. For a two-year-old, the RCH acceptable respiratory-rate range for unwell children is 20 to 40 breaths per minute. A count in range does not establish adequate ventilation. The count is within this broad reference, yet stridor at rest and recession establish meaningful upper-airway obstruction. Croup commonly adds a barking cough, hoarse voice and inspiratory stridor; these are already documented. Oxygen saturation is 96 percent on room air. At sea level, oxygen saturation is usually 95 to 100 percent in a person without a special oxygen target. Verify signal quality and the oxygen support in use. This is within the usual oxygenation reference. Upper-airway obstruction can be important before desaturation develops. Retractions and stridor at rest can precede cyanosis. A normal saturation does not make these findings mild. Temperature is 38.1 degrees Celsius. Temperature depends on site and time of day; about 36.5 to 37.5 degrees Celsius is a useful reference. A temperature of 38 or more is fever, and below 36 is concerning in an ill patient. This is fever, consistent with the viral prodrome. The modest elevation does not itself identify the pathogen. Viral croup commonly includes coryza and hoarseness. High fever with drooling or toxic appearance would broaden the differential. The oxygen reading is reassuring about current oxygenation. The stridor at rest and recession still tell us that the upper airway is significantly narrowed.

Calm care, treatment, observation
Upper-airway narrowing may be important before oxygen saturation falls. Stridor at rest, recession, behavior and air entry guide severity assessment. Keep the child calm and minimize distressing interventions. Use corticosteroid and nebulized epinephrine when indicated by the clinical pathway. Observe for recurrence and escalate if response is inadequate or the presentation is atypical. The response over time matters. A treatment can reduce symptoms before we know whether that improvement will last, so the observation timeline belongs in the plan.
