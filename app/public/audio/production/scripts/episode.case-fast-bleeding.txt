Now: The negative scan.

eFAST. Structured point-of-care ultrasound for pericardial, intraperitoneal and pleural pathology in trauma.

Build the mechanism first. eFAST uses focused ultrasound windows to look for pericardial, intraperitoneal and pleural pathology. Sensitivity depends on volume, timing, operator technique and where pathology is located.

At the bedside, use a repeatable sweep rather than one frozen image and repeat the exam when physiology changes.

Two traps to actively avoid: Calling one negative scan a permanent rule-out. And: Interpreting an image without proving orientation and anatomy first.

The integration move is this: eFAST is a time-stamped piece of evidence inside trauma physiology, not a substitute for the rest of the evaluation.

Keep these terms available in your mental model: Morison pouch, splenorenal, lung sliding.

Clinical uncertainty & model revision. Expert reasoning explicitly tracks uncertainty, contradictory data and what finding would falsify the current working model.

Build the mechanism first. Clinical decisions are made with incomplete information. Calibrated reasoning assigns provisional probability, makes predictions and updates when new data contradict the model.

At the bedside, state confidence, name disconfirming evidence and define what response you expect from a treatment if the model is correct.

Two traps to actively avoid: Equating uncertainty with indecision. And: Becoming more confident simply because the same hypothesis has been repeated.

The integration move is this: A model that cannot be falsified is not useful clinical reasoning.

Now: Imaging and hemorrhage control move together.

Massive transfusion & hemorrhagic resuscitation. Hemorrhagic resuscitation integrates source control, balanced component replacement, calcium, temperature, coagulation and perfusion.

Build the mechanism first. Major hemorrhage produces loss of oxygen-carrying capacity, volume and hemostatic components while hypothermia, hypocalcemia, dilution and acidosis can worsen coagulation.

At the bedside, activate the hemorrhage system early, warm the resuscitation, track calcium/coagulation/temperature and never lose sight of definitive source control.

Two traps to actively avoid: Running product delivery as a logistics task disconnected from physiology. And: Allowing source control to become secondary to transfusion mechanics.

The integration move is this: Resuscitation buys time; source control ends the hemorrhage.

Keep these terms available in your mental model: M T P, fibrinogen, ionized calcium, lethal triad.

What expert-level use of this concept looks like is this: Run the resuscitation as a system rather than as isolated blood-unit administration. Recognize dilutional/coagulopathic, hypocalcemic and hypothermic deterioration early.

Now connect Massive transfusion & hemorrhagic resuscitation to Critical-illness coagulopathy. Bleeding and thrombosis reflect platelets, fibrinogen, coagulation factors, fibrinolysis, temperature, calcium, dilution and disease-specific pathways.

The relationship matters because Hemostasis depends on platelets, fibrinogen, coagulation factors, fibrinolysis, calcium, pH and temperature. Critical illness can produce simultaneous bleeding and thrombosis risk. At the bedside, interpret conventional labs and viscoelastic tests in the clinical context and replace the failing component rather than one arbitrary number.

Now connect Massive transfusion & hemorrhagic resuscitation to Oxygen delivery. DO2 is cardiac output multiplied by arterial oxygen content; saturation alone does not describe tissue oxygen delivery.

The relationship matters because Oxygen delivery equals cardiac output multiplied by arterial oxygen content, which is dominated by hemoglobin-bound oxygen rather than dissolved oxygen. At the bedside, normal saturation can coexist with critically low delivery in severe anemia or low-flow shock. Ask about hemoglobin and flow whenever tissue perfusion looks worse than the pulse oximeter.
