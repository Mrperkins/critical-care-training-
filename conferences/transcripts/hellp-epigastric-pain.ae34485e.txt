An indigestion story at 35 weeks
Case conference. HELLP syndrome with severe systolic hypertension. This is a fictional teaching case, with clinical review pending. A 30-year-old patient is pregnant at 35 weeks and 1 day. Nausea and upper abdominal pain worsen through the afternoon. The patient initially attributes the symptoms to reflux. There is no external bleeding. EMS is called because presumed indigestion becomes persistent right upper quadrant pain and vomiting. The patient is late in pregnancy, has no known liver disease and has not had trauma. The laboratory pattern turns the complaint into a specific obstetric emergency: hemolysis, elevated liver enzymes and low platelets. The patient’s explanation is understandable: upper abdominal discomfort often sounds gastrointestinal. Our task is to test that explanation against the pregnancy context and the rest of the examination.

The abdominal complaint needs a systemic assessment
Right upper quadrant tenderness and malaise are present. The patient is alert. The team is concerned about a systemic pregnancy complication rather than treating the pain as isolated indigestion. The pain is the opening complaint, but it may be a sign of organ involvement. Ask what else would have to be true for reflux to explain the whole presentation.

Pressure adds concern to the pain story
Heart rate is 112 per minute. A resting adult heart rate is commonly 60 to 100 beats per minute. Pregnancy, pain and medications can shift the baseline. This is tachycardia. Pain and systemic illness can contribute; absence of external bleeding does not exclude a developing complication. Tachycardia may accompany pain, anxiety or reduced circulating volume; weak pulses, cool skin and oliguria would add evidence of poor perfusion. Blood pressure is 162 over 108. In pregnancy, a pressure below 140 over 90 is below the hypertension threshold. Severe hypertension is a systolic pressure of at least 160 or a diastolic pressure of at least 110; either component qualifies. The systolic component alone meets the severe threshold. A diastolic value below 110 does not make this nonsevere. Headache, visual disturbance, seizures or epigastric pain may accompany severe disease; abdominal pain is the presenting feature here. Saturation is 97 percent. At sea level, oxygen saturation is usually 95 to 100 percent in a person without a special oxygen target. Verify signal quality and the oxygen support in use. This is in the usual oxygenation interval; it does not exclude major hematologic or hepatic disease. Hypoxemia can cause restlessness, tachycardia or cyanosis; oxygen saturation alone does not measure perfusion or cardiac output. Fetal heart rate is 155 per minute. The usual fetal baseline heart rate is 110 to 160 beats per minute. Baseline duration, variability and decelerations must also be reviewed. This is in the baseline interval at this assessment. Maternal stabilization and ongoing fetal monitoring remain necessary. Recurrent decelerations or abnormal variability would add concern even before a sustained abnormal baseline develops. The systolic pressure is in a severe range at this assessment. It adds urgency to the symptom pattern and needs prompt repeat assessment and management under the obstetric pathway.

Three abnormalities form a pattern
Platelet count is 62 billion per liter. An adult platelet count is commonly about 150 to 400 times ten to the ninth per liter. In preeclampsia, a count below 100 is a severe-feature threshold. This is marked thrombocytopenia, below 100 and consistent with the low-platelet component of HELLP. Thrombocytopenia may cause petechiae, bruising or mucosal bleeding, but significant depletion can exist without visible bleeding. A S T is 280 units per liter. The adult AST teaching reference is 12 to 38 units per liter; local assay limits vary. More than twice the upper limit is relevant to preeclampsia assessment. This is substantially elevated, indicating hepatocellular injury in the right clinical setting. Liver injury in this setting may accompany right upper quadrant pain, nausea or malaise; severe escalating pain warrants evaluation for hepatic complications. L D H is 920 units per liter. The adult LDH teaching reference is 45 to 200 units per liter, with substantial assay variation. In HELLP, an LDH of at least 600 is commonly used as evidence supporting hemolysis. This is markedly elevated and exceeds the HELLP-supporting threshold. LDH is nonspecific, but schistocytes add evidence of microangiopathic hemolysis. Hemolysis may produce pallor, jaundice or dark urine; their absence would not negate the laboratory pattern. The laboratory reports schistocytes on the blood smear. A normal smear does not contain a pathological excess of fragmented red cells. Schistocytes are red-cell fragments, supporting microangiopathic hemolysis when interpreted with the platelet and enzyme findings. Hemolysis can accompany fatigue, pallor and jaundice; the smear supplies evidence even before these become prominent. The low platelets, liver enzyme elevation and evidence of hemolysis turn an apparently isolated complaint into a multisystem problem. Interpret the pattern together rather than treating each result as a separate issue.

HELLP changes the urgency
Hemolysis, elevated liver enzymes and low platelets support HELLP syndrome in context. Severe or escalating abdominal pain also raises concern for hepatic complications requiring urgent specialist assessment.  Naming HELLP explains why the pain cannot simply be observed as indigestion. The intensity and course of pain remain important because complications may change the next definitive action.

Escalate for systemic obstetric disease
The platelet count and evidence of liver injury and hemolysis change the meaning of an apparently gastrointestinal complaint. Obstetric evaluation must accompany resuscitation. Escalate immediately to obstetric and critical care teams and coordinate definitive care. Monitor for bleeding, neurological change and worsening abdominal pain; repeat laboratories as directed. Treat associated severe hypertension and provide indicated seizure prophylaxis under the obstetric pathway. The clinical plan needs to anticipate more than the presenting symptom. Laboratory trajectory, bleeding risk and the potential for rapid change all influence the destination and the resources required.

The trend belongs in the handoff
The receiving team arranges urgent obstetric management. Serial symptoms and laboratory values are communicated, with preparation for hemorrhage and blood products if required. The team treats the severe systolic pressure, gives indicated magnesium and arranges urgent delivery after maternal stabilization with obstetric, anesthesia and blood-bank support. Serial blood counts and coagulation studies guide hemorrhage preparation. In this synthetic course, platelet count initially falls further after delivery before recovering with clinical stabilization; liver tests then improve. Persistent or escalating pain would require evaluation for hepatic hematoma or rupture rather than attributing it to routine HELLP discomfort. A single panel establishes the concern; serial values help describe its pace. The receiving team needs both the most recent results and how quickly the symptoms and abnormalities have developed.

HELLP syndrome with severe systolic hypertension

HELLP required prompt specialist management. The useful move was revisiting the original explanation when the evidence stopped fitting.
