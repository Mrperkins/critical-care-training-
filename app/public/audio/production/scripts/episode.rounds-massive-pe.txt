Now: Pulmonary vascular obstruction.

Pulmonary embolism physiology. Acute pulmonary vascular obstruction can abruptly raise RV afterload, impair LV filling and cause shock despite structurally normal lungs.

Build the mechanism first. Large pulmonary embolic obstruction abruptly raises RV afterload, causing RV dilation, reduced LV filling and potential obstructive shock; gas exchange often reflects increased dead space.

At the bedside, look for RV strain, low-flow signs and widening PaCO2–EtCO2 gap; be cautious with induction and excessive positive pressure in a preload- and RV-dependent circulation.

Two traps to actively avoid: Assuming clear lungs mean respiratory physiology is benign. And: Intubating without preparing for hemodynamic collapse.

The integration move is this: Massive PE is primarily a circulation problem expressed through the pulmonary vasculature.

Keep these terms available in your mental model: obstructive shock, RV strain, dead space.

What expert-level use of this concept looks like is this: Connect clot burden to RV pressure load, septal interaction and gas-exchange findings. Recognize why intubation and excessive positive pressure may destabilize severe PE.

Pulmonary vascular resistance. Resistance faced by the RV, influenced by lung volume, oxygenation, CO2, pH, pulmonary vascular disease and mechanical ventilation.

Build the mechanism first. Pulmonary vascular resistance reflects the combined behavior of alveolar and extra-alveolar vessels and is affected by lung volume, oxygen, CO2, pH and pulmonary vascular disease.

At the bedside, both severe derecruitment and overdistension can raise RV afterload. Hypoxemia, hypercapnia and acidosis can compound the problem.

Two traps to actively avoid: Assuming more peep always lowers P V R by recruiting lung. And: Ignoring gas chemistry as an RV afterload intervention.

The integration move is this: Interpret ventilator changes through both lung mechanics and the pulmonary circulation.

Keep these terms available in your mental model: RV afterload, West zones.

What expert-level use of this concept looks like is this: Explain the U-shaped relationship between lung volume and pulmonary vascular resistance. Identify reversible contributors to acute RV afterload.

Tie this back to the patient. Ask what variable is changing, what downstream effect you expect, and what bedside finding would support or contradict that model.

Now: Why the RV fails abruptly.

Right ventricular failure. Failure of a thin-walled ventricle that is highly sensitive to acute afterload, volume, perfusion pressure and ventricular interaction.

Build the mechanism first. The RV is thin-walled and highly sensitive to acute afterload. When it dilates, wall stress rises, coronary perfusion can fall and septal shift can reduce LV filling.

At the bedside, track systemic pressure, venous congestion, echo geometry, oxygenation, CO2, acid-base status and ventilator pressures together. Small changes in P V R or intrathoracic pressure can have large hemodynamic consequences.

Two traps to actively avoid: Reflexively giving fluid to every hypotensive RV patient. And: Focusing on systemic pressure while ignoring worsening RV dilation and congestion.

The integration move is this: Every intervention should be judged by its effects on RV preload, RV afterload, contractility and coronary perfusion.

Keep these terms available in your mental model: RV-PA coupling, septal shift, transmural pressure.

What expert-level use of this concept looks like is this: Recognize RV shock physiology before systemic hypotension becomes profound. Predict how peep, hypercapnia, hypoxemia, fluids and vasoactives may change RV performance.

Tie this back to the patient. Ask what variable is changing, what downstream effect you expect, and what bedside finding would support or contradict that model.

Now: Dead space and the gas-exchange clues.

Dead space. Ventilation that does not effectively participate in CO2 exchange, from conducting airways or ventilated but poorly perfused alveoli.

Build the mechanism first. Dead-space ventilation reaches regions that do not effectively eliminate CO2 because perfusion is absent or inadequate, or because gas remains in conducting airways.

At the bedside, rising minute-ventilation requirement, widening PaCO2–EtCO2 gap or unexpectedly low EtCO2 can signal increasing dead space when ventilation is otherwise unchanged.

Two traps to actively avoid: Equating all hypercapnia with low minute ventilation. And: Using EtCO2 as a fixed conversion to PaCO2 in shock.

The integration move is this: CO2 is a ventilation-perfusion signal as well as a ventilation signal.

Keep these terms available in your mental model: VD/VT, alveolar dead space.

What expert-level use of this concept looks like is this: Use PaCO2–EtCO2 behavior and minute ventilation requirements to reason about changing dead space.

Tie this back to the patient. Ask what variable is changing, what downstream effect you expect, and what bedside finding would support or contradict that model.

Now: Why intubation can be dangerous.

Positive-pressure ventilation & circulation. Positive intrathoracic pressure changes venous return, ventricular transmural pressures and pulmonary vascular resistance.

Build the mechanism first. Positive intrathoracic pressure can reduce the gradient for venous return, change transmural ventricular pressures and alter P V R depending on lung volume and recruitability.

At the bedside, post-intubation hypotension may reflect vasodilation, reduced venous return, increased RV afterload, auto-peep or previously compensated shock becoming unmasked.

Two traps to actively avoid: Attributing every post-intubation pressure drop to induction medication. And: Assuming positive pressure has a single predictable effect on both ventricles.

The integration move is this: Think preload, RV afterload and LV transmural afterload separately.

Keep these terms available in your mental model: transmural pressure, mean airway pressure.

What expert-level use of this concept looks like is this: Predict why induction and positive pressure can precipitate hypotension. Distinguish effects on RV preload, RV afterload and LV transmural afterload.
