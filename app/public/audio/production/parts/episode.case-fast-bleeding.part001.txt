eFAST. Structured point-of-care ultrasound for pericardial, intraperitoneal and pleural pathology in trauma.

Build the mechanism first. eFAST uses focused ultrasound windows to look for pericardial, intraperitoneal and pleural pathology. Sensitivity depends on volume, timing, operator technique and where pathology is located.

At the bedside, use a repeatable sweep rather than one frozen image and repeat the exam when physiology changes.

Two traps to actively avoid: Calling one negative scan a permanent rule-out. And: Interpreting an image without proving orientation and anatomy first.

The integration move is this: eFAST is a time-stamped piece of evidence inside trauma physiology, not a substitute for the rest of the evaluation.

Keep these terms available in your mental model: Morison pouch, splenorenal, lung sliding.

Now connect eFAST to Pneumothorax ultrasound. Pleural ultrasound combines sliding, B-lines, M-mode patterns and lung point rather than relying on a single sign.

The relationship matters because Pleural ultrasound infers apposition or separation of pleural layers through sliding, B-lines, lung pulse, M-mode patterns and lung point. At the bedside, absent sliding raises suspicion but is nonspecific; lung point is more specific when present in the appropriate context.

Now connect eFAST to Hemoperitoneum on ultrasound. Free intraperitoneal fluid is sought in dependent spaces, but sensitivity depends on volume, timing, clot and the location of bleeding.

The relationship matters because Free intraperitoneal blood accumulates in dependent spaces, but early volume, clot, body habitus and retroperitoneal bleeding can limit ultrasound detection. At the bedside, sweep RUQ, LUQ and pelvis in more than one view and repeat when physiology changes.
