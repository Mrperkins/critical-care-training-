Now: Parent compound versus toxic metabolite.

Toxic alcohols. Methanol and ethylene glycol toxicity evolve through parent-compound intoxication, toxic metabolites, anion-gap acidosis and end-organ injury.

Build the mechanism first. Methanol and ethylene glycol parent compounds create early osmolar burden while toxic metabolites later produce high-anion-gap acidosis and organ-specific injury.

At the bedside, use time course, osmolar gap, anion gap, visual/renal findings and acid-base state together; antidotal blockade and dialysis solve different kinetic problems.

Two traps to actively avoid: Waiting for a confirmatory level when physiology strongly supports toxicity. And: Assuming a normal osmolar gap late in presentation is reassuring.

The integration move is this: As parent alcohol is metabolized, the osmolar gap can fall while toxicity worsens.

Keep these terms available in your mental model: osmolar gap, anion gap, formate, oxalate.

What expert-level use of this concept looks like is this: Use timing, gaps and organ findings together rather than waiting for a confirmatory level. Understand the roles of alcohol dehydrogenase blockade and extracorporeal removal.

Tie this back to the patient. Ask what variable is changing, what downstream effect you expect, and what bedside finding would support or contradict that model.

Now: The gaps change with time.

Advanced acid-base reasoning. Integrate respiratory and metabolic processes, compensation, anion gap, albumin, chloride and mixed disorders.

Build the mechanism first. pH reflects interacting respiratory and metabolic processes. Compensation has expected patterns, so deviations can expose mixed disorders that a single pH label hides.

At the bedside, calculate the anion gap, correct for relevant confounders, compare compensation and consider chloride, albumin and lactate in context.

Two traps to actively avoid: Calling a normal pH normal acid-base physiology. And: Treating bicarbonate concentration as if it identifies the mechanism by itself.

The integration move is this: Acid-base analysis should lead back to the disease process causing the disturbance.

Keep these terms available in your mental model: delta gap, expected compensation.

What expert-level use of this concept looks like is this: Identify mixed disorders that a single pH label hides. Connect acid-base abnormalities to the underlying physiology and treatment.

Now: When extracorporeal removal matters.

C R R T physiology & prescription literacy. Continuous renal replacement uses diffusion and/or convection to manage solute, acid-base and fluid balance in unstable patients.

Build the mechanism first. C R R T uses diffusion, convection and ultrafiltration to control solute, acid-base and volume continuously. Prescribed dose can differ substantially from delivered dose.

At the bedside, separate total effluent from net patient fluid removal and interpret access, filter and return pressures as clues to the physical circuit.

Two traps to actively avoid: Calling every effluent liter “fluid removed from the patient.” And: Ignoring downtime and clotting when assessing delivered therapy.

The integration move is this: C R R T is both renal support and a hemodynamic intervention.

Keep these terms available in your mental model: effluent dose, CVVH, CVVHD, CVVHDF, filtration fraction.

What expert-level use of this concept looks like is this: Explain diffusion versus convection and net ultrafiltration. Recognize how circuit, anticoagulation and prescribed versus delivered dose change therapy.

Tie this back to the patient. Ask what variable is changing, what downstream effect you expect, and what bedside finding would support or contradict that model.
