Now: When intracranial compliance runs out.

ICP, CPP & cerebral perfusion. Cerebral perfusion depends on the gradient across the cerebral circulation and is influenced by pressure, venous outflow, CO2 and intracranial compliance.

Build the mechanism first. CPP is the pressure gradient available to drive cerebral blood flow and is influenced by arterial pressure, ICP, venous pressure and autoregulation. Intracranial compliance falls as compensatory reserve is exhausted.

At the bedside, interpret ICP as a dynamic system with waveform, position, venous drainage, CO2 and sedation effects. A “normal” MAP does not guarantee adequate cerebral perfusion.

Two traps to actively avoid: Treating ICP and CPP as isolated targets. And: Ignoring neck position, intrathoracic pressure and venous outflow.

The integration move is this: Protect the brain by preserving oxygenation and perfusion while minimizing factors that increase intracranial volume or venous pressure.

Keep these terms available in your mental model: CPP, autoregulation, Monro-Kellie.

What expert-level use of this concept looks like is this: Predict how systemic pressure, CO2 and venous pressure affect cerebral perfusion. Recognize when a normal MAP does not guarantee adequate CPP.

Tie this back to the patient. Ask what variable is changing, what downstream effect you expect, and what bedside finding would support or contradict that model.

Now: Pressure gradients move tissue.

Brain herniation physiology. Rising intracranial pressure and compartment shifts distort brain structures, impair perfusion and can produce rapidly evolving focal and brainstem signs.

Build the mechanism first. When intracranial pressure gradients exceed compensatory reserve, brain tissue shifts across fixed boundaries, compressing cranial nerves, vessels and brainstem structures.

At the bedside, recognize trajectory—declining consciousness, pupillary asymmetry, motor changes and evolving brainstem physiology—while protecting oxygenation and cerebral perfusion.

Two traps to actively avoid: Waiting for the complete Cushing response. And: Aggressively lowering systemic pressure in a patient whose CPP is already threatened.

The integration move is this: Temporizing therapy buys time for definitive imaging/neurosurgical treatment; it does not solve the underlying lesion.

Keep these terms available in your mental model: uncal, central, tonsillar, Cushing response.

What expert-level use of this concept looks like is this: Recognize a herniation trajectory from examination and physiology. Prioritize temporizing actions while preserving cerebral perfusion and definitive neurosurgical access.

Clinical uncertainty & model revision. Expert reasoning explicitly tracks uncertainty, contradictory data and what finding would falsify the current working model.

Build the mechanism first. Clinical decisions are made with incomplete information. Calibrated reasoning assigns provisional probability, makes predictions and updates when new data contradict the model.

At the bedside, state confidence, name disconfirming evidence and define what response you expect from a treatment if the model is correct.

Two traps to actively avoid: Equating uncertainty with indecision. And: Becoming more confident simply because the same hypothesis has been repeated.

The integration move is this: A model that cannot be falsified is not useful clinical reasoning.

Keep these terms available in your mental model: calibration, falsification, diagnostic time-out.

What expert-level use of this concept looks like is this: State confidence, identify disconfirming evidence and update the model when the patient fails to respond as predicted.
