The child who can only say one word
Case conference. The child who can only say one word. This is a fictional teaching case, with clinical review pending. An 8-year-old child, measured weight 26 kilograms, has asthma and a previous intensive care admission. A viral illness preceded rapidly worsening dyspnea. Home albuterol brought little relief. The child arrives sitting forward, speaking one word per breath. A caregiver reports no choking episode or allergen exposure.

What do you notice?
At the bedside. The child is frightened and alert, with intercostal recession, prolonged expiration and diffuse wheeze. Air entry is reduced at both bases. The trachea is central and pulses are strong.

Vital signs
The initial vital signs are as follows. Heart rate is 148 per minute. Blood pressure is 110 over 68. Respiratory rate is 38 per minute. Oxygen saturation is 90 percent on room air. Temperature is 37.6 degrees Celsius. These are observations at this point in the case, rather than treatment targets.

Laboratory findings
Laboratory findings. Venous blood pH is 7.34. Venous carbon dioxide is 43 millimeters of mercury; this is not an arterial oxygen measurement. Potassium is 3.4 millimoles per liter. Interpret these results alongside the examination and the timing of sampling.

Diagnostic findings
Diagnostic findings. The clinical pattern favors an acute severe asthma exacerbation. A chest radiograph is not routinely required for a typical presentation. Imaging becomes relevant if asymmetry, fever with focal findings, or a complication changes the differential. In visual mode, an optional real reference image can be opened. It belongs to a different patient and is not the scan from this fictional case.

What would you prioritize?
Think it through. Does loud wheeze tell you how close the child is to respiratory failure? What would you trend during the first treatment cycle? Consider your answer while the case continues. No screen interaction is needed.

Clinical reasoning & priorities
Conference discussion. Wheeze requires airflow. Severity depends on work of breathing, air entry, mental status and response to treatment. An apparently reassuring carbon dioxide value does not overrule a tiring child. Priorities. 1. Give oxygen and repeated inhaled bronchodilator therapy with an effective interface; add indicated adjuncts under the pediatric protocol. 2. Reassess air entry, speech, effort and alertness after each treatment; check drug delivery before calling treatment ineffective. 3. Prepare escalation and pediatric critical care consultation while preserving spontaneous ventilation when feasible.

Reassessment & handoff
Reassessment and handoff. After initial treatment the child speaks short sentences, respiratory rate falls to 30, and saturation reaches 95 percent on supplemental oxygen. Continued monitoring and reassessment guide the transfer; a single improvement does not establish sustained stability. This is an authored fictional course; improvement is not guaranteed in a real patient.

Pediatric status asthmaticus: Severe obstruction with preserved alertness
Case synopsis. Pediatric status asthmaticus. Severe obstruction with preserved alertness. A child with severe asthma remained alert but had marked obstruction. The useful trend was better air movement, speech and effort. Wheeze volume alone was an unreliable severity measure. This concludes the case conference.
