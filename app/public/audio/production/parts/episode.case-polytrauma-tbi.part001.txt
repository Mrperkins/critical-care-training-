Now: Two life threats, competing priorities.

Severe traumatic brain injury. TBI management centers on preventing secondary brain injury by protecting oxygenation, perfusion and intracranial dynamics.

Build the mechanism first. Primary injury is followed by potentially preventable secondary injury from hypoxemia, hypotension, impaired cerebral perfusion, edema and temperature/metabolic stress.

At the bedside, prioritize oxygenation and perfusion while integrating ICP/CPP with extracranial hemorrhage and ventilation needs.

Two traps to actively avoid: Permissive hypotension without considering severe TBI. And: Overventilating chronically after an acute herniation concern has passed.

The integration move is this: Trauma resuscitation sometimes contains competing pressure goals; make the tradeoff explicit.

Keep these terms available in your mental model: secondary injury, CPP, intracranial compliance.

Now connect Severe traumatic brain injury to Brain herniation physiology. Rising intracranial pressure and compartment shifts distort brain structures, impair perfusion and can produce rapidly evolving focal and brainstem signs.

The relationship matters because When intracranial pressure gradients exceed compensatory reserve, brain tissue shifts across fixed boundaries, compressing cranial nerves, vessels and brainstem structures. At the bedside, recognize trajectory—declining consciousness, pupillary asymmetry, motor changes and evolving brainstem physiology—while protecting oxygenation and cerebral perfusion.

Now connect Severe traumatic brain injury to Critical-illness coagulopathy. Bleeding and thrombosis reflect platelets, fibrinogen, coagulation factors, fibrinolysis, temperature, calcium, dilution and disease-specific pathways.

The relationship matters because Hemostasis depends on platelets, fibrinogen, coagulation factors, fibrinolysis, calcium, pH and temperature. Critical illness can produce simultaneous bleeding and thrombosis risk. At the bedside, interpret conventional labs and viscoelastic tests in the clinical context and replace the failing component rather than one arbitrary number.
