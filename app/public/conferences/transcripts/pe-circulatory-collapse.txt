Syncope followed by persistent shock
Case conference. Syncope followed by shock. This is a fictional teaching case, with clinical review pending. A 57-year-old patient develops sudden dyspnea and collapses briefly. On recovery the patient remains confused and hypotensive. There is no reported major trauma or external bleeding. Recovery of consciousness after a brief collapse does not mean the event is over. The persistent shock tells us that the underlying physiological problem remains active.

A possible obstructive circulation problem
There are weak pulses, cool extremities and elevated neck veins. The lungs are relatively clear. The team considers obstructive, cardiac and other shock causes. The pattern points us toward a circulation-focused assessment. It is suggestive, but it is not specific enough to replace a careful evaluation of competing causes of shock.

Profound instability changes diagnostic logistics
Heart rate is 138 per minute. Blood pressure is 70 over 42. Respiratory rate is 36 per minute. Saturation is 84 percent on supplemental oxygen. These observations affect where testing can safely occur. Moving the patient for confirmation is itself a clinical decision with risks, not a neutral step on a checklist.

Severe perfusion stress adds urgency
Lactate is 7.2 millimoles per liter. Arterial pH is 7.27. The laboratory pattern agrees with the bedside assessment. It helps describe severity while we organize support and an urgent diagnostic strategy that the patient can tolerate.

RV dysfunction supports concern, not certainty
Bedside echocardiography shows a dilated, poorly functioning right ventricle. This supports a shock assessment but is not specific for acute pulmonary embolism. The instability may limit safe transport to CT before initial resuscitation. The echo is a piece of evidence in an unstable patient. Communicate both what it shows and what it cannot prove, because definitive treatment decisions depend on that distinction.

What is the cost of confirmation away from resuscitation?
What is the risk of taking a profoundly unstable patient away for confirmatory imaging? Imagine the patient deteriorating in the scanner corridor. What support, evidence and shared decision would you want in place before committing to that movement?

Support the circulation and define a definitive pathway
Shock requires immediate support and an urgent diagnostic and reperfusion strategy. Airway induction and positive pressure can further destabilize right-ventricular failure. Activate critical care and the pulmonary embolism response team for an urgent individualized plan. Support circulation and oxygenation, anticipating the hemodynamic risk of airway interventions. Coordinate bedside diagnostics and reperfusion decisions with explicit assessment of contraindications and alternative causes. Airway induction is a major physiological event in a failing right ventricle. The respiratory and circulatory plans should be coordinated before an intervention that could abruptly change preload and pressure.

Separate observations from the working diagnosis
The receiving team selects a definitive pathway based on the evolving evidence and risk. The handoff distinguishes confirmed observations from the working diagnosis. A clear handoff can be urgent without being overconfident. State the confirmed shock and RV findings, the suspected cause and the decisions made from the available evidence.

Suspected high-risk PE: stabilize while defining the pathway
Case synopsis. The suspected embolism presented as circulatory collapse. Stabilization, bedside assessment and specialist coordination had to precede any unsafe diagnostic transport. The most important step was matching the diagnostic plan to the patient’s ability to tolerate it while support and expert coordination continued.
