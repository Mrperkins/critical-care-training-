Now: Vasoplegia and venous capacitance.

Septic shock physiology. Distributive shock with dynamic changes in vascular tone, venous capacitance, myocardial performance, microcirculation and oxygen extraction.

Build the mechanism first. Septic shock can combine vasoplegia, venous pooling, myocardial dysfunction, endothelial leak and disturbed microcirculation. Cardiac output may be low, normal or high.

At the bedside, reassess vascular tone, flow, congestion and perfusion after each intervention rather than assuming a fixed distributive phenotype.

Two traps to actively avoid: Reducing sepsis to “low S V R.” And: Using lactate or MAP alone as the resuscitation target.

The integration move is this: Source control, antimicrobial exposure and circulatory support must progress in parallel.

Keep these terms available in your mental model: vasoplegia, microcirculation, distributive shock.

What expert-level use of this concept looks like is this: Recognize when septic shock is not simply low S V R. Integrate source control, flow, tone, cardiac function and perfusion response over time.

Now connect Septic shock physiology to Source control. Definitive management of infected material, devitalized tissue or an infected device can be as important as antimicrobial therapy.

The relationship matters because Antibiotics cannot sterilize every infected collection, necrotic tissue or infected device. Persistent nidus can sustain inflammatory and microbial burden despite apparently appropriate antimicrobial therapy. At the bedside, ask early whether drainage, debridement, device removal or surgery is required and whether it is actually happening.

Now connect Septic shock physiology to Lactate physiology. Lactate reflects production and clearance across multiple pathways and is not a direct tissue-hypoxia meter.

The relationship matters because Lactate rises from multiple pathways including adrenergic glycolysis, impaired clearance and cellular metabolic changes; it is not a direct meter of tissue hypoxia. At the bedside, trend lactate with perfusion, liver function, catecholamine exposure and clinical trajectory. A falling value can be reassuring but should not override a deteriorating patient.

Stress-test the model. Imagine the patient changes in a way that makes reducing sepsis to “low svr.” tempting. Before acting, state the mechanism you think is operating, predict what your intervention should change, and name the finding that would make you stop and reconsider. Then run the same exercise against this second trap: Using lactate or MAP alone as the resuscitation target.

Vasopressor & inotrope physiology. Vasoactive drugs change arterial tone, venous tone, heart rate, contractility and regional flow; the desired effect depends on the shock model.

Build the mechanism first. Vasoactive drugs alter arterial tone, venous tone, contractility and heart rate to different degrees. The same MAP response can hide very different effects on flow and myocardial demand.

At the bedside, choose the agent from the dominant deficit and define what improvement and toxicity should look like before titration.
