Confusion without fever
Case conference. Confusion without fever. This is a fictional teaching case, with clinical review pending. A 79-year-old patient has dysuria followed by new confusion. The family reports reduced intake and urine output. The patient is cold rather than febrile. The initial handoff describes “weakness,” but the examination suggests a systemic illness.

What do you notice?
At the bedside. There is altered attention, mottling and delayed refill. The team evaluates infection and other causes of shock and delirium.

Vital signs
The initial vital signs are as follows. Heart rate is 114 per minute. Blood pressure is 82 over 46. Respiratory rate is 28 per minute. Temperature is 35.3 degrees Celsius. These are observations at this point in the case, rather than treatment targets.

Laboratory findings
Laboratory findings. Lactate is 5.1 millimoles per liter. Creatinine is 2.2 milligrams per deciliter, compared with a baseline of 0.9. Urinalysis shows pyuria, interpreted with symptoms and the broader assessment. Interpret these results alongside the examination and the timing of sampling.

Diagnostic findings
Diagnostic findings. Suspected infection, organ dysfunction and poor perfusion prompt immediate sepsis management. The team avoids declaring a definitive urinary source from urinalysis alone.

What would you prioritize?
Think it through. Does the absence of fever make sepsis unlikely enough to delay treatment? Consider your answer while the case continues. No screen interaction is needed.

Clinical reasoning & priorities
Conference discussion. Older adults may present with confusion or hypothermia. The source remains a working diagnosis while resuscitation and evaluation proceed. Priorities. 1. Deliver timely antimicrobials and obtain appropriate cultures without avoidable treatment delay. 2. Resuscitate and assess fluid responsiveness and tolerance; begin indicated vasoactive support under protocol. 3. Evaluate source control needs and alternative causes of shock.

Reassessment & handoff
Reassessment and handoff. After initial treatment, the team repeats perfusion assessment and reviews the pressure and urine trend. An improving number does not remove the need for source evaluation. This is an authored fictional course; improvement is not guaranteed in a real patient.
