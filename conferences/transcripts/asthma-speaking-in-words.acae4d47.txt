One word per breath
Case conference. Acute severe asthma exacerbation with hypoxemia. This is a fictional teaching case, with clinical review pending. An 8-year-old child, measured weight 26 kilograms, has asthma and a previous intensive care admission. A viral illness preceded rapidly worsening dyspnea. Home albuterol brought little relief. The child arrives sitting forward, speaking one word per breath. A caregiver reports no choking episode or allergen exposure. EMS brings this child from home to the emergency department. The mother says the usual rescue inhaler has stopped giving lasting relief since a cold began yesterday. The crew found bilateral wheeze, gave inhaled albuterol with oxygen, and reports only brief improvement in speech. There is no urticaria, facial swelling or sudden choking event. We are presenting severe asthma, not an unexplained wheezing syndrome. Before looking at a gas, listen to how much of a sentence this child can manage. One word per breath tells us that ordinary conversation is competing with the work of breathing.

Alert does not mean comfortable
The child is frightened and alert, with intercostal recession, prolonged expiration and diffuse wheeze. Air entry is reduced at both bases. The trachea is central and pulses are strong. Imagine kneeling beside the bed rather than talking over the child. You can follow air entry, effort and interaction together. The loudness of the wheeze is only one part of that picture.

A strained respiratory system
Heart rate is 148 per minute. For an eight-year-old, the RCH acceptable heart-rate range for unwell children is 70 to 130 beats per minute. This is broader than a healthy resting range; trends and examination still determine severity. This is tachycardia. Distress, hypoxemia and albuterol can all contribute; the rate alone cannot separate them. A fast pulse may accompany agitation, tremor or palpitations; poor perfusion would add concern rather than being assumed from the rate. Blood pressure is 110 over 68. For an eight-year-old, the RCH acceptable systolic-pressure range for unwell children is 80 to 115 millimeters of mercury. It is an observation reference, not a shock rule-out threshold. The systolic pressure is within this reference. Strong pulses fit preserved perfusion at this assessment, while the respiratory problem remains severe. Shock would commonly add weak pulses, delayed refill, cool extremities or altered interaction; we are actively checking for those changes. Respiratory rate is 38 per minute. For an eight-year-old, the RCH acceptable respiratory-rate range for unwell children is 16 to 30 breaths per minute. A count in range does not establish adequate ventilation. This is tachypnea. The prolonged expiratory phase, recession and one-word speech connect the high count to lower-airway obstruction. Associated signs include accessory-muscle use, inability to speak normally and fatigue. This child already has the first two. Oxygen saturation is 90 percent on room air. At sea level, oxygen saturation is usually 95 to 100 percent in a person without a special oxygen target. Verify signal quality and the oxygen support in use. This is hypoxemia on room air. Obstructed lung units are ventilated unevenly, reducing effective oxygen transfer. Restlessness and tachycardia may precede cyanosis; absence of visible cyanosis would not make this saturation normal. Temperature is 37.6 degrees Celsius. Temperature depends on site and time of day; about 36.5 to 37.5 degrees Celsius is a useful reference. A temperature of 38 or more is fever, and below 36 is concerning in an ill patient. This reading is slightly above the teaching interval but below the fever threshold. A viral trigger is plausible; this value does not establish bacterial pneumonia. Fever may cause flushing or chills. New focal crackles or a sustained high fever would change the infection assessment. Read these observations as a group: rapid breathing, tachycardia and hypoxemia in a child who is still working hard. Preserved pressure does not reduce the urgency of the breathing problem.

Does the gas fit the child?
Venous blood pH is 7.34. This pediatric peripheral venous pH reference is 7.30 to 7.40. Venous and arterial samples must be identified separately. This venous pH is in the stated range. It does not show severe acidemia, but it also does not measure how much respiratory reserve remains. Worsening ventilatory failure can add drowsiness and shallow breathing; those bedside changes outrank a previously acceptable pH. Venous carbon dioxide is 43 millimeters of mercury; this is not an arterial oxygen measurement. This laboratory lists pediatric peripheral venous carbon dioxide from 32 to 45 for girls and 35 to 48 for boys, in millimeters of mercury. It cannot measure arterial oxygenation. This value is within the cited peripheral venous ranges. A severely distressed child may initially lower carbon dioxide; a normal-looking value with worsening effort can therefore be concerning. Carbon dioxide retention may be accompanied by headache, confusion or drowsiness. None is inferred merely from this single venous value. Potassium is 3.4 millimoles per liter. The serum potassium teaching reference is 3.5 to 5.0 millimoles per liter; use the reporting laboratory and exclude sample hemolysis. This is mild hypokalemia. Beta agonists can shift potassium into cells, so repeated treatment makes the trend relevant. More marked hypokalemia can cause weakness, cramps, ectopy and flattened T waves; those are associations to monitor, not documented findings here. The temptation is to find one value that sounds reassuring and relax. Here the bedside trajectory has to carry more weight than a carbon dioxide value that seems unremarkable in isolation.

A clinical diagnosis first
The clinical pattern favors an acute severe asthma exacerbation. A chest radiograph is not routinely required for a typical presentation. Imaging becomes relevant if asymmetry, fever with focal findings, or a complication changes the differential. The real comparison radiograph comes from a different child with near-fatal asthma. Look at the expanded dark lung fields and low flattened diaphragms, appearances of hyperinflation from trapped gas. A normal film would have less expansion and more domed diaphragms. For an audio-only listener, imagine air getting in more easily than it gets out through narrowed bronchi. Hyperinflation is not specific to asthma, and a radiograph is not needed to recognize this child’s typical exacerbation. A test should answer a question. If the story becomes asymmetric or a complication is suspected, the imaging question changes. A routine film should not become a detour from effective treatment.

Treat, then return to the bedside
Wheeze requires airflow. Severity depends on work of breathing, air entry, mental status and response to treatment. An apparently reassuring carbon dioxide value does not overrule a tiring child. Give oxygen and repeated inhaled bronchodilator therapy with an effective interface; add indicated adjuncts under the pediatric protocol. Reassess air entry, speech, effort and alertness after each treatment; check drug delivery before calling treatment ineffective. Prepare escalation and pediatric critical care consultation while preserving spontaneous ventilation when feasible. Think of the first treatment as both therapy and a chance to learn. If the response is poor, check whether the medication is actually being delivered before concluding that the disease is unresponsive.

A coherent improvement
After initial treatment the child speaks short sentences, respiratory rate falls to 30, and saturation reaches 95 percent on supplemental oxygen. Continued monitoring and reassessment guide the transfer; a single improvement does not establish sustained stability. In the emergency department, oxygen, repeated albuterol with initial ipratropium, and an early systemic corticosteroid are delivered under the pediatric asthma pathway. Staff confirm that the mask fits and that medication is actually reaching the child. Because substantial distress persists after the initial cycle, pediatric critical care is consulted and intravenous magnesium is considered under that pathway. With improved speech and air entry, the child is admitted for continued bronchodilators and monitoring rather than discharged after one better saturation. In this synthetic course, there is no intubation. Before discharge after sustained improvement, the family demonstrates spacer technique and receives a controller review and a written action plan. The important feature is that several bedside findings move in the same direction. Better saturation alone would be less persuasive if the child were becoming less interactive or moving less air.

Acute severe asthma exacerbation with hypoxemia
Case synopsis. Acute severe asthma exacerbation with hypoxemia. A child with severe asthma remained alert but had marked obstruction. The useful trend was better air movement, speech and effort. Wheeze volume alone was an unreliable severity measure. Carry this into the disease lesson: severity and response are patterns over time. A noisy chest can be very sick, and a quieter chest needs a fresh examination rather than an automatic label of improvement.
