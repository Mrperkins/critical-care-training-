Breathing interrupts feeding
Case conference. Too breathless to feed. This is a fictional teaching case, with clinical review pending. A 5-month-old infant, measured weight 6.4 kilograms, was born at term. After three days of coryza, the infant has increasing cough, reduced feeds and fewer wet diapers. There has been no witnessed apnea. For an infant, a feeding history is also a functional respiratory history. Ask the caregiver what happens when the bottle or breast is offered, and how that compares with yesterday.

Effort and intake are linked
The infant is alert with subcostal recession, diffuse crackles and wheeze, and copious nasal secretions. Feeding attempts increase distress. The child has to coordinate breathing with feeding. When that ordinary task becomes difficult, it adds information that a spot saturation cannot capture.

Persistent hypoxemia with rapid breathing
Heart rate is 164 per minute. Respiratory rate is 66 per minute. Oxygen saturation is persistently 88 percent on room air. Temperature is 37.9 degrees Celsius. The saturation needs attention, and so does the sustained effort. An oxygen response will help one part of the problem while we continue to assess fatigue and the ability to hydrate safely.

Testing follows a clinical question
A bedside glucose is 86 milligrams per deciliter. Routine blood tests have not been obtained because they would not currently change management. The absence of a routine blood panel is a deliberate decision here. A test earns its place when its result would change the next step, particularly in a distressed infant.

A clinical bronchiolitis pattern
This is a clinical presentation of bronchiolitis. Routine chest radiographs and viral testing are not necessary for every typical case; testing depends on the clinical question and setting. The wheeze can tempt us to borrow an asthma treatment story. Start instead with the infant’s age, course and overall pattern, and build supportive care around the problems actually present.

What does the feeding history change?
What does the inability to feed add to the respiratory assessment? Imagine the oxygen number improves but the infant still cannot feed without distress. Would the overall problem be solved? Keep that question for the reassessment.

Support breathing and hydration together
Respiratory distress can compromise both ventilation and hydration. Supportive care and reassessment are central; wheeze in an infant does not automatically imply asthma. Support oxygenation and clear obstructing nasal secretions when needed. Plan hydration that the infant can tolerate safely. Escalate respiratory support for persistent distress, hypoxemia, apnea or fatigue under the local pediatric pathway. A useful plan describes how we will know supportive care is enough. That means naming the signs that would trigger a change in respiratory support, rather than only naming a saturation goal.

The saturation improves first
With support, saturation reaches 94 percent, but respiratory rate remains high. The team continues to assess effort and hydration rather than declaring success from saturation alone. This is a real improvement in one domain. The continuing respiratory rate and feeding problem explain why the team keeps watching and adjusting rather than closing the case.

Bronchiolitis: breathing and feeding are one story
Case synopsis. The infant needed assessment of breathing and feeding together. A better saturation did not erase the ongoing work of breathing or hydration problem. The caregiver’s account of feeding was central evidence. Carry it forward alongside the respiratory examination when you decide whether an infant is improving.
