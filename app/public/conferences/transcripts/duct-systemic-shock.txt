The newborn who deteriorates at home
Case conference. The newborn who deteriorates at home. This is a fictional teaching case, with clinical review pending. A 6-day-old term newborn weighs 3.2 kilograms. Feeding and urine output have declined over a day. The infant now looks gray and breathes rapidly. No congenital heart diagnosis was made before discharge.

What do you notice?
At the bedside. The infant is poorly responsive, with weak femoral pulses, cool legs and a prolonged refill time. Upper-extremity pulses are easier to feel. Sepsis remains an urgent competing diagnosis.

Vital signs
The initial vital signs are as follows. Heart rate is 188 per minute. Right-arm blood pressure is 68 over 42. Leg blood pressure is 44 over 26; measurement quality is being checked. Respiratory rate is 72 per minute. Right-hand oxygen saturation is 94 percent. These are observations at this point in the case, rather than treatment targets.

Laboratory findings
Laboratory findings. Venous pH is 7.14. Lactate is 7.1 millimoles per liter. Glucose is 58 milligrams per deciliter. Interpret these results alongside the examination and the timing of sampling.

Diagnostic findings
Diagnostic findings. The combination of age, poor systemic perfusion and pulse differences raises concern for critical congenital heart disease as the duct closes. Definitive anatomy requires expert echocardiography. Saturation alone cannot exclude poor systemic blood flow.

What would you prioritize?
Think it through. Would a saturation in the nineties reassure you about systemic perfusion? Consider your answer while the case continues. No screen interaction is needed.

Clinical reasoning & priorities
Conference discussion. A duct-dependent systemic lesion can present as shock rather than profound cyanosis. Sepsis and other causes must be addressed in parallel until the cause is established. Priorities. 1. Consult neonatal and cardiac specialists urgently; arrange definitive echocardiography and a capable destination. 2. Discuss prostaglandin therapy for suspected duct dependence under protocol, anticipating apnea and an airway support requirement. 3. Support perfusion, temperature and glucose while evaluating infection and other causes of neonatal shock.

Reassessment & handoff
Reassessment and handoff. The cardiac team directs stabilization and transfer. The handoff includes limb pressures, pulse findings, perfusion trends and any response to therapy. The anatomical diagnosis is not assumed from the examination alone. This is an authored fictional course; improvement is not guaranteed in a real patient.
