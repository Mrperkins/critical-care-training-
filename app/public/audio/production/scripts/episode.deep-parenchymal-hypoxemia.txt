This is a level 4 critical-care session. The goal is not to memorize a list. The goal is to build a model that lets you predict what the patient will do next.

By the end, you should be able to differentiate major diffuse lung phenotypes using context, mechanics and imaging; and avoid treating every bilateral opacity as the same recruitable lung.

Now: Start with the physiologic pattern.

Diffuse parenchymal lung disease. Pneumonia, edema, ILD and diffuse inflammatory injury can all cause hypoxemia but differ in recruitability, compliance and treatment.

Build the mechanism first. Alveolar filling, interstitial disease and inflammatory injury impair gas exchange through varying combinations of shunt, diffusion limitation, reduced compliance and heterogeneous recruitability.

At the bedside, combine imaging pattern, mechanics, history and hemodynamics to distinguish pneumonia, hydrostatic edema, A R D S and chronic interstitial disease.

Two traps to actively avoid: Treating all bilateral opacities as A R D S. And: Using oxygenation response alone to infer recruitability.

The integration move is this: The same chest image can represent very different mechanics and circulatory problems.

Keep these terms available in your mental model: shunt, recruitability, interstitial disease.

What expert-level use of this concept looks like is this: Use imaging, mechanics and context to distinguish major parenchymal phenotypes. Avoid applying an A R D S strategy blindly to every diffuse opacity.

Now connect Diffuse parenchymal lung disease to A R D S physiology. Diffuse inflammatory lung injury producing heterogeneity, shunt, low aerated lung volume and risk from ventilator-induced injury.

The relationship matters because A R D S produces heterogeneous inflammatory lung injury, shunt, edema and a reduced aerated “baby lung,” making regional overdistension possible even at seemingly modest tidal volumes. At the bedside, integrate gas exchange, compliance, recruitability, prone response, driving pressure and hemodynamics rather than treating the P/F ratio alone.

Now connect Diffuse parenchymal lung disease to Cardiogenic pulmonary edema. Elevated hydrostatic pulmonary-capillary pressure drives interstitial/alveolar edema and alters lung compliance and gas exchange.

The relationship matters because Hydrostatic pulmonary edema develops when pulmonary capillary pressure drives fluid into interstitium/alveoli faster than lymphatic clearance can compensate. At the bedside, use noninvasive positive pressure and afterload/preload strategy according to the circulatory lesion while reassessing blood pressure and forward flow.

Now: Hydrostatic edema.

Cardiogenic pulmonary edema. Elevated hydrostatic pulmonary-capillary pressure drives interstitial/alveolar edema and alters lung compliance and gas exchange.

Build the mechanism first. Hydrostatic pulmonary edema develops when pulmonary capillary pressure drives fluid into interstitium/alveoli faster than lymphatic clearance can compensate.

At the bedside, use noninvasive positive pressure and afterload/preload strategy according to the circulatory lesion while reassessing blood pressure and forward flow.

Two traps to actively avoid: Treating every B-line pattern as volume overload. And: Diuresing a hypotensive patient without understanding the valve/ventricular physiology.

The integration move is this: Pulmonary edema is often a cardiac pressure problem expressed in the lung.

Keep these terms available in your mental model: hydrostatic edema, afterload, NIV.

What expert-level use of this concept looks like is this: Distinguish hydrostatic edema from permeability injury in context. Use positive pressure and afterload reduction while respecting the circulation.

Now connect Cardiogenic pulmonary edema to Critical valvular disease. Severe stenosis or regurgitation creates fixed or dynamic loading constraints that make usual shock interventions behave differently.

The relationship matters because Critical stenosis creates fixed outflow obstruction while acute regurgitation abruptly raises upstream pressure and reduces effective forward flow; loading interventions therefore have lesion-specific effects. At the bedside, identify the lesion and dominant problem—fixed output, pulmonary edema, low coronary perfusion or regurgitant volume—before applying generic shock treatment.

Now connect Cardiogenic pulmonary edema to Positive-pressure ventilation & circulation. Positive intrathoracic pressure changes venous return, ventricular transmural pressures and pulmonary vascular resistance.

The relationship matters because Positive intrathoracic pressure can reduce the gradient for venous return, change transmural ventricular pressures and alter P V R depending on lung volume and recruitability. At the bedside, post-intubation hypotension may reflect vasodilation, reduced venous return, increased RV afterload, auto-peep or previously compensated shock becoming unmasked.

Now connect A R D S physiology to Driving pressure. Plateau pressure minus total peep; a bedside expression of tidal strain relative to respiratory-system compliance.

The relationship matters because Driving pressure is plateau pressure minus total peep and reflects tidal pressure applied to the respiratory system relative to available compliance. At the bedside, a fixed tidal volume can impose very different stress depending on how much functional lung is available. Trend driving pressure alongside plateau pressure and mechanics.

Now: Compliance and recruitability.

Compliance & elastance. Compliance describes change in volume for change in pressure; elastance is its inverse.

Build the mechanism first. Compliance is change in volume divided by change in pressure; elastance is its inverse. Respiratory-system compliance includes both lung and chest-wall contributions.

At the bedside, use peak and plateau pressures to separate resistive from elastic loading, then ask whether chest wall, abdomen or lung is responsible.

Two traps to actively avoid: Calling every high airway pressure “stiff lungs.” And: Ignoring chest-wall and abdominal mechanics.

The integration move is this: A ventilator pressure only becomes physiologic information when flow state and pressure component are known.

Keep these terms available in your mental model: static compliance, elastance.

What expert-level use of this concept looks like is this: Separate resistance problems from compliance problems using peak and plateau pressures.

Now: Bedside lung pattern mapping.

Critical-care lung ultrasound. Lung ultrasound uses pleural and artifact patterns to evaluate interstitial syndrome, consolidation, effusion and pneumothorax.

Build the mechanism first. Lung ultrasound uses pleural motion and reverberation/consolidation artifacts to infer air-fluid-tissue relationships near the pleural surface.

At the bedside, scan multiple standardized zones and combine A-lines, B-lines, consolidation, effusion and pleural signs with mechanics and clinical context.

Two traps to actively avoid: Treating B-lines as synonymous with cardiogenic edema. And: Using one negative window to exclude focal lung disease.

The integration move is this: Lung ultrasound is a pattern map that becomes diagnostic only after physiology and distribution are considered.

Keep these terms available in your mental model: A-lines, B-lines, consolidation, shred sign.

What expert-level use of this concept looks like is this: Use patterns across zones rather than one isolated artifact. Integrate ultrasound with mechanics and clinical context.

Now connect Critical-care lung ultrasound to Pneumothorax ultrasound. Pleural ultrasound combines sliding, B-lines, M-mode patterns and lung point rather than relying on a single sign.

The relationship matters because Pleural ultrasound infers apposition or separation of pleural layers through sliding, B-lines, lung pulse, M-mode patterns and lung point. At the bedside, absent sliding raises suspicion but is nonspecific; lung point is more specific when present in the appropriate context.

Now connect Critical-care lung ultrasound to Pleural effusion & empyema. Pleural fluid can impair mechanics or represent infection; ultrasound helps characterize volume, complexity and procedural access.

The relationship matters because Pleural fluid changes lung/chest-wall mechanics and may reflect hydrostatic, inflammatory, malignant or infected processes; loculation changes drainability. At the bedside, use ultrasound to assess volume, complexity, diaphragm/lung relationships and a safe procedural window while integrating the cause and need for source control.
