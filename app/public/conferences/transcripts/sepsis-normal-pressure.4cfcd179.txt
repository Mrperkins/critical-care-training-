A normal pressure in a sick child
Case conference. Pediatric sepsis with compensated shock from a febrile urinary infection. This is a fictional teaching case, with clinical review pending. A 4-year-old child, measured weight 17 kilograms, has fever and poor intake. The caregiver reports rapidly reduced interaction and no urine for eight hours. A clinic recorded a normal blood pressure and sent the child for further assessment. There is no known heart disease. The mother tells EMS that the child has become unusually sleepy, has pain on urination and has not passed urine for eight hours. At the community department, urine microscopy shows pyuria and cultures are sent. There is no focal chest finding. We diagnose sepsis with clinical shock before the pressure falls; the urinary source remains provisional until cultures return. The clinic’s blood pressure reading is accurate information, but it is an incomplete summary. Before accepting the word stable, ask how the child has been behaving and whether the kidneys are producing urine.

The periphery tells a different story
The child is lethargic but rousable, with cool mottled extremities, weak peripheral pulses and capillary refill of five seconds. Breathing is rapid without focal wheeze. These are direct clues about perfusion. Notice the mismatch between a maintained pressure and the examination. That mismatch should sharpen our assessment rather than reassure us.

Compensation can preserve pressure
Heart rate is 166 per minute. For a four-year-old, the RCH acceptable heart-rate range for unwell children is 80 to 150 beats per minute. This is broader than a healthy resting range; trends and examination still determine severity. This is tachycardia beyond the broad unwell-child range. Fever contributes, but the weak pulses, lethargy and prolonged refill indicate more than fever alone. Shock may be accompanied by cool or mottled skin, delayed refill, reduced urine and altered interaction; several are present. Blood pressure is 96 over 58. For a four-year-old, the RCH acceptable systolic-pressure range for unwell children is 75 to 110 millimeters of mercury. It is an observation reference, not a shock rule-out threshold. Systolic pressure is preserved within the reference. This is compensated shock: the examination shows poor perfusion despite a maintained pressure. Vasoconstriction may preserve pressure while extremities cool, pulses weaken and urine output falls. Respiratory rate is 40 per minute. For a four-year-old, the RCH acceptable respiratory-rate range for unwell children is 17 to 30 breaths per minute. A count in range does not establish adequate ventilation. This is tachypnea. Fever and metabolic stress from poor perfusion can drive breathing even without a focal lung disease. In sepsis, rapid breathing may accompany lethargy, fever and poor perfusion; new crackles would prompt reassessment for lung involvement or fluid intolerance. Temperature is 39.4 degrees Celsius. Temperature depends on site and time of day; about 36.5 to 37.5 degrees Celsius is a useful reference. A temperature of 38 or more is fever, and below 36 is concerning in an ill patient. This is fever. It supports the infectious history but neither measures shock severity nor identifies the organism. Fever can produce flushing, rigors and a rapid pulse; a child with sepsis may also be normothermic or hypothermic. Oxygen saturation is 95 percent on room air. At sea level, oxygen saturation is usually 95 to 100 percent in a person without a special oxygen target. Verify signal quality and the oxygen support in use. This is at the lower boundary of the usual reference. It does not measure systemic oxygen delivery or exclude shock. Tissue hypoperfusion can cause mottling and altered consciousness despite an acceptable saturation. The child is using a fast heart rate and compensatory physiology to maintain the measured pressure. Waiting for that pressure to fall would mean waiting for a later stage of deterioration.

Lactate supports the concern
Venous lactate is 4.7 millimoles per liter. The whole-blood lactate reference here is 0.5 to 2.2 millimoles per liter below age sixteen. Sampling method and the clinical trend matter. This is hyperlactatemia in a child with poor perfusion. Adrenergic stress and impaired clearance can contribute, so we follow the clinical response as well as the number. Associated bedside findings of shock include delayed refill, oliguria and reduced interaction; those are actually documented here. Glucose is 72 milligrams per deciliter. For a random sample after the first month, this laboratory lists glucose from 65 to 139 milligrams per deciliter. Fasting reference limits and treatment thresholds differ. This is within the random reference toward its lower end. Poor intake and sepsis justify repeat monitoring, but this result does not establish hypoglycemia. Hypoglycemia may cause sweating, pallor, irritability or seizures; none should be assumed from an in-range measurement. Venous pH is 7.28. This pediatric peripheral venous pH reference is 7.30 to 7.40. Venous and arterial samples must be identified separately. This is acidemia. In this circulation-focused presentation, metabolic acidosis is likely; carbon dioxide and bicarbonate are needed to characterize the full acid-base disorder. Poor perfusion with acidosis may produce tachypnea and lethargy, both seen in this child. The lactate fits the clinical picture of impaired perfusion, but we did not need a laboratory permission slip to recognize a sick child. We still need repeated clinical assessment as treatment begins.

Suspected infection with impaired perfusion
Suspected infection with poor perfusion raises concern for septic shock despite preserved blood pressure. Cultures and source evaluation should be coordinated without delaying time-critical treatment.  A working diagnosis is enough to organize urgent care while we continue to investigate. The source may become clearer later; the perfusion problem is already in front of us.

Intervene and reassess each step
Children can maintain blood pressure through compensatory vasoconstriction. Perfusion, interaction and urine output reveal deterioration earlier. Lactate supports the assessment but cannot establish or exclude shock on its own. Activate pediatric sepsis care and give timely antimicrobials under the local pathway. Use measured, reassessed fluid therapy appropriate to the setting and physiology, watching for overload. Prepare vasoactive support when indicated and escalate early to pediatric critical care. Resuscitation is a series of decisions informed by response. After each intervention, ask whether perfusion improved and whether the lungs or heart are showing a cost from the treatment.

A partial response is useful information
After an initial measured fluid intervention, pulses and capillary refill improve only partly. The team reassesses lungs and cardiac function before further fluid, and prepares vasoactive support rather than repeating boluses automatically. The team gives prompt intravenous antimicrobials and carefully reassessed isotonic fluid under the pediatric sepsis pathway. Refill improves only partly, so repeated boluses are not automatic and vasoactive escalation is prepared. During the next hour the child becomes more interactive, pulses strengthen and urine output returns after sustained resuscitation. The receiving service later identifies Escherichia coli in urine and blood cultures and narrows therapy to susceptibility results. This synthetic outcome establishes a urinary source with bacteremia rather than leaving infection unspecified. A partial response is neither a reason to abandon the diagnosis nor a reason to repeat the same action indefinitely. It tells us to re-examine the physiology before choosing the next intervention.
