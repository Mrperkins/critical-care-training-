Oxygen produces little change
Case conference. Blue despite oxygen. This is a fictional teaching case, with clinical review pending. A 3-day-old term newborn weighs 3.5 kilograms. The infant has persistent central cyanosis and feeds poorly. Supplemental oxygen produces little change in saturation. When the expected response to oxygen is small, revisit the mechanism of the hypoxemia. The lungs are part of the question, but the route blood takes through the circulation matters too.

Cyanosis with modest visible effort
The infant is cyanotic with modest respiratory effort. Breath sounds are equal. The presentation prompts consideration of congenital heart disease, pulmonary hypertension, lung disease and infection. The degree of visible respiratory distress does not fully explain the cyanosis. That mismatch is a reason to broaden the evaluation rather than simply turn up the same support.

Compare preductal and postductal readings
Heart rate is 164 per minute. Respiratory rate is 54 per minute. Right-hand oxygen saturation is 76 percent on supplemental oxygen. Foot oxygen saturation is 74 percent on supplemental oxygen. Both readings are very low. A small difference between the hand and foot does not establish normal cardiac anatomy; the pattern still needs expert interpretation.

The gas confirms severe hypoxemia
Arterial pH is 7.29. Arterial oxygen tension is 38 millimeters of mercury on the current oxygen support. Lactate is 3.5 millimoles per liter. The result describes the physiological problem on the current support. It does not tell us which lesion, if any, is present or what circulation balance that anatomy will require.

Urgent echocardiography changes the plan
Echocardiography is needed urgently to distinguish structural disease and pulmonary hypertension. A small preductal–postductal difference does not rule out a cardiac lesion. The purpose of echocardiography is practical: different circulations may need different support goals. We should avoid applying one generic target to every cyanotic newborn.

What would you do while defining the circulation?
Should you keep increasing oxygen while postponing a cardiac evaluation? Think about the balance between immediate supportive care and time to a definitive explanation. Repeatedly increasing oxygen is not a substitute for that explanation.

Link support to a specialist circulation plan
Severe hypoxemia with limited response to oxygen requires a broader circulation-focused assessment. The required balance of pulmonary and systemic blood flow depends on the anatomy. Support ventilation and oxygenation while obtaining neonatal cardiac consultation. Discuss prostaglandin and transport planning under the specialist pathway; prepare for apnea if it is used. Use specialist-directed oxygen and ventilation goals rather than imposing one saturation target on every congenital lesion. The transport plan needs to match the anticipated physiology. Make sure the team understands the goals and can provide the required infusion, airway support and supplies without interruption.

Anatomy refines stabilization goals
Expert echocardiography establishes the lesion and the stabilization goals. The transport team verifies that infusion, airway and oxygen supplies can sustain the planned journey. Once the anatomy is known, the handoff should explain what changed in the support goals and why. That protects the plan when a new team sees an unfamiliar saturation target.

Cyanotic newborn: connect oxygenation and circulation
Case synopsis. Persistent cyanosis required assessment of circulation as well as lungs. Definitive anatomy, rather than a generic oxygen target, guided subsequent care. The key move was asking why oxygen was not producing the expected response. That question leads from a monitor value to a circulation-focused assessment.
