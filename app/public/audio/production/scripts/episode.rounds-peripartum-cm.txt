This is a level 4 critical-care session. The goal is not to memorize a list. The goal is to build a model that lets you predict what the patient will do next.

By the end, you should be able to recognize peripartum heart failure early; and apply cardiogenic-shock physiology while respecting pregnancy/postpartum constraints.

Now: Normal symptoms can camouflage failure.

Peripartum cardiomyopathy. Pregnancy-associated systolic heart failure can present from late pregnancy into the postpartum period and may mimic normal peripartum symptoms.

Build the mechanism first. Pregnancy-associated systolic dysfunction can emerge late in pregnancy or postpartum, producing congestion and low output while symptoms can resemble normal peripartum physiology.

At the bedside, look for orthopnea, hypoxemia, pulmonary edema, ventricular dysfunction and shock rather than dismissing dyspnea as normal postpartum change.

Two traps to actively avoid: Missing heart failure because edema and tachycardia seem expected. And: Forgetting pregnancy/postpartum constraints on some therapies.

The integration move is this: Apply ordinary pump-failure physiology inside an extraordinary physiologic state.

Keep these terms available in your mental model: postpartum, systolic failure, pulmonary edema.

What expert-level use of this concept looks like is this: Recognize decompensated heart failure in the peripartum period. Account for pregnancy/postpartum medication and hemodynamic considerations.

Tie this back to the patient. Ask what variable is changing, what downstream effect you expect, and what bedside finding would support or contradict that model.

Now: The pump-failure model.

Cardiogenic shock. Pump failure where restoring perfusion must be balanced against congestion, myocardial oxygen demand, coronary perfusion and mechanical complications.

Build the mechanism first. Cardiogenic shock is inadequate forward flow from pump failure, often accompanied by elevated filling pressures and congestion. RV, LV and biventricular phenotypes behave differently.

At the bedside, track flow, pressure, congestion, coronary perfusion, rhythm and mechanical complications; escalate support when pharmacology cannot restore effective circulation.

Two traps to actively avoid: Treating all cardiogenic shock with the same inotrope/pressor pattern. And: Ignoring congestion while chasing blood pressure.

The integration move is this: The support strategy should match the failing ventricle and the cause of pump failure.

Keep these terms available in your mental model: cardiac power output, congestion, inotropy.

What expert-level use of this concept looks like is this: Phenotype left-, right- and biventricular pump failure. Choose support based on pressure, flow, congestion and the cause of pump failure.

Tie this back to the patient. Ask what variable is changing, what downstream effect you expect, and what bedside finding would support or contradict that model.
