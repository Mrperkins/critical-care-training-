Vasopressor & inotrope physiology. Vasoactive drugs change arterial tone, venous tone, heart rate, contractility and regional flow; the desired effect depends on the shock model.

Build the mechanism first. Vasoactive drugs alter arterial tone, venous tone, contractility and heart rate to different degrees. The same MAP response can hide very different effects on flow and myocardial demand.

At the bedside, choose the agent from the dominant deficit and define what improvement and toxicity should look like before titration.

Two traps to actively avoid: Treating MAP increase as proof of improved perfusion. And: Ignoring venous effects and cardiac workload.

The integration move is this: Use vasoactive drugs to correct a physiologic mechanism, not to normalize a monitor number.

Now: The venous circulation matters.

Venous return. Flow back to the right heart determined by the pressure gradient from the systemic circulation and resistance to venous return.

Build the mechanism first. Venous return depends on the pressure gradient from the systemic venous reservoir toward the right atrium and on resistance to that flow. Mean systemic filling pressure and right-atrial pressure can move independently.

At the bedside, a rise in right-atrial pressure can reduce venous return even when the patient appears more “full.” Venoconstriction can recruit unstressed volume and change the upstream pressure without adding external fluid.

Two traps to actively avoid: Treating a high CVP as proof of adequate forward flow. And: Ignoring abdominal, thoracic or pericardial pressure as downstream resistance.

The integration move is this: Predict how fluid, venoconstriction, peep and RV failure each alter the venous-return gradient.

Keep these terms available in your mental model: mean systemic filling pressure, stressed volume, venous return gradient.

What expert-level use of this concept looks like is this: Explain why CVP can rise while venous return falls. Predict effects of vasodilation, venoconstriction and positive pressure on venous return.

Now connect Venous return to Stressed vs unstressed volume. The fraction of blood volume generating elastic recoil pressure versus volume that fills the vasculature without generating pressure.

The relationship matters because Only part of the vascular blood volume generates recoil pressure that drives venous return. Venous tone can convert unstressed volume into stressed volume and raise mean systemic filling pressure. At the bedside, a vasoactive drug can sometimes improve venous return partly by changing venous capacitance, not only by raising arterial pressure.

Now: Vasoplegic shock.

Septic shock physiology. Distributive shock with dynamic changes in vascular tone, venous capacitance, myocardial performance, microcirculation and oxygen extraction.

Build the mechanism first. Septic shock can combine vasoplegia, venous pooling, myocardial dysfunction, endothelial leak and disturbed microcirculation. Cardiac output may be low, normal or high.

At the bedside, reassess vascular tone, flow, congestion and perfusion after each intervention rather than assuming a fixed distributive phenotype.

Two traps to actively avoid: Reducing sepsis to “low S V R.” And: Using lactate or MAP alone as the resuscitation target.

The integration move is this: Source control, antimicrobial exposure and circulatory support must progress in parallel.

Keep these terms available in your mental model: vasoplegia, microcirculation, distributive shock.

Now connect Septic shock physiology to Source control. Definitive management of infected material, devitalized tissue or an infected device can be as important as antimicrobial therapy.

The relationship matters because Antibiotics cannot sterilize every infected collection, necrotic tissue or infected device. Now connect Septic shock physiology to Lactate physiology. Lactate reflects production and clearance across multiple pathways and is not a direct tissue-hypoxia meter.

Now: Pump failure and inotropy.

Cardiogenic shock. Pump failure where restoring perfusion must be balanced against congestion, myocardial oxygen demand, coronary perfusion and mechanical complications.

Build the mechanism first. Cardiogenic shock is inadequate forward flow from pump failure, often accompanied by elevated filling pressures and congestion. RV, LV and biventricular phenotypes behave differently.

At the bedside, track flow, pressure, congestion, coronary perfusion, rhythm and mechanical complications; escalate support when pharmacology cannot restore effective circulation.

Two traps to actively avoid: Treating all cardiogenic shock with the same inotrope/pressor pattern. And: Ignoring congestion while chasing blood pressure.

The integration move is this: The support strategy should match the failing ventricle and the cause of pump failure.

Keep these terms available in your mental model: cardiac power output, congestion, inotropy.

What expert-level use of this concept looks like is this: Phenotype left-, right- and biventricular pump failure. Choose support based on pressure, flow, congestion and the cause of pump failure.

Now: The failing right ventricle.

Right ventricular failure. Failure of a thin-walled ventricle that is highly sensitive to acute afterload, volume, perfusion pressure and ventricular interaction.

Build the mechanism first. The RV is thin-walled and highly sensitive to acute afterload. When it dilates, wall stress rises, coronary perfusion can fall and septal shift can reduce LV filling.

At the bedside, track systemic pressure, venous congestion, echo geometry, oxygenation, CO2, acid-base status and ventilator pressures together. Small changes in P V R or intrathoracic pressure can have large hemodynamic consequences.

Two traps to actively avoid: Reflexively giving fluid to every hypotensive RV patient. And: Focusing on systemic pressure while ignoring worsening RV dilation and congestion.

The integration move is this: Every intervention should be judged by its effects on RV preload, RV afterload, contractility and coronary perfusion.

Keep these terms available in your mental model: RV-PA coupling, septal shift, transmural pressure.

What expert-level use of this concept looks like is this: Recognize RV shock physiology before systemic hypotension becomes profound. Predict how peep, hypercapnia, hypoxemia, fluids and vasoactives may change RV performance.

Now connect Right ventricular failure to Pulmonary vascular resistance. Resistance faced by the RV, influenced by lung volume, oxygenation, CO2, pH, pulmonary vascular disease and mechanical ventilation.

The relationship matters because Pulmonary vascular resistance reflects the combined behavior of alveolar and extra-alveolar vessels and is affected by lung volume, oxygen, CO2, pH and pulmonary vascular disease. At the bedside, both severe derecruitment and overdistension can raise RV afterload. Hypoxemia, hypercapnia and acidosis can compound the problem.

Now connect Right ventricular failure to Ventricular interdependence. The ventricles share a septum and constrained pericardial space, so pressure or volume loading of one changes filling and geometry of the other.

The relationship matters because The ventricles share a septum and a constrained pericardial space. Acute RV pressure or volume loading can shift the septum and reduce LV diastolic filling. At the bedside, a falling LV stroke volume in RV failure may occur despite apparently adequate intravascular volume because geometry, not supply, is limiting filling.

Clinical uncertainty & model revision. Expert reasoning explicitly tracks uncertainty, contradictory data and what finding would falsify the current working model.

Build the mechanism first. Clinical decisions are made with incomplete information. Calibrated reasoning assigns provisional probability, makes predictions and updates when new data contradict the model.

At the bedside, state confidence, name disconfirming evidence and define what response you expect from a treatment if the model is correct.

Two traps to actively avoid: Equating uncertainty with indecision. And: Becoming more confident simply because the same hypothesis has been repeated.

The integration move is this: A model that cannot be falsified is not useful clinical reasoning.

Keep these terms available in your mental model: calibration, falsification, diagnostic time-out.

What expert-level use of this concept looks like is this: State confidence, identify disconfirming evidence and update the model when the patient fails to respond as predicted.

Now connect Clinical uncertainty & model revision to Cognitive bias & diagnostic error. Expert reasoning uses deliberate checks against anchoring, premature closure, confirmation bias and overconfidence while still making timely decisions.

The relationship matters because Anchoring, confirmation bias and premature closure arise because fast pattern recognition is useful but can overcommit to the first plausible model. At the bedside, name the finding that does not fit, generate at least one alternative mechanism and use treatment response as new diagnostic evidence.

Now connect Clinical uncertainty & model revision to Multisystem treatment tradeoffs. A therapy that helps one organ can worsen another; expert care weighs global benefit, reversibility and monitoring.

The relationship matters because Organ-support interventions frequently redistribute risk: peep can help oxygenation but worsen RV load; fluids can increase flow but worsen congestion; sedation can improve synchrony but obscure neurologic assessment. At the bedside, before intervening, name the intended benefit, the organ/system at risk and the signal that would tell you the tradeoff has become unfavorable.
