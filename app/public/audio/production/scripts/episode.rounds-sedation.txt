You're listening to The agitated ventilated patient: do not reflexively deepen sedation. Pain, dyssynchrony, delirium, hypoxemia and physiology can all look like agitation.

This is a level 3 critical-care session. The goal is not to memorize a list. The goal is to build a model that lets you predict what the patient will do next.

By the end, you should be able to build a differential for agitation before escalating sedation and recognize when the underlying problem is pain, ventilator interaction or delirium.

Now: Agitation is a sign, not a diagnosis.

ICU analgesia, sedation & synchrony. Sedation strategy balances analgesia, comfort, ventilator synchrony, neurologic assessment, hemodynamics and delirium risk.

Build the mechanism first. Analgesics and sedatives alter pain, awareness, respiratory drive, vascular tone and neurologic assessment. Deep sedation is a physiologic intervention with downstream consequences.

At the bedside, separate pain, anxiety, delirium, dyssynchrony and hypoxemia before escalating sedation; use an explicit target and reassess it.

Two traps to actively avoid: Using paralysis as evidence of comfort. And: Treating agitation as a medication deficiency without finding the cause.

The integration move is this: Sedation strategy should serve a clinical goal, not simply silence movement.

Keep these terms available in your mental model: analgosedation, RASS, daily awakening.

What expert-level use of this concept looks like is this: Separate pain, anxiety, delirium and dyssynchrony before simply deepening sedation. Predict major hemodynamic and respiratory tradeoffs of sedative strategies.

Now: When the ventilator is causing the fight.

Ventilator emergency troubleshooting. A structured patient-to-device approach to acute hypoxemia, high pressure, low pressure, dyssynchrony and post-intubation deterioration.

Build the mechanism first. Acute ventilator deterioration can originate from the patient, tube, circuit or machine. Peak and plateau pressures help separate resistance from compliance when measurements are trustworthy.

At the bedside, look at the patient first, rescue oxygenation/ventilation if the device cannot be trusted, then trace from patient to tube to circuit to machine.

Two traps to actively avoid: Staring at the ventilator while the patient deteriorates. And: Treating every high-pressure alarm as bronchospasm.

The integration move is this: Use pressure pattern, capnography, exam and circuit inspection to localize the problem quickly.

Keep these terms available in your mental model: DOPES, peak pressure, plateau pressure, auto-peep.

What expert-level use of this concept looks like is this: Separate resistance, compliance, circuit and patient problems using waveforms and bedside examination. Prioritize oxygenation and manual ventilation when the device or circuit cannot be trusted.

Now connect Ventilator emergency troubleshooting to Dead space. Ventilation that does not effectively participate in CO2 exchange, from conducting airways or ventilated but poorly perfused alveoli.

The relationship matters because Dead-space ventilation reaches regions that do not effectively eliminate CO2 because perfusion is absent or inadequate, or because gas remains in conducting airways. At the bedside, rising minute-ventilation requirement, widening PaCO2–EtCO2 gap or unexpectedly low EtCO2 can signal increasing dead space when ventilation is otherwise unchanged.

Now connect Ventilator emergency troubleshooting to peep. Positive end-expiratory pressure used to preserve recruitment and oxygenation, with important mechanical and hemodynamic effects.

The relationship matters because peep can prevent end-expiratory collapse and recruit unstable lung units, but can also overdistend already-open units and transmit pressure to the thorax. At the bedside, judge a peep change by oxygenation, compliance, driving pressure, hemodynamics and the disease’s recruitability rather than by oxygen saturation alone.

Now: Delirium and the ICU environment.

Delirium, sleep & mobility. Brain dysfunction in critical illness is shaped by disease, drugs, sleep disruption, immobility and environment.

Build the mechanism first. Delirium reflects acute brain dysfunction shaped by illness, medications, sleep disruption, sensory deprivation, immobility and environmental stressors.

At the bedside, identify reversible drivers, lighten unnecessary sedation, restore sleep-wake cues, mobilize when feasible and involve family/orientation strategies.

Two traps to actively avoid: Calling hypoactive delirium “calm.” And: Treating delirium exclusively with sedating medication.

The integration move is this: Brain recovery depends on the entire ICU environment, not one drug.

Keep these terms available in your mental model: CAM-ICU, ABCDE/ABCDEF bundle.

What expert-level use of this concept looks like is this: Recognize modifiable drivers of delirium. Integrate sedation reduction, sleep, mobility and family engagement into daily ICU care.
