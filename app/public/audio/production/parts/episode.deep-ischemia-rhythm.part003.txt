Now: Membrane chemistry changes the ECG.

Electrolytes & cardiac electrophysiology. Potassium, magnesium, calcium and acid-base changes alter depolarization, repolarization and conduction.

Build the mechanism first. Potassium, magnesium and calcium alter resting membrane potential, channel function and action-potential duration, changing conduction and repolarization.

At the bedside, use ECG morphology as evidence of membrane toxicity and correct the responsible electrolyte while monitoring for rapid electrical change.

Two traps to actively avoid: Waiting for a numeric threshold despite toxic ECG findings. And: Correcting potassium without magnesium when refractory ectopy or QT-related risk persists.

The integration move is this: The ECG is a real-time readout of electrolyte physiology.

Keep these terms available in your mental model: QT, QRS, repolarization.

Now: Sudden post-MI mechanical failure.

Acute coronary ischemia & complications. Acute myocardial ischemia can produce pump failure, dysrhythmia and mechanical complications whose physiology may dominate over the ECG diagnosis.

Build the mechanism first. Myocardial ischemia reduces contractility and electrical stability and can cause papillary muscle, septal or free-wall mechanical complications after infarction.

At the bedside, when a patient with ACS suddenly develops shock, pulmonary edema or a new murmur, broaden beyond “more ischemia” to mechanical failure and rhythm disturbance.

Two traps to actively avoid: Treating the ECG diagnosis while missing a mechanical complication. And: Using vasopressor escalation as a substitute for reperfusion or structural intervention.

The integration move is this: Reperfusion, rhythm and mechanical hemodynamics must be managed as one evolving syndrome.
